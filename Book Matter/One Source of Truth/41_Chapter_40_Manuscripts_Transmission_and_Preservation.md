# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 40 — Manuscripts, Transmission, and Preservation

> **Movement V — How the Claim Is Authenticated**

> **Central objective:** Examine how the biblical writings have been copied, translated, printed, and preserved, and determine what the multiply attested manuscript tradition establishes for authentication: recoverability of a public text.

---

## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 39 established historical embeddedness. Independent inscriptions, monuments, administrative records, and related traces correspond with numerous public claims of the writings. The record is not a closed verbal world. It names a world that left independent marks.

A further question is then forced by the object in the reader’s hands.

That object is not an autograph stored in a palace archive. It is a transmitted body of writings. If the historical claims are claims about the world, the wording that carries those claims has a history of copying, translation, and preservation.

> **How do autograph, copy, version, and printed edition relate — and what does the multiply attested manuscript tradition establish for authentication?**

Chapters 20–22 established that the object under investigation is γραφή (*graphē*): writing. Chapters 30 and 31 distinguished inspiration from preservation. Chapters 33 and 34 established that a manuscript is evidence concerning transmission and documentary existence, and that the existence of manuscripts is not identical with a conclusion about every original wording. Chapter 37 used early manuscripts and translations as chronological control for prophetic texts.

Those points are now premises.

This chapter takes up a distinct authentication line:

> **Recoverability of the public written object.**

The writings cohere as one testimony. Their public history corresponds with independently recoverable reality. The remaining documentary question, before the lines are gathered, is whether that testimony still exists as a recoverable text.

---

## Central Objective

> **To distinguish autograph, copy, version, and printed edition; to examine the public, multiply attested manuscript tradition of the biblical writings; and to determine what that tradition establishes for authentication: recoverability of the substance of a public text.**

The governing movement is:

> **Written object → copying and circulation → surviving witnesses (manuscripts, versions, quotations) → comparison of independent streams → recoverability of substance → warranted authentication conclusion.**

The chapter’s conclusion is affirmative:

> **The biblical writings survive as a public, multiply attested tradition whose substance is recoverable. Hopeless-corruption fails. Identifying any one printed edition with the autograph fails. Recoverability of the public text is an authentication fact of full force: there is a written object to investigate.**

The remaining authentication lines follow by sequence. Their later place does not weaken the recoverability established here.

---

# 40.1 The Question Created by the Preceding Chapter

Chapter 39 asked whether public historical claims in the writings correspond to independently recoverable realities.

They do.

A historically embedded testimony must still be recoverable as text if it is to be examined as Graphē. Correspondence with Sennacherib or Gallio does not, by itself, settle whether the wording that names those persons has reached the present as a public writing rather than as an irretrievable rumor.

The authentication question therefore expands from correspondence with the world to survival of the writing:

> **Do we still have a recoverable written object whose claims can be examined?**

The question is forced by the character of ancient writing itself. Authors write. Copies are made. Translations are made. Printed editions are later still. Those are four different kinds of object. Collapsing them produces two opposite errors: treating the transmitted text as hopelessly lost, and treating one later printed artifact as if it were the original writing.

This chapter inspects the actual tradition.

---

# 40.2 What This Chapter Does Not Repeat

Chapter 16 established that the Bible is a library of writings in human language.

Chapter 17 established that those writings make historical claims and that historical correspondence is a genuine evidential category.

Chapters 20–22 established γραφή as writing, and distinguished Graphē, Holy Writ, Scripture, Bible, and Written Word as related designations of a written object.

Chapter 30 distinguished inspiration from preservation: inspiration concerns the production of Scripture; preservation concerns its continued transmission.

Chapter 31 restated the sequence:

> **writing → copying → transmission → preservation → textual witnesses.**

Chapter 33 established that “there are manuscripts” establishes that manuscripts exist, and that a manuscript of a biblical book bears directly upon the existence and transmission of that textual form.

Chapter 34 established documentary authentication: once testimony is written, the document is a historical artifact. It reserved the examination of the manuscript tradition for this chapter.

Chapter 37 established that the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Septuagint bear upon the antiquity of substantial prophetic texts. That chronological use is now a premise. The present chapter uses those same classes of witness as transmission evidence: they show how a public text was copied and translated.

Chapter 38 established internal coherence. Chapter 39 established external correspondence.

This chapter does not re-demonstrate that the Bible is a writing, that inspiration is not identical with copying, or that manuscripts exist. The new evidential function is this:

> **Given those premises, the transmitted tradition is public and multiply attested, the substance of the text is recoverable, and both hopeless-corruption and the identification of one printed edition with the autograph fail.**

The investigation now inspects the witnesses.

---

# 40.3 The Fourfold Distinction

Four objects must be kept distinct throughout.

### Autograph

The autograph is the original writing produced in the act of composition: the first complete written form of a given biblical book or letter as it left the author, or the author’s authorized scribe, in the language of composition.

### Copy

A copy is a manuscript transcribed from an exemplar. It is handwritten. It may be complete or fragmentary. It may be made from the autograph or, far more often, from an earlier copy. Every surviving biblical manuscript is, in this sense, a copy.

### Version

A version is a translation of the text into another language: Hebrew or Aramaic into Greek, Greek into Latin or Syriac or Coptic, and, later, into the vernacular languages of Europe and the wider world.

### Printed edition

A printed edition is a typeset publication of a text, whether in the original languages or in translation. It is a product of the press, of editorial selection among witnesses, and, in the case of a translation, of linguistic rendering.

These four are related as a historical sequence:

> **Autograph → copies → versions of those copies → printed editions of reconstructed texts and of translations.**

They are not four names for the same thing.

---

# 40.4 Autograph

**Autograph**, in this chapter, names the original written composition.

Moses is presented as a writer of words in a book (Exodus 17:14; 24:4; 34:27). Jeremiah dictates words to Baruch, who writes them in a roll (Jeremiah 36:4). Luke writes an orderly account (Luke 1:3). Paul writes, or causes to be written, letters that churches are to read (Colossians 4:16). Those are claims about original written acts.

The autograph is therefore:

> **the first written instantiation of a given work in the language of composition.**

Several consequences follow.

The autograph is a historical object. It had material, date, place, and a first readership.

The autograph is not identical with later copies of it. A copy may be excellent. It remains a copy.

The autograph is not identical with a translation of it. A version may be faithful. It remains a version.

The autograph is not identical with a printed edition, however carefully that edition is made.

The investigation of transmission begins here because authentication of a written claim requires a recoverable text, and recoverability is a relation between later witnesses and an earlier written act. If there was no original writing, there is nothing to transmit. The biblical writings present themselves as original writings. The manuscript tradition is the historical trail of those writings.

---

# 40.5 Copy and Manuscript

**Manuscript** means a handwritten document. In biblical study it ordinarily names a surviving handwritten copy of some portion of the biblical text.

A manuscript may be:

- a fragment of a few verses;
- a substantial portion of a book;
- a collection of books;
- a complete testament;
- or a pandect containing both testaments.

It may be written on papyrus, parchment (vellum), or, later, paper. It may be written in formal majuscule (uncial) letters or in later minuscule (cursive) script. It may be a continuous-text copy or a lectionary arranged for public reading.

The manuscript is evidence of two facts at once.

It is evidence that a textual form existed at the time and place of copying.

It is evidence of the copying process itself: the scribe’s exemplar, corrections, omissions, additions, and orthography.

Chapter 34 already required this double character. A manuscript is not merely a container of ideas. It is a historical artifact. This chapter examines the artifacts as a tradition.

A copy is not a failure of writing. It is how ancient writings live. Before printing, publication *was* copying. A letter sent to Colossae is copied for Laodicea. A Gospel used in one congregation is copied for another. A law deposited beside the ark is copied for a king (Deuteronomy 17:18). The existence of copies is the ordinary life of a public text.

---

# 40.6 Version

**Version**, in this chapter, names a translation.

The biblical writings did not remain in one language. The Hebrew Scriptures were translated into Greek centuries before the New Testament period. New Testament writings in Greek were translated into Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Gothic, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, and other languages in late antiquity and the early medieval period. Still later, printed vernacular Bibles carried the text into English and a multitude of other tongues.

A version is evidence of two different kinds.

It is evidence that a source text was available to the translator at the time of translation. A second-century Greek translation of Hebrew Isaiah is a witness that a Hebrew Isaiah existed to be translated.

It is evidence of the translator’s rendering. A version is not a photograph of the source. It is a linguistic act. Word order, idiom, and vocabulary choices belong to the version as well as to the exemplar.

That double character must be kept. Versions are early, independent, and geographically distributed witnesses. They are not autographs. They are not, by themselves, the original-language wording. They bear upon recoverability because they can confirm, at the level of substance and often at the level of particular readings, what the source tradition contained.

---

# 40.7 Printed Edition

**Printed edition** names a typeset publication.

Printing, in Europe, belongs to the fifteenth century. The first complete printed Latin Bible is a printed edition of a version. Erasmus’s 1516 Greek New Testament is a printed edition of a reconstructed Greek text based on a small number of later manuscripts. The King James Bible of 1611 is a printed English version, itself dependent on earlier English versions and on printed Hebrew and Greek editions. Modern scholarly editions of the Hebrew Bible and of the Greek New Testament are printed editions with critical apparatuses recording variants among witnesses.

A printed edition may be:

- a printing of one manuscript;
- an editorial reconstruction from many manuscripts;
- a translation of such a reconstruction;
- or a revision of an earlier printed translation.

In every case it is later than the autograph, later than the manuscript tradition it uses, and distinct from both.

Printed editions are historically important. They stabilize a text for wide reading. They are not the original writing. Treating any one of them as identical with the autograph is a category error. That error will be examined with full force below. It must be named at the outset so that the manuscript evidence is not read as a contest among modern title pages.

---

# 40.8 Why the Distinctions Matter for Authentication

Authentication in Movement V concerns the written claim of the Graphē.

If autograph, copy, version, and printed edition are collapsed, the investigation cannot state what it has recovered.

If they are distinguished, the proper proposition can be stated:

> **The substance of the original written text is recoverable from a public, multiply attested tradition of copies, versions, and quotations, and is now available in printed editions that are editions, not autographs.**

That proposition is an authentication proposition. It concerns whether the object under investigation still exists as a text.

Two failures of distinction produce two failures of argument.

The first treats every difference among copies as proof that the original is lost. It confuses the existence of variation with the non-existence of a recoverable text.

The second treats a favored printed Bible as the original writing. It confuses a late publication with the autograph.

Both fail for the same reason: they do not describe the actual relation among the four objects.

The remainder of the chapter examines that relation in the surviving evidence.

---

# 40.9 The Writings Themselves Record Copying and Circulation

Transmission is not a modern theory imposed on a silent corpus. The writings themselves describe writing, depositing, copying, public reading, and circulation.

Moses is commanded, “Write this for a memorial in a book” (Exodus 17:14). He writes the words of the Lord and reads them in the audience of the people (Exodus 24:4–7). A future king is to “write him a copy of this law in a book out of that which is before the priests the Levites” (Deuteronomy 17:18). Moses writes the law and delivers it to the priests, to be read before Israel at the feast of tabernacles (Deuteronomy 31:9–11). The law is placed in the side of the ark (Deuteronomy 31:24–26). Joshua writes a copy of the law of Moses on stones (Joshua 8:32). Samuel writes the manner of the kingdom in a book and lays it up before the Lord (1 Samuel 10:25). Isaiah is told, “write it before them in a table, and note it in a book, that it may be for the time to come” (Isaiah 30:8). Habakkuk is told, “Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables, that he may run that readeth it” (Habakkuk 2:2).

The New Testament writings exhibit the same public character. Luke writes because many have already taken in hand to set forth a declaration of those things which are most surely believed, as delivered by eyewitnesses (Luke 1:1–4). Paul’s letter to the Colossians is to be read also in the church of the Laodiceans, and the letter from Laodicea is to be read at Colossae (Colossians 4:16). Peter knows a collection of Paul’s epistles and classifies them with “the other scriptures” (2 Peter 3:15–16).

These passages are not, in this chapter, a doctrine of inspiration. They are documentary facts internal to the record:

> **The writings present themselves as public texts, copied, deposited, read aloud, and circulated among congregations and generations.**

A public text of that kind produces a manuscript tradition. The tradition examined below is the historical continuation of the activity the writings already describe.

---

# 40.10 Jeremiah 36 as a Transmission Episode

Jeremiah 36 supplies a concrete episode of writing, destruction, and rewriting.

The Lord commands Jeremiah to take a roll of a book and write against Israel and Judah. Jeremiah dictates. Baruch writes the words from Jeremiah’s mouth (Jeremiah 36:1–4). The roll is read in the temple and then in the hearing of officials (Jeremiah 36:8–19). King Jehoiakim cuts it with a penknife and burns it in the fire (Jeremiah 36:21–23). The Lord commands Jeremiah to take another roll. Baruch writes again “all the words of the book which Jehoiakim king of Judah had burned in the fire: and there were added besides unto them many like words” (Jeremiah 36:32).

Several transmission facts are visible in the narrative.

Writing is material. A roll can be cut and burned.

Writing is transferable. A prophet’s words can be written by a scribe.

Writing is public. The roll is read in institutional settings.

Destruction of a particular copy is not identical with destruction of the words. The words are rewritten.

The second roll is a copy in the sense that it recovers the destroyed writing, and it is more than a mechanical duplicate: further words are added. The episode therefore illustrates both recoverability after the loss of a particular artifact and the distinction between a given material copy and the text that copy carries.

The authentication implication is direct. The non-survival of a particular original artifact is already contemplated inside the record. The question is not whether a given skin or papyrus still exists. The question is whether the writing remains available as a public text.

---

# 40.11 Materials, Use, and the Non-Survival of Autographs

No autograph of a biblical book is known to survive.

That fact has a historical explanation before it has an evidential interpretation.

The earliest biblical writings were made on perishable materials: papyrus, prepared skins, and, in some settings, other organic writing surfaces. Papyrus becomes brittle. Skins wear. Scrolls used in public reading deteriorate. The dry climate of Egypt has preserved papyri that would have perished elsewhere. Palestine and the Judean wilderness have, in particular deposits, preserved skins and papyri in caves. Ordinary use in wetter climates destroys the artifact.

Use is as important as climate. A text that is read, copied, and carried is handled. Complete and correct copies were placed in service and wore out. Worn copies were repaired, washed for reuse as palimpsests, stored, or discarded. Survival of an autograph through two millennia of liturgical and congregational use would have been exceptional. Survival of copies, including copies of copies, is the expected pattern for a living public literature.

The supplied Bible-introduction treatment of manuscripts states the material point: original manuscripts cannot be produced because of perishable writing materials, yet thousands of copies — whole books and portions — have been preserved, especially where climate favored survival. The argument to be taken from that material is historical, not rhetorical. Perishability explains the absence of autographs. It does not explain away the presence of copies.

The non-survival of autographs is therefore the ordinary fate of ancient books that were actually used. It is not, by itself, an evidential crisis. The crisis would be the non-survival of a recoverable tradition.

---

# 40.12 Autographs Are Not Required for Recoverability of a Public Text

A public text can be recovered from copies.

The point is not peculiar to Scripture. Later public documents are known from copies, transcripts, and printings even when a particular original sheet is not in the reader’s hands. The supplied introduction uses the analogy of a later national founding document: one need not produce the original artifact in order to establish that the copies carry the public wording. The analogy is serviceable if it is kept proportional. It establishes a principle of documentary method:

> **Where a text has been public, copied, and widely attested, the original artifact is not the sole condition of knowing the text.**

The biblical case is stronger than the analogy, not weaker, because the tradition is older, more widely copied, and attested in more independent streams.

Classical historical method already accepts the genuineness of ancient works on the basis of far thinner manuscript traditions: a handful of medieval copies, often separated from composition by many centuries. The biblical writings, especially the New Testament in Greek, are attested by thousands of catalogued witnesses, with substantial portions surviving from within a few generations of composition, and with additional streams in versions and quotations. On the ordinary documentary standards by which ancient texts are judged recoverable, this tradition is not borderline. It is abundant.

The warranted conclusion is not that every letter of every autograph is displayed in every copy. It is that the autograph is not a missing condition of investigation. The public text is available through the witnesses.

---

# 40.13 What a Manuscript Establishes as Evidence

Chapter 33 already forbade treating “there are manuscripts” as if it were the whole authentication of Scripture. That prohibition remains. This chapter asks a narrower question: what, exactly, do the manuscripts establish?

A dated manuscript containing a biblical text establishes:

1. that this textual form existed by the date of the copy;
2. that a scribe, community, or commissioner judged the text worth the cost of copying;
3. that the wording of this exemplar, with this scribe’s accidents and corrections, was transmitted at this place;
4. and, when compared with other witnesses, that agreements and disagreements among copies can be mapped.

A second-century fragment of John establishes that a Greek text of those verses of John existed in the second century. A fourth-century pandect establishes that large collections of biblical books were being copied as a corpus on parchment. A medieval minuscule establishes continuity of copying into the age immediately before print.

What a single manuscript does not establish, and is not here asked to establish, is every later doctrinal conclusion of the book. The manuscript answers a documentary question. The documentary question is necessary for authentication because there is no examination of a written claim without a written object.

The tradition as a whole establishes more than any single artifact: independent confirmation of the same works, the same substantial wording, and the same public availability across regions and centuries.

---

# 40.14 Public Tradition versus a Controlled Private Chain

Some ancient texts survive through a narrow channel: one medieval copy, or a small family of copies, in a single linguistic stream.

The biblical tradition is not of that kind.

It is public. The law is to be read before all Israel (Deuteronomy 31:11). The king’s copy is taken from the priests’ copy (Deuteronomy 17:18). Letters circulate among churches (Colossians 4:16). Gospels are written that the reader might know the certainty of those things in which he has been instructed (Luke 1:4).

It is distributed. Hebrew Scriptures were copied in Judea, in the diaspora, and, in translation, in Alexandria. New Testament writings were copied in Egypt, in the Latin West, in the Syriac East, in Ethiopia, in Armenia, in Georgia, and throughout the Greek-speaking churches. No single scriptorium, empire, or century controlled all the copies.

It is early relative to composition. Substantial Hebrew evidence now reaches, for some texts, into the Second Temple period and, for at least one priestly text, into the late monarchic period. Substantial Greek New Testament evidence reaches into the second and third centuries, with fourth-century parchment Bibles already presenting a collected corpus.

A public, distributed, early tradition has a distinctive evidential property:

> **A later alteration must infect independent streams in order to become the only surviving text. Independent streams make wholesale undetected replacement extraordinarily difficult.**

That property is the core of recoverability. It is not a claim that scribes never erred. It is a claim that error is locally detectable because the tradition is not a single private chain.

---

# 40.15 Multiply Attested Transmission

**Multiply attested** means that the same work, and in the great majority of passages the same substantial wording, is witnessed by more than one independent line.

The lines include:

- Hebrew manuscripts of different dates and locales;
- the Samaritan Pentateuch as a related but distinct Hebrew pentateuchal tradition;
- the Septuagint and later Greek Old Testament translations;
- Aramaic Targums;
- Greek New Testament papyri, majuscules, minuscules, and lectionaries;
- ancient versions of the New Testament;
- and quotations, allusions, and lectionary use in early Christian and Jewish writers.

Independence here has the same sense it had in Chapter 39. A Coptic version produced in Egypt is not the same act of copying as a Syriac version produced in the East or a Latin version produced in the West. A second-century papyrus is not the same artifact as a fourth-century parchment codex. A citation in an early writer is not a medieval minuscule.

Agreement across such lines is correspondingly strong. When witnesses that do not derive from one another support the same reading, that reading is multiply attested.

Disagreement is also informative. A disagreement localized to one stream, one region, or one later period is identifiable as such. Multiplicity does not erase variants. It locates them.

The authentication value of multiplicity is therefore not a mere count. It is a structure:

> **Many witnesses, in more than one language, in more than one region, from more than one century, transmitting the same public works.**

That is the opposite of a lost original known only by rumor.

---

# 40.16 The Work of Copying and the Kinds of Variation

Copying is a human act. It produces variation.

The earliest continuous manuscripts are commonly written without the later helps of word-division, punctuation, and chapter-and-verse numbers. Scribes employed abbreviations, including the *nomina sacra* for sacred names. They copied by eye and, in some settings, by ear. They corrected, sometimes from a second exemplar. They introduced accidents.

The ordinary classes of variation are well known to documentary study:

- **Orthography:** spelling, movable letters, itacism in Greek, *plene* and defective spelling in Hebrew.
- **Omission:** skipping a word, a line, or a phrase, especially by homoeoteleuton (similar endings).
- **Addition:** dittography (writing twice), insertion of a marginal note, or expansion.
- **Substitution:** a synonymous word, a familiar parallel phrasing, or a grammatical adjustment.
- **Transposition:** change of word order without change of sense.
- **Harmonization:** especially among the Gospels, where a scribe familiar with a parallel passage may assimilate wording.
- **Correction:** a later hand repairing what it takes to be an error, sometimes restoring an earlier reading, sometimes introducing a new one.

These are the accidents of transmission. They are not a second composition of the Bible. They are the marks of copies.

Some variants are more substantial: a longer or shorter ending, a disputed pericope, a clause present in one stream and absent in another. Those cases are known, published, and discussed. Their existence is part of the evidence, not a fact discovered only by opponents of the record. A tradition that hides nothing of this kind is a public tradition.

---

# 40.17 Variation Is Not Equivalent to Loss of the Text

The inference from “copies differ” to “the original is lost” does not follow.

Difference among copies is what makes comparison possible. If every surviving copy were a unique, unrelated composition, recoverability would fail. That is not the situation. The copies are copies of the same works. The overwhelming bulk of variation is trivial in respect of substance: spelling, article, word order, synonymous particles. A smaller class of variants affects a phrase, a verse, or, in a few famous cases, a longer unit. Those units are identified. The rest of the work is not placed in doubt by them.

The correct inference is:

> **Variation is evidence that the text was copied by many hands in many places. The pattern of variation, together with the pattern of agreement, is evidence that the same public text is being copied.**

A hopelessly lost text would not generate thousands of copies of identifiable books, agreeing in substance across languages and centuries, with a residue of localized, classifiable differences. That is the profile of a transmitted literature, not of a vanished one.

The method that reads the profile is comparison of witnesses. It is ordinary historical method applied to documents. It is not a solvent of the text. It is the means by which the text is recovered.

---

# 40.18 Languages of the Writings

The original-language situation is part of the transmission story.

The Old Testament writings are overwhelmingly Hebrew, with specified Aramaic sections: Ezra 4:8–6:18; 7:12–26; Jeremiah 10:11; Daniel 2:4–7:28. After the exile, Aramaic became increasingly the spoken language of Judean life; Hebrew remained the language of the sacred writings, with Aramaic paraphrase arising as a pastoral and liturgical companion.

The New Testament writings are in the common (Hellenistic) Greek of the eastern Roman world, the commercial and administrative language in which a letter could travel from Corinth to Rome and be read in both. That Greek is the language of composition for the New Testament autographs as historical objects.

Transmission therefore includes:

- copying within the original languages;
- translation out of those languages;
- and, much later, printed publication in both.

A version is not a replacement of the original-language tradition. The Hebrew and Greek manuscript traditions remain the primary documentary base for recoverability of original-language wording. Versions are additional streams. Printed vernacular Bibles are additional streams still further downstream.

The authentication question is recoverable wording of the public text. That wording is sought first in the language of composition, with versions and quotations as independent checks.

---

# 40.19 Early Hebrew Witnesses

The Hebrew Bible’s manuscript history is no longer bounded by the great medieval codices alone.

The silver amulets from Ketef Hinnom, recovered in Jerusalem and dated to the late monarchic period (commonly placed in the late seventh or early sixth century B.C.), bear the priestly blessing known from Numbers 6:24–26. They are not a book-scroll of Numbers. They are written witnesses, in paleo-Hebrew, to a text that later manuscripts transmit as Scripture. They establish that this blessing existed in written form in Judah before the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem.

The Nash Papyrus, commonly dated to the second century B.C., preserves the Decalogue and the Shema. It is a liturgical or instructional text, not a complete Pentateuch. It is nonetheless an early Hebrew witness to material that the later manuscript tradition also carries.

These artifacts do not reconstruct the entire Hebrew Bible by themselves. They establish something more specific and, for authentication, more important than a catalogue:

> **Biblical texts were public written objects in Israel and Second Temple Judaism, not merely medieval productions.**

The great expansion of early Hebrew evidence is the library of the Judean wilderness.

---

# 40.20 The Dead Sea Scrolls as Transmission Evidence

The Dead Sea Scrolls, recovered from caves near Qumran and related sites from 1947 onward, include roughly two hundred biblical manuscripts among a larger collection of Second Temple Jewish writings. They date, in the main, from the third century B.C. to the first century A.D. Every book of the Hebrew Bible is represented except Esther, whose absence from the recovered fragments remains a historical silence rather than a demonstration that Esther was unknown.

The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ) is a substantially complete copy of Isaiah from about the second century B.C. Other Isaiah material, including 1QIsaᵇ, supplies additional witnesses. Chapter 37 already used this evidence to close the claim that Isaiah as a written book is a post-Christian composition. The present use is transmissional.

Comparison of 1QIsaᵃ with the later Masoretic form of Isaiah shows substantial continuity of the book across a span of a millennium. Much of the difference is orthographic and morphological. A smaller class of variants is more substantial. Other Qumran biblical manuscripts align more closely with the later Masoretic tradition; still others show affinities with the Hebrew underlying the Septuagint, or with pentateuchal forms related to the Samaritan tradition.

The disciplined conclusion is twofold, and both sides are positive for recoverability.

First, a public Hebrew text of the biblical books was already being copied, in recognizable form, centuries before the medieval codices. The Masoretic manuscripts are late copies of an early tradition, not the first creation of that tradition.

Second, ancient Hebrew transmission included known plurality of textual forms. Recoverability is recoverability of a public text with a documented history, not the pretense that every ancient copy was letter-identical. The existence of more than one ancient form is itself evidence that the books were widely copied rather than privately invented at a late date.

For authentication, the Scrolls establish that the Hebrew Scriptures are a Second Temple — and, in particular cases, late monarchic — written corpus whose substance is available for comparison with the later standard copies. That is recoverability across the most dangerous chronological gap in the Hebrew evidence, the gap that once stretched from composition to the tenth century A.D.

---

# 40.21 The Masoretic Tradition

The Masoretic Text is the medieval Hebrew tradition of the biblical books, equipped with vocalization, accentuation, and a mass of marginal notes (the Masora) designed to stabilize copying.

The Aleppo Codex, produced in the tenth century A.D. and associated with the ben Asher school, was long the most prized complete manuscript of this tradition; damage in 1947 left it incomplete. The Leningrad Codex (1008/1009 A.D.) is the oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible in this tradition and is the base of widely used printed scholarly editions. Other important witnesses include the Cairo Codex of the Prophets and a large body of medieval manuscripts, together with fragments from the Cairo Geniza.

The Masoretes did not compose the Hebrew Bible. They copied it with an apparatus of control that recorded extraordinary, defective, and unusual forms so that scribes would reproduce them rather than “correct” them. That apparatus is itself transmission evidence: a community treating an already-public text as a text to be preserved, not rewritten.

Because the Dead Sea Scrolls now supply a control a millennium earlier, the Masoretic tradition can be seen as what it is: a carefully copied medieval representative of an ancient text, not an isolated late invention. Where Qumran and the Masora agree, the agreement spans that millennium. Where they differ, the difference is a documented variant among ancient witnesses, not a modern suspicion.

Printed Hebrew Bibles — the Rabbinic Bibles of the sixteenth century, *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, and subsequent scholarly editions — are printed editions of this tradition, with apparatuses. They are not autographs of Moses, Isaiah, or Ezra. They are the public form in which the recovered Hebrew text is now read.

---

# 40.22 Parallel Hebrew and Aramaic Streams

Two further streams belong to Old Testament transmission.

The Samaritan Pentateuch is a Hebrew pentateuchal text preserved in the Samaritan community. Its surviving complete manuscripts are medieval, but the tradition represents an ancient bifurcation in the copying of the Torah. It agrees with the Jewish Hebrew text in the great substance of the Pentateuch. It also exhibits characteristic differences, some of them sectarian, some of them shared with readings known from Qumran or from the Septuagint. For authentication, its value is independence. A rival community, with its own worship and its own copyists, transmitted the same five books.

The Targums are Aramaic paraphrases and translations of the Hebrew Scriptures, arising as Hebrew receded from ordinary speech. They range from relatively close rendering to expansive paraphrase. They are versions, and often interpretive versions. They remain witnesses that particular Hebrew books were public, read, and rendered for congregations who needed them in another tongue.

Neither stream is the autograph. Together with the Masoretic manuscripts, the Qumran copies, and the Greek versions, they constitute a multiply attested Hebrew Bible: Jewish, Samaritan, Greek-speaking, and Aramaic-speaking communities transmitting the same body of writings.

---

# 40.23 The Septuagint and Other Greek Old Testament Versions

The Septuagint is the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, begun with the Pentateuch in Alexandria in the third century B.C. and extended to other books through the following century. Later legend about seventy-two translators is not required for the historical fact: Greek-speaking Jews produced a written Greek Bible before the New Testament period.

Fragments of Greek Deuteronomy (including Papyrus Rylands 458) and other early papyri place the translation, for some books, in the second century B.C. The great fourth- and fifth-century parchment Bibles — Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus — carry substantial Greek Old Testaments. The Greek Minor Prophets scroll from Naḥal Ḥever is a further early witness.

Chapter 37 already used the Septuagint as chronological control: prophetic texts existed in translation before the events of the first century. The present point is transmissional. The Septuagint is:

- a version, and therefore a witness to a Hebrew source then available;
- itself a copied tradition, with its own manuscripts and variants;
- and, because it sometimes reflects a Hebrew form different from the later Masoretic copies, an independent control on the history of the Hebrew text.

In the second century A.D., additional Greek translations and recensions — associated with Aquila, Symmachus, and Theodotion — retranslated or revised the Greek toward forms of the Hebrew then current. Those versions are further evidence of a public Hebrew text being compared with a public Greek text. They are not autographs. They thicken the tradition.

For authentication, the Septuagint establishes that the Hebrew Scriptures were already an international written corpus, translated and copied outside the land of Israel, centuries before medieval Hebrew codices. A text that can be translated is a text that exists. A translation that is then copied for centuries is a public text.

---

# 40.24 New Testament Greek Witnesses: Catalogue and Classes

The Greek New Testament manuscript tradition is catalogued by the Institute for New Testament Textual Research in Münster in the *Kurzgefasste Liste*, the standard register of Gregory–Aland numbers. The register is a living inventory. Entries are added as copies are identified. Some entries are later combined when fragments prove to belong to one artifact. Some entries are removed when they prove to lack New Testament text or to duplicate another number. A published snapshot of that catalogue in 2023 recorded on the order of **5,700** Greek New Testament witnesses. Subtracting copies then tagged as destroyed or long missing yields a somewhat lower figure of copies whose present location is known. The count will move.

Several cautions are required, and they strengthen rather than weaken the argument if they are observed.

The figure is not 5,700 complete New Testaments. It is a count of catalogued handwritten witnesses, many of them fragmentary, some of them lectionaries containing appointed readings rather than continuous text, a few of them amulets or other non-continuous forms.

The four catalogue classes are papyri, majuscules (uncials), minuscules, and lectionaries. Those classes mix material, script, and use. They are a finding-aid, not four different Bibles.

Older popular tallies — for example, a few hundred uncials and a couple of thousand cursives, or a round “about 5,000 Greek manuscripts” — belong to earlier states of the catalogue. They must not be dumped as current facts. The order of magnitude that has remained stable across a century of discovery is the relevant fact: **thousands of Greek witnesses, not dozens.**

That order of magnitude, even after every qualification about fragments, destroyed copies, and duplicate numbers, is documentary abundance. It is the abundance of a public literature.

---

# 40.25 Papyri

The papyri are the earliest class of New Testament copies, written on papyrus, most of them from Egypt, most of them fragmentary. More than one hundred forty such witnesses have been registered. Before 1900 only a handful were known. The twentieth century, through finds at Oxyrhynchus, among the Chester Beatty papyri, among the Bodmer papyri, and in later publications, moved the documentary base of the New Testament into the second and third centuries.

Papyrus 𝔓⁵², a fragment of John 18, is commonly assigned to the second century. Precise year-claims for paleographic dates outrun the method; the disciplined point is early: a Greek text of John was already being copied within a century or so of composition.

Papyrus 𝔓⁴⁶ (Chester Beatty) preserves a substantial collection of Pauline letters, commonly dated around the turn of the third century. 𝔓⁶⁶ (Bodmer) preserves a substantial text of John from a similar horizon. 𝔓⁷⁵ (Bodmer) preserves substantial portions of Luke and John and stands in close agreement with the later parchment Codex Vaticanus, showing that a carefully copied form of those Gospels is not a fourth-century invention. 𝔓⁴⁵ (Chester Beatty) preserves portions of the Gospels and Acts. 𝔓⁴⁷ preserves portions of Revelation. 𝔓¹³⁷ preserves an early fragment of Mark.

No papyrus except the unusual 𝔓⁷² is a complete New Testament book-collection in the later sense; most are broken pages. Completeness is not the evidential requirement. Earliness and distribution are. These copies place the Gospels, Paul, Acts, and other writings in the second and third centuries as circulating Greek books.

The dry climate of Egypt, which the older introduction already noted as a condition of survival, explains why so many of the earliest copies come from one region. Egypt is over-represented among papyri because papyrus survives there. It is not the only place the books were copied. The later parchment tradition, and the versions, show the rest of the map.

---

# 40.26 The Great Uncial Codices

From the fourth century, parchment codices become the principal surviving form of substantial biblical manuscripts.

**Codex Vaticanus** (B, 03), fourth century, contains nearly the entire Greek Bible, the New Testament extending through Hebrews 9:14 in the surviving leaves.

**Codex Sinaiticus** (א, 01), fourth century, recovered in the nineteenth century from St. Catherine’s Monastery at Sinai, contains the Greek Old Testament substantially and the complete New Testament, together with the Epistle of Barnabas and part of the Shepherd of Hermas.

**Codex Alexandrinus** (A, 02), fifth century, contains most of both testaments, with specified lacunae.

**Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus** (C, 04), fifth century, is a palimpsest: a biblical text later overwritten, recoverable in substantial part, lacking some New Testament books.

**Codex Bezae** (D, 05), fifth or sixth century, presents the Gospels and Acts in Greek and Latin columns, with a distinctive textual character that is itself evidence of more than one ancient stream.

**Codex Claromontanus** (D, 06), sixth century, presents the Pauline epistles in Greek and Latin.

These are the manuscripts the older introduction listed as principal uncials. The identifications remain sound. What must not be carried forward from older catalogues is the implication that the uncial class numbers only a little over a hundred copies. The registered majuscule class now runs into the hundreds, most of them fragmentary, a smaller number of them the famous complete or nearly complete Bibles.

The fourth-century pandects establish that the New Testament writings were already being copied as a collected corpus on durable parchment, in the same generation as the first Christian emperors, and that the Old Testament in Greek was being bound with them. That is a transmissional fact about a public library of writings. The artifact is already a collected Bible as a copied object.

---

# 40.27 Minuscules and Lectionaries

From about the ninth century, minuscule (cursive) script becomes the ordinary book-hand of Greek copying. The great majority of surviving continuous-text Greek New Testament manuscripts are minuscules. They carry the text through the Byzantine centuries to the eve of print. They are later than the papyri and the great uncials. They are also numerous, geographically widespread, and, in many cases, carefully produced. A late copy of a stable work is still a copy of that work. Where minuscules agree with early witnesses, they confirm continuity. Where a large later group shares a reading absent from the earliest witnesses, the reading is identifiable as a later majority form. Both comparisons are possible only because the later copies exist.

Lectionaries are manuscripts arranged for reading in the services of the church: appointed portions of the Gospels, Acts, and Epistles. The older introduction noted more than a thousand Gospel lectionaries and some hundreds of Apostolos lectionaries. The modern catalogue places the lectionary class in the thousands, with the same cautions already stated about fragments, liturgical books, and incomplete volumes. Lectionaries are not continuous Bibles. They are evidence of public, repeated, congregational reading of the same writings. A text read aloud, year after year, in many churches, is a public text. Public reading is a mechanism of preservation as well as a source of occasional liturgical smoothing.

Together, minuscules and lectionaries show that transmission did not stop with the fourth century. The books continued to be copied because they continued to be used.

---

# 40.28 Geographic Distribution and Independent Streams

Recoverability is strengthened by geography.

Greek copies survive from Egypt, Sinai, Palestine, Constantinople and the Byzantine world, Greece, southern Italy, and the monasteries of Athos and the East. Latin copies cover the West. Syriac copies cover the churches of the East. Coptic copies cover the Nile valley in more than one dialect. Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, Gothic, and later Slavonic and Arabic versions extend the map again.

No single city could rewrite all of these. A change introduced in a Constantinopolitan minuscule does not automatically appear in a Sahidic Coptic manuscript already copied in Egypt, or in a Syriac manuscript already copied in Edessa, or in a Latin manuscript already copied in Italy. The streams can be compared precisely because they were not one stream.

Textual groupings used in scholarly editions — often called Alexandrian, Western, Byzantine, and related labels — are attempts to describe this geography and genealogy of copying. The labels are tools. They must not be reified into four Bibles. They name tendencies among witnesses. The authentication point does not depend on adopting one scholarly label as decisive. It depends on the existence of more than one tendency:

> **Early diversity of copies, within a recognizable identity of work and substance, is the profile of a text that spread faster than any one copyist could control.**

Controlled late homogenization of a previously chaotic set of unrelated documents is the profile Chapter 38 already judged inadequate for the corpus as literature. It is equally inadequate as an account of the copies. The copies of John are copies of John. Their differences are differences within John.

---

# 40.29 Ancient Versions of the New Testament

The New Testament was translated early.

**Old Latin** translations appear by the second and third centuries, before Jerome. They are multiple, not a single authorized Latin Bible, and that very multiplicity is evidence of demand and of more than one translator working from Greek copies.

**Jerome’s Vulgate**, at the end of the fourth and the beginning of the fifth century, is a learned Latin revision and translation that became the dominant Latin Bible of the medieval West. The Latin manuscript tradition is vast: many thousands of copies. It is a version, not the Greek autograph. It is a major independent stream.

**Syriac** transmission includes the Old Syriac Gospels (the Sinaitic palimpsest and the Curetonian manuscript) and the later standard Peshitta. Older introductions sometimes place “the Peshitta” in the second century as a single event. The disciplined account is more careful: Syriac translation of Gospel material is early; the standardized Peshitta New Testament, especially beyond the Gospels, is a later ecclesiastical text, commonly associated with the fifth century. The authentication value does not require the earliest possible date for every Syriac book. It requires that an Eastern church, in another language, copied and read the same writings.

**Coptic** versions, especially Sahidic in the third and fourth centuries and later Bohairic, render the Greek into the language of Egypt. They are geographically close to many of the papyri and independent of the Latin West.

**Gothic** (fourth century, associated with Ulfilas), **Armenian** (fifth century), **Georgian** (fifth and sixth centuries), and **Ethiopic** (beginning in late antiquity, with a long manuscript history) extend the tradition into still other languages and regions. Arabic translations, from the early Islamic centuries onward, add another vernacular stream.

The older introduction’s list of ancient versions — Septuagint, Targums, Samaritan Pentateuch, Aquila, Theodotion, Symmachus, Peshitta, Arabic, Armenian, Egyptian, Ethiopic, Gothic, Georgian, Latin, Persian — is, as a map of streams, substantially right. Several of its dates need the qualifications just given. The list is transmission history. It is not a claim that every version is equally close to the autograph, and it is not a claim that any one later vernacular printed Bible is the original.

Versions establish that the writings were international public texts. A Greek New Testament that can be read in Latin, Syriac, Coptic, and Gothic in late antiquity is not a local secret.

---

# 40.30 Patristic Quotations as Early Witnesses

Writers of the first centuries quote, allude to, and argue from biblical texts.

Those citations occupy the chronological interval between the autographs and the great parchment Bibles. They are geographically distributed: Asia, Egypt, Rome, North Africa, Syria, Gaul. They are often paraphrastic, from memory, or from a version rather than from a continuous-text Greek manuscript. They are, for that reason, not a substitute for manuscripts. They are an additional stream.

The older introduction states that if the New Testament were destroyed it could be practically reproduced from the quotations of the early writers, and it offers a very large round figure for the number of such quotations. That claim is a popular compression of a real phenomenon. A precise census of “quotations” is not available in the way a catalogue of manuscripts is available, because citation, allusion, paraphrase, and commentary interpenetrate. The disciplined point, which does not require an inflated total, is this:

> **From the second century onward, Christian writers treat particular Gospels, Acts, and epistles as public written authorities, cite their wording, and thereby witness to the early circulation of those books.**

Irenaeus, Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Cyprian, and their contemporaries are not producing the New Testament. They are reading it. Their citations can confirm that a given passage was known in a given form in a given region. Where they agree with the early papyri and the later uncials, the agreement is threefold. Where they preserve a variant, the variant is early enough to matter.

Lectionaries, already treated, belong beside this stream as evidence of public reading. Quotations in commentary and controversy belong as evidence of public argument. Both are uses of a text that is already there.

---

# 40.31 English Bibles as a Later Transmission Chapter

English translation is a late chapter in a much older story. It must be told as transmission history, not as a doctrine that one English printed Bible is the autograph.

Portions of Scripture were rendered into Old English from the Latin Vulgate: Aldhelm’s Psalms in the late seventh century; Bede’s work on the Gospels, including the report of his translating John at the end of his life in 735; Alfred’s work on the Psalms; Ælfric’s rendering of the Pentateuch and historical books in the tenth century. These are versions of a version, made in a language ancestral to modern English.

John Wycliffe and his associates produced the first complete English Bible in the 1380s, from the Vulgate. It circulated in manuscript. It is a complete English version still one step from the original languages.

William Tyndale translated the New Testament from Greek (first printed 1526) and the Pentateuch from Hebrew (1530). That is a decisive transmissional fact: English now meets the languages of composition, not only Latin. Miles Coverdale produced the first complete printed English Bible in 1535. The Geneva Bible (1560), the Bishops’ Bible (1568), and the Roman Catholic Douay–Rheims (New Testament 1582; Old Testament 1609–1610) are further printed versions, from different ecclesiastical and textual bases.

In 1611 the Authorized (King James) Version was published, the work of companies of translators using Hebrew and Greek printed editions together with earlier English versions. It became, for centuries, the dominant English Bible of the Protestant world. Later English printed Bibles — the Revised Version and a long series of modern translations — continue the same activity: rendering a reconstructed original-language text, or revising an earlier rendering, into current English.

Every item in this list is a **version** or a **printed edition of a version**. None is an autograph. None is a Greek or Hebrew manuscript. The history shows the writings remaining public as languages change and as print replaces exclusive dependence on handwriting. It does not establish that the last widely loved English printing is identical with Moses’ book or Paul’s letter.

The older introduction’s preference for one English version as unsurpassed is a judgment about later reception. It is not a transmissional conclusion, and it is not adopted here. Reception of a printed vernacular Bible is a fact of modern reading. Identity with the autograph is a different claim and a false one.

---

# 40.32 Printed Editions of the Original-Language Text

Print also produced editions of Hebrew and Greek.

Erasmus’s *Novum Instrumentum* (1516) is the first published printed Greek New Testament. It rested on a handful of late minuscule manuscripts then available in Basel. Subsequent sixteenth-century editors — including Estienne, Beza, and the Elzevirs — printed Greek texts that came to be called, in Elzevir’s phrase, the *textus receptus*, the received text. That phrase names a printed tradition, not the autograph. The Complutensian Polyglot and later critical editors (Mill, Bengel, Wettstein, Griesbach, Lachmann, Tregelles, Tischendorf, Westcott and Hort, and the Nestle–Aland / United Bible Societies tradition, now joined by the *Editio Critica Maior*) used more manuscripts, then earlier manuscripts, then a fuller catalogue, to print Greek texts with apparatuses of variants.

Printed Hebrew Bibles, from the Rabbinic Bibles of the sixteenth century to *Biblia Hebraica* and its successors, put the Masoretic Text into type, commonly now from the Leningrad Codex, with apparatuses recording Masoretic notes and, in later editions, selected evidence from the Scrolls, the versions, and other witnesses.

These printed original-language editions are the ordinary form in which modern readers meet the recovered text. They are tools of recoverability. They are not the event of inspiration, not the autograph, and not a substitute for the manuscript tradition they report. A critical apparatus is a public display of the tradition’s variation. It is the opposite of a hidden text. It is also the opposite of a confession that the text is lost. An apparatus exists because the witnesses exist and can be compared.

---

# 40.33 Recoverability of Substance

The central positive conclusion can now be stated.

**Substance**, in this chapter, names the identity of the work and the wording that carries its claims, as distinct from the accidents of spelling, word order, and localized variation.

The tradition recovers:

- the identity of the books as the same books;
- the continuity of their wording across independent streams;
- the great body of sentences in which no variant of substance exists;
- and, where a variant of substance exists, a bounded set of documented alternatives rather than an unbounded fog.

Recoverability does not mean that every disputed unit has only one remaining letter-sequence in the witnesses. It means that the public text is available: the investigator can state what the writings say, and can state where a known variant affects a particular unit.

That is the condition required for authentication of a written claim. If the object were not recoverable, Movement V would be investigating a rumor. The object is recoverable. The investigation has a text.

The older introduction’s argument that originals are not required, that copies in great number survive, and that other ancient writings are accepted on far thinner attestation, is here received as a transmissional argument and stated with full force:

> **On the ordinary documentary standards by which ancient texts are judged recoverable, the biblical writings are recoverable. The Hebrew Scriptures are recoverable as a public Second Temple — and, in their earlier composition, monarchic and exilic — written corpus. The New Testament writings are recoverable as a public Greek corpus circulating from the second century, collected in fourth-century Bibles, and copied thereafter in thousands of witnesses and in many languages.**

That is not a modest residual. It is the authentication line this chapter exists to establish.

---

# 40.34 Textual Comparison as Historical Method

The method by which recoverability is exhibited is comparison of witnesses.

The investigator asks, of a given unit of text:

- Which manuscripts, versions, and citations support which reading?
- What is the date, locale, and genealogy of those witnesses?
- Which reading explains the origin of the others?
- Is the variation orthographic, accidental, harmonizing, or a more substantial divergence?

The method is historical. It treats scribes as historical agents and copies as historical artifacts. It does not require a prior theological conclusion in order to operate. It operates because there are artifacts to compare.

Agreement among early, independent, geographically distributed witnesses is strong evidence for an early reading. A reading that appears only in late copies of one stream is identifiable as late in that stream. A longer reading that can be explained as expansion, or a shorter reading that can be explained as omission, is weighed as such. The famous larger units — the ending of Mark, the pericope of the adulteress, the Johannine comma in printed Latin and late Greek copies — are not secret. They are the places where the method is most visible to a modern reader, because printed Bibles often mark them.

Visibility of difficult units is evidence of a public tradition. A tradition that identifies its own disputed places is not a tradition concealing collapse. It is a tradition that can be examined.

---

# 40.35 Identified Remaining Uncertainties

Honesty about local uncertainty belongs to recoverability, not to its denial.

There are units of text where the witnesses divide in a way that leaves more than one early reading standing. There are units where a longer form is strongly attested in one stream and absent in another. There are spellings and names where more than one form is ancient.

These uncertainties are:

- **local**, not global;
- **identified**, not unlimited;
- and **bounded by witnesses**, not by imagination.

They do not place the existence of the Gospel of John, the Epistle to the Romans, or the book of Isaiah in question. They do not render the public historical claims examined in Chapter 39 unreadable. They do not erase the coherent testimony examined in Chapter 38. They are the residue that a multiply attested handwritten tradition is expected to have.

A method that can point to the residual units is a method that has already recovered the rest. The residual is not the whole.

---

# 40.36 The Hopeless-Corruption Account Fails

One rival account of the tradition must be judged with full force.

The hopeless-corruption account claims that because autographs have perished, and because copies differ, the original wording is in principle unrecoverable, and the object in the reader’s hands is a late, uncontrolled, or indefinitely altered substitute.

That account fails for the conjunction of the facts already established.

It fails because **absence of autographs is the ordinary fate of used ancient books**, not a special catastrophe of these books.

It fails because **copies exist in documentary abundance**, in Greek by the thousands of catalogued witnesses, and in other languages by many thousands more.

It fails because **early witnesses exist**: Hebrew texts from Qumran and earlier; Greek New Testament papyri from the second and third centuries; fourth-century parchment Bibles.

It fails because **independent streams exist**: Hebrew, Samaritan, Greek, Latin, Syriac, Coptic, and the rest. A late rewrite cannot silently occupy all of them.

It fails because **the actual profile of variation is the profile of copying**, not of free recomposition. The books remain the same books. The substance remains the same substance. The disputed units are listed.

It fails because **quotations and public reading** place the same writings in the second and third centuries as already-authoritative public texts.

A hopelessly corrupted tradition would not look like this. It would not yield identifiable works, early copies, cross-linguistic agreement, and a residue of classified variants. The hopeless-corruption account is inadequate to the evidence. It is not a cautious restatement of the existence of variants. It is a global inference those variants do not support.

---

# 40.37 The One-Printed-Edition-as-Autograph Account Fails

The opposite rival also fails, and with equal force.

The one-printed-edition-as-autograph account claims, in substance, that a particular printed Bible — commonly a particular English printed Bible, sometimes a particular printed Greek or Hebrew edition — *is* the original writing, so that later comparison of manuscripts is either unnecessary or hostile.

That account fails by the fourfold distinction.

A printed English Bible is a **version of a printed original-language edition**, itself reconstructed from **manuscripts**, which are **copies**. The chain has four steps. Collapsing the last step into the first is a category error.

It fails historically. Erasmus’s Greek New Testament is a 1516 printing from a few late copies. The King James Bible is a 1611 English translation, itself revised in later printings, dependent on Tyndale and other English versions and on printed Hebrew and Greek. *Biblia Hebraica* is a typesetting of a medieval codex with an apparatus. Nestle–Aland is a scholarly reconstruction with an apparatus. Each is an edition. None is Moses’ autograph, Isaiah’s autograph, or Paul’s autograph.

It fails evidentially. If one printed edition were identical with the autograph, the thousands of earlier witnesses would be either superfluous or threatening. They are neither. They are the reason the printed edition can be made at all. Print publishes a recovered text. It does not create the text in 1516 or 1611.

It fails as a confusion of language and of date. A vernacular printed Bible is a rendering into a later tongue. The writings are recoverable as Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek public texts. English printed Bibles are how those texts are now widely read in English. A version has its proper place as a version. That place is not identity with the autograph.

The two rivals stand or fall together in this respect: both refuse the actual relation of autograph, copy, version, and printed edition. Hopeless-corruption refuses recoverability. Edition-as-autograph refuses history. The manuscript tradition refutes both.

---

# 40.38 Other Inadequate Rivals

Several lesser accounts fail for the same evidence.

### A single lost original known only by rumor

The writings are not rumored titles. They are copied books with names, opening lines, and continuous text across witnesses.

### A late wholesale rewrite

A rewrite imposed in one century or one empire does not explain Qumran Hebrew, second-century papyri, Old Latin, Coptic, and Syriac at once. The chronology of the witnesses is too early and too wide.

### Translation as original

The Septuagint is a translation of Hebrew, not the Hebrew autograph. The Vulgate is a translation of Hebrew and Greek, not the autograph. An English Bible is a translation still further downstream. Versions are witnesses. They are not the original-language writing.

### Variation as proof of many unrelated Bibles

Witnesses of John that differ in spelling, in a clause, or even in a pericope remain witnesses of John. The identity of the work is not dissolved by the existence of variants within the work.

### Catalogues as complete New Testaments

The thousands of Greek witnesses are not thousands of identical complete Bibles. Treating every fragment as a full Bible would overstate. Treating the fragments as nothing would understate. They are what they are: early and later copies of portions of a public text, and as such they contribute to recoverability.

These lesser rivals do not need a separate later verdict. They fail now, as descriptions of the tradition.

---

# 40.39 What Transmission Evidence Establishes for Authentication

Transmission evidence establishes a specified authentication proposition:

> **The written object under investigation is recoverable as a public text.**

That proposition has several integrated parts.

It establishes **documentary existence**: these writings existed as writings and continued to exist as writings.

It establishes **continuity**: later copies are copies of earlier texts of the same works, not free substitutions of different works.

It establishes **publicity**: the tradition is not a private chain. It is distributed, liturgical, translational, and citational.

It establishes **recoverability of substance**: the investigator can examine what the writings claim because the claims are available in a recoverable wording.

It therefore establishes the condition on which the rest of Movement V operates. Coherence (Chapter 38) is coherence of a text. Correspondence (Chapter 39) is correspondence of claims in a text. Testimony, prophecy, miracles, and witness are claims in a text. If the text were not recoverable, those lines would have no stable object. The object is stable enough to examine.

Transmission evidence bears upon authentication as **recoverability of the Graphē as Graphē**: as writing. That is the line. It is a full line.

---

# 40.40 What Is Explicit and What Is Inferred

### Explicit in the record and in the artifacts

- The writings describe themselves as written, copied, deposited, read, and circulated.
- Jeremiah 36 narrates the destruction of a roll and the rewriting of the words.
- Surviving manuscripts exist, in Hebrew, Greek, and many versions, from a long chronological range.
- Autographs are not among the surviving artifacts.
- Copies differ in identifiable ways.
- Printed editions exist and are historically datable.

### Inferred by this chapter

- That the autographs perished by ordinary material and use-conditions of ancient books.
- That the surviving witnesses, compared as independent streams, recover the substance of the public text.
- That hopeless-corruption is inadequate to the abundance, earliness, distribution, and profile of the tradition.
- That any identification of one printed edition with the autograph is a category error refuted by the history of copies and versions.
- That recoverability of the public text is an authentication fact of full force.

The inference is historical and documentary. It follows from the artifacts and from the public character of the writings. It is abductive in the sense Chapter 39 already used: an inference to the best explanation of the tradition as it actually exists.

---

# 40.41 The Authentication Function of Transmission

Transmission functions as an authentication line when it is specified:

> **The writings claimed as Graphē are available as a recoverable public text, attested by many independent witnesses.**

That line establishes that the coherent, historically embedded testimony already examined is not an unrecoverable past utterance. It is a present written object.

A claimed revelation in history that could not be recovered as wording would be evidentially weaker at the point of examination. This record can be examined. The copies, versions, and quotations make the examination possible.

The next line, by sequence, is the gathering of the independent authentication lines already drawn:

> **What follows when recoverability is placed together with coherence, correspondence, testimony, prophecy, and the related evidential strands of Movement V?**

The authentication structure is therefore:

> **Coherent testimony (Chapter 38) → correspondence with independently attested public history (Chapter 39) → recoverability of the transmitted text (Chapter 40) → cumulative evidential case (Chapter 41).**

This chapter’s line is manuscripts, transmission, and preservation as recoverability of a public text.

By sequence, not by weakness of the evidence here, later chapters examine:

Those questions remain later because they are different questions. Recoverability of a public text is already established as an authentication line. Sequence continues the case. It does not reopen the documentary tradition as doubtful.

This chapter has not established that every copy is letter-identical with every other copy. It has established that the tradition is public and multiply attested, that the substance of the writings is recoverable, and that the two global rivals — hopeless corruption, and one printed edition as autograph — fail.

# 40.43 Apply the Standard

The reader should now be able to take any proposed manuscript “proof” or any proposed manuscript “disproof” and ask:

> **Which of the four objects is in view — autograph, copy, version, or printed edition?**

Then:

> **What does this witness actually contain, and from what date and stream?**

Then:

> **Is the claim about a local variant, or about the recoverability of the work?**

Then:

> **Do independent streams confirm the substance?**

Then:

> **Does a rival explanation account for this witness and for the conjunction of witnesses?**

If a witness is early and independent, it bears with full force on the existence of that textual form at that date.

If a variant is real, it is classified and localized; it is not converted into a global loss of the text.

If a printed edition is excellent, it is commended as an edition. It is not identified with the autograph.

The discipline is the same discipline the book has required throughout Movement V:

> **Infer from the actual evidence what that evidence can bear. Do not ask a copy to be the autograph. Do not refuse the copies what they do establish: a recoverable public text.**

# What Has Been Established

This chapter has established:

1. **Autograph, copy, version, and printed edition are four distinct objects, related as a historical sequence, not as four names for one thing.**
2. **The autograph is the original written composition. Surviving biblical manuscripts are copies. Translations are versions. Typeset Bibles are printed editions.**
3. **The writings themselves record writing, depositing, copying, public reading, and circulation. Transmission is the historical continuation of that public character.**
4. **Jeremiah 36 exhibits material writing, destruction of a particular roll, and rewriting of the words: loss of an artifact is not identical with loss of the text.**
5. **Autographs are not known to survive, for ordinary reasons of perishable materials and use. Their absence is not, by itself, an evidential crisis.**
6. **Autographs are not required for recoverability of a public text. Copies, versions, and quotations can recover the wording.**
7. **A manuscript is a historical artifact establishing a textual form at a date and place, and, in comparison with other witnesses, a map of agreement and variation.**
8. **The biblical tradition is public, distributed, and multiply attested, not a single controlled private chain.**
9. **Variation among copies is the expected result of handwriting. It is not equivalent to loss of the text. Most variation is accidental; more substantial variants are local and identified.**
10. **The Hebrew Scriptures are attested by late-monarchic writing (Ketef Hinnom), Second Temple manuscripts (including the Dead Sea Scrolls), the Masoretic codices, the Samaritan Pentateuch, Targums, and the Septuagint and later Greek versions.**
11. **The Dead Sea Scrolls close the millennium between Qumran and the medieval Hebrew codices as a gap of ignorance: a public Hebrew text of the biblical books was already being copied in recognizable form.**
12. **The Greek New Testament is catalogued in a living register on the order of 5,700 witnesses as of a 2023 snapshot, in four classes (papyri, majuscules, minuscules, lectionaries). The figure is not 5,700 complete New Testaments. The order of magnitude is thousands of Greek copies, not dozens. Older popular tallies are superseded as current counts.**
13. **Early papyri place Gospels, Paul, and other writings in the second and third centuries. Fourth-century parchment Bibles already copy a collected corpus.**
14. **Ancient versions — Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Gothic, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, and others — are independent streams, not autographs. Their dates must be stated carefully; their existence as a map of public translation stands.**
15. **Patristic quotations are early, geographically distributed witnesses, often paraphrastic, not a substitute for manuscripts, and not a precise census. They confirm early public reading of the same books.**
16. **English Bibles from Old English fragments through Wycliffe, Tyndale, Coverdale, Geneva, Douay–Rheims, the 1611 Authorized Version, and later printed translations are versions and printed editions. Transmission history does not confer autograph-status on any English title page.**
17. **Printed Hebrew and Greek editions, from Erasmus and the Rabbinic Bibles to modern critical texts, are tools of recoverability with apparatuses. They are not autographs.**
18. **The substance of the public text is recoverable. Remaining uncertainties are local, identified, and bounded by witnesses.**
19. **Hopeless-corruption fails for the conjunction of abundance, earliness, independent streams, and the actual profile of variation.**
20. **Identifying any one printed edition with the autograph fails as a category error and as a historical claim.**
21. **Transmission evidence establishes recoverability of the Graphē as a written object. That recoverability is an authentication line of full force. Later chapters complete other lines by sequence because those lines are different questions, not because the documentary evidence is weak.**

# Chapter Summary

> **The chapter’s central conclusion:** Autograph, copy, version, and printed edition are distinct. The biblical writings survive as a public, multiply attested manuscript tradition — Hebrew and Greek copies, ancient versions, and early quotations — whose substance is recoverable. Hopeless-corruption fails. The identification of any one printed edition with the autograph fails. Recoverability of the public text is an authentication fact: there is a written object to investigate.

The essential evidential architecture is:

> **Original writing → public copying and circulation → surviving independent witnesses → comparison → recoverability of substance → authentication of the written object as available.**

The governing distinctions are:

- **Autograph** is the original composition.
- **Copy** is a handwritten manuscript of that composition.
- **Version** is a translation.
- **Printed edition** is a typeset publication of a reconstructed text or of a translation.
- **Recoverability** is availability of the public text in substance, not letter-identity of every copy.
- **Multiplicity** is independent attestation across regions, centuries, and languages.

The decisive principle is:

> **Infer from the actual tradition what that tradition can bear. The copies establish a recoverable public text. They do not become the autograph. Sequence then asks what the recovered, historically embedded, coherent testimony warrants as a cumulative evidential case.**
