# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 23 — God Speaking: The Audible Voice

> **Movement IV — How the Written Record Was Given**

---

## Chapter Assignment

The preceding movement established the object of the investigation.

We have examined:

- the Bible as a divine library and written record;
- its historical character;
- its eyewitness and apostolic testimony;
- its own claims concerning its origin;
- **γραφή (*graphē*)**;
- Holy Writ;
- Scripture;
- the Scriptures;
- the Bible as a corpus;
- and the Written Word.

Chapter 22 therefore established the written authority under investigation.

The question now changes.

We are no longer asking primarily:

> **What is the Bible?**

We are asking:

> **How was the written record given?**

The supplied biblical framework identifies several modes through which divine communication enters the historical record. The first is the most direct:

> **God speaks.**

Chapter 23 therefore examines the biblical presentation of **direct divine speech**, with particular attention to:

- **Exodus 19:19**
- **Deuteronomy 5**
- **Matthew 3:16–17**
- **John 12:28**

The purpose is not to collapse every mode of revelation into audible speech. Nor is it to explain inspiration, canon, or textual transmission prematurely.

The purpose is narrower and foundational:

> **To establish from the biblical record that God is presented as speaking directly to human beings, that such speech is received as genuine divine communication, and that this provides a concrete beginning point for understanding how divine communication can enter the historical process that ultimately produces written testimony.**

---

# Central Objective

> **To examine the biblical evidence for direct divine speech and establish God speaking audibly as a genuine mode of divine communication, while clearly distinguishing God's speech, human hearing, witnessing, transmission, and subsequent written testimony.**

The chapter must therefore establish five things:

1. **The Bible presents God as speaking.**
2. **The speech is presented as originating from God.**
3. **Human beings can hear and receive that communication.**
4. **Such communication can become part of remembered, witnessed, transmitted, and written testimony.**
5. **Direct divine speech is one mode of divine communication, not the whole doctrine of revelation or inspiration.**

---

# 23.1 The First Question: Does God Speak?

The investigation begins with a simple question:

> **Does the biblical record actually present God as speaking to human beings?**

The answer is not derived from philosophical speculation.

It is found repeatedly in the biblical narratives themselves.

The language is concrete.

God:

- speaks;
- answers;
- commands;
- declares;
- calls;
- addresses;
- and communicates.

Human beings:

- hear;
- listen;
- answer;
- obey;
- remember;
- testify;
- and transmit what was communicated.

This matters because divine revelation is not presented merely as an abstract idea occurring inside human consciousness.

The biblical record presents God as an active communicator.

The first mode examined in this movement is therefore:

> **God speaking with a voice.**

---

# 23.2 Divine Speech Is Communication

Speech is communication.

When one person speaks to another, information, command, warning, promise, instruction, testimony, or declaration is communicated.

When Scripture presents God as speaking, it presents divine communication as having an identifiable source and recipient.

The basic structure is:

> **God speaks**
>
> ↓
>
> **a recipient hears**
>
> ↓
>
> **the communication is understood or interpreted**
>
> ↓
>
> **the recipient can respond**
>
> ↓
>
> **the event can become testimony**

This is significant for the Doctrine of the Graphē because the written record does not emerge from nowhere.

There is a historical sequence.

Before a divine communication can be recorded, there must be something communicated.

Before there is testimony concerning a communication, there must be something experienced, received, heard, seen, or otherwise disclosed.

The Bible's presentation of direct divine speech therefore gives us a concrete starting point.

---

# 23.3 God Answers by a Voice — Exodus 19:19

Exodus 19 places Israel at Mount Sinai.

The setting is extraordinary.

Israel has come out of Egypt.

The people are assembled at Sinai.

The mountain is associated with the manifestation of God's presence.

The chapter describes thunderings, lightnings, a thick cloud, the sound of a trumpet, and the trembling of the people.

Then Exodus 19:19 records:

> **“Moses spake, and God answered him by a voice.”**

The statement is remarkably direct.

The text does not merely say that Moses experienced a private religious impression.

It says:

> **Moses spoke.**

And:

> **God answered.**

And the answer was:

> **“by a voice.”**

The narrative therefore presents a communicative exchange.

The structure is:

> **Moses speaks**
>
> ↓
>
> **God answers**
>
> ↓
>
> **God's answer comes by a voice**

This is direct divine speech.

---

# 23.4 The Significance of “God Answered”

The verb matters to the narrative.

Moses speaks.

God answers.

The event is therefore dialogical.

The text presents God as responding to human speech.

That is a different category from a human being independently constructing a theory about God.

The source of the communication is identified within the narrative:

> **God.**

The direction is identified:

> **God → Moses.**

The mode is identified:

> **voice.**

The response is identifiable:

> **God answered.**

The biblical claim is therefore concrete enough to be historically described and investigated.

---

# 23.5 Sinai as a Public Covenant Setting

The Sinai setting also matters.

This is not an isolated mystical statement detached from a historical narrative.

The speech occurs in the context of the establishment of Israel's covenant relationship with God.

Exodus 19 introduces the covenant setting, and the following chapters contain commandments and covenant instructions.

The communication therefore has consequences.

God's speech is not presented merely as sound.

It communicates authoritative content.

The sequence becomes:

> **Divine presence**
>
> ↓
>
> **Divine speech**
>
> ↓
>
> **Human reception**
>
> ↓
>
> **Divine command and covenant instruction**
>
> ↓
>
> **Preservation and transmission**

This relationship between speech and written covenant testimony becomes especially important in Deuteronomy.

---

# 23.6 Deuteronomy 5 — Israel Heard the Voice

Deuteronomy 5 recounts the covenant encounter at Horeb.

Moses speaks to Israel concerning what happened when they stood before the LORD.

The chapter repeatedly emphasizes that Israel heard God's voice.

Deuteronomy 5:24 records their response:

> **“Behold, the LORD our God hath shewed us his glory and his greatness, and we have heard his voice out of the midst of the fire.”**

The claim is explicit.

Israel did not merely receive a later report that God had spoken.

The text presents the people themselves as having heard.

This creates a significant evidential category:

> **collective reception of divine speech.**

---

# 23.7 A People Hearing, Not Merely One Individual

Deuteronomy 5 is particularly important because the event is presented in a communal setting.

The text says:

> **“Ye have seen that God talked with you out of the midst of the fire.”**

Moses then recalls the people's fear and their request that Moses serve as mediator.

The biblical narrative therefore distinguishes between:

- God speaking;
- the people hearing;
- the people responding;
- and Moses subsequently serving as mediator.

This is not a private inward experience.

It is presented as a covenant event involving a community.

That makes the passage especially important for understanding how divine communication can become historical testimony.

---

# 23.8 Hearing and Mediation

Deuteronomy 5 also introduces an important distinction that will become increasingly important in later chapters.

The people hear God's voice.

Yet Moses also occupies a mediating role.

The people say, in effect, that they do not want to continue hearing the voice directly and ask Moses to hear from God and communicate God's words to them.

Thus the biblical record already contains two related but distinct modes:

> **direct divine speech to the people**

and:

> **divine communication received and transmitted through a human mediator.**

Chapter 23 concerns the first.

Chapter 24 will develop the second through angels and prophetic mediation.

This distinction must remain clear.

---

# 23.9 What the Sinai Record Establishes

The Sinai passages establish several propositions.

### Proposition 1

The Bible presents God as personally communicating.

### Proposition 2

The mode of communication can be audible speech.

### Proposition 3

Human beings can hear that speech.

### Proposition 4

The communication can contain authoritative commands and covenantal instruction.

### Proposition 5

The communication can be transmitted through human agency.

### Proposition 6

The event can become part of a continuing historical record.

These propositions do not yet establish every later doctrine concerning Scripture.

But they establish the first stage of the process.

---

# 23.10 From Spoken Communication to Written Testimony

The relationship between speech and writing is crucial.

God's speech is not itself identical to the later manuscript.

Nor is the manuscript the event itself.

There is a historical sequence.

For example:

> **God speaks**
>
> ↓
>
> **people hear**
>
> ↓
>
> **the communication is understood**
>
> ↓
>
> **the recipients remember and testify**
>
> ↓
>
> **the communication is transmitted**
>
> ↓
>
> **the testimony is written**
>
> ↓
>
> **the written testimony becomes available to later generations**

This is one of the fundamental transitions that Movement IV must eventually explain in full.

Chapter 23 establishes the first stage.

It does not yet attempt to answer every subsequent stage.

---

# 23.11 The Written Record Does Not Begin as Ink

This point deserves emphasis.

The existence of a written record does not mean that the first form of every divine communication was writing.

A message can begin as:

- speech;
- proclamation;
- vision;
- dream;
- encounter;
- prophetic revelation;
- apostolic testimony;

and only later become written.

Therefore the origin of the written record must be studied as a process.

The biblical record presents God as communicating through different modes.

The written Scriptures preserve and transmit the resulting testimony.

This is why the sequence of Movement IV matters.

---

# 23.12 The Voice From Heaven — Matthew 3:16–17

The New Testament provides another direct example.

At the baptism of Jesus, Matthew 3:16–17 records:

- the heavens opening;
- the Spirit of God descending like a dove;
- and a voice from heaven.

The passage concludes with the declaration:

> **“This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.”**

The source of the voice is identified:

> **from heaven.**

The content is identifiable.

The setting is identifiable.

The event is connected to the public ministry of Jesus.

The New Testament therefore continues the biblical pattern in which God communicates through a voice.

---

# 23.13 The Father's Declaration

The statement at the baptism is not incidental.

It identifies Jesus publicly as:

> **“my beloved Son.”**

It also expresses divine approval:

> **“in whom I am well pleased.”**

The voice therefore communicates propositional content.

It is not merely an unexplained sound.

The biblical narrative identifies:

- who is speaking;
- what is being declared;
- and the historical setting in which it occurs.

The event becomes part of the Gospel testimony.

---

# 23.14 Divine Speech at the Beginning of Christ's Public Ministry

The placement of the event is also significant.

Jesus' baptism marks the beginning of His public ministry.

The voice from heaven therefore functions as a divine declaration concerning Christ at a decisive point in the Gospel narrative.

This illustrates a broader principle:

> **Divine speech can identify, authorize, declare, confirm, command, or reveal.**

The precise function depends upon the context.

In Matthew 3, the declaration concerns the identity and approval of the Son.

---

# 23.15 John 12:28 — The Father Answers the Son

John 12 provides another direct example.

Jesus says:

> **“Father, glorify thy name.”**

The text then records:

> **“Then came there a voice from heaven, saying, I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again.”**

Again the pattern is direct.

> **Jesus speaks to the Father**
>
> ↓
>
> **the Father answers**
>
> ↓
>
> **the answer comes as a voice from heaven**

This is closely analogous to Exodus 19:19:

> **human speech → divine response → voice.**

The New Testament therefore presents direct divine speech not merely as an Old Testament phenomenon.

---

# 23.16 The Crowd's Interpretation

John 12:29 records that the people standing nearby heard the voice but interpreted the sound differently.

Some said:

> **“It thundered.”**

Others said:

> **“An angel spake to him.”**

This detail is important because it shows that:

> **hearing a sound and correctly identifying its source are not identical acts.**

The text nevertheless provides Jesus' interpretation of the event.

He explains:

> **“This voice came not because of me, but for your sakes.”**

The event is therefore interpreted within the narrative itself as divine communication.

This distinction is useful for the larger evidential method.

An event may be:

1. experienced;
2. perceived;
3. interpreted;
4. reported;
5. and later examined.

These are related but distinct stages.

---

# 23.17 Divine Communication and Human Reception

The biblical examples reveal a recurring structure.

### Exodus 19

> Moses speaks → God answers by a voice.

### Deuteronomy 5

> Israel hears God's voice in the covenant encounter.

### Matthew 3

> A voice from heaven declares the Father's testimony concerning the Son.

### John 12

> Jesus speaks to the Father → a voice from heaven answers.

The cumulative pattern is unmistakable:

> **The Bible presents God as an active speaker who communicates with human beings through an identifiable voice.**

This is not a marginal biblical idea.

It occurs in foundational covenant history and in the public ministry of Christ.

---

# 23.18 Voice and Revelation

Direct speech is a mode by which something is made known.

When God speaks, He can reveal:

- His will;
- His commands;
- His identity;
- His purposes;
- His approval;
- His promises;
- His warnings;
- or other truths.

Therefore:

> **Audible divine speech is a mode of revelation.**

But the terms should not be collapsed.

**Revelation** is the broader category.

**Audible divine speech** is one mode through which revelation may occur.

Chapter 29 will examine revelation as a broader biblical category.

---

# 23.19 Speech Is Not the Entire Doctrine of Revelation

The Bible presents other modes of divine communication.

The supplied framework identifies:

- angels;
- prophets;
- Jesus Christ;
- apostles;
- visions;
- dreams;
- revelation;
- inspiration;
- and written testimony.

Therefore the conclusion of Chapter 23 must remain properly bounded.

We have established:

> **God speaks.**

We have not established:

> **God communicates only by audible speech.**

Nor:

> **Every Scripture was first delivered as an audible voice.**

The evidence does not require either conclusion.

The correct conclusion is:

> **Direct divine speech is one genuine biblical mode of divine communication.**

---

# 23.20 The Human Recipient Is Not the Source

The biblical narratives carefully identify the source of the communication.

In Exodus:

> **God answered.**

In Matthew:

> **a voice from heaven.**

In John:

> **a voice from heaven.**

This distinction matters.

The human recipient is not thereby the originator of the communication.

Moses speaks and God answers.

Israel hears God's voice.

Jesus speaks and the Father answers.

Therefore:

> **Human participation in receiving divine communication does not make the human recipient the source of that communication.**

This principle becomes foundational when later chapters examine prophets and apostles.

---

# 23.21 Hearing Does Not Make the Human Hearer the Author

A person can receive a message without originating it.

A messenger can transmit a command without inventing the command.

A witness can testify concerning an event without being the cause of the event.

Likewise, the biblical model permits a distinction between:

> **divine source**

and:

> **human recipient or transmitter.**

This distinction is central to understanding the divine-human character of Scripture already established in Chapter 19.

The human agent is genuinely involved.

God remains the claimed source.

---

# 23.22 Divine Agency and Human Agency

The Bible therefore gives us a pattern that will recur throughout Movement IV.

> **God communicates.**

> **Human beings receive.**

> **Human beings can transmit.**

> **Human beings can witness.**

> **Human beings can write.**

The existence of human agency is not evidence that divine agency is absent.

Nor does divine agency eliminate genuine human participation.

The two must be kept distinct.

This will become increasingly important when the chapter moves from direct speech to:

- angels;
- prophets;
- Christ;
- apostles;
- visions;
- dreams;
- and finally inspiration.

---

# 23.23 Direct Speech and Witness

A spoken communication can become testimony when someone who heard it reports what occurred.

This is already visible in the Sinai tradition.

Israel hears.

Moses hears.

The people respond.

The event is remembered and transmitted.

The later biblical record presents the event to subsequent generations.

The testimony therefore has a historical structure:

> **event → reception → memory → testimony → record.**

The written record preserves the testimony.

This is one reason the eyewitness and historical dimensions established in Chapters 17 and 18 are relevant here.

---

# 23.24 Direct Divine Speech and Historical Testimony

Chapter 17 established that biblical claims about events, persons, places, declarations, and actions are historical claims capable of investigation.

Chapter 18 established the importance of eyewitness and apostolic testimony.

Chapter 23 now gives a concrete example of the kind of event that testimony can concern:

> **a claimed divine speech event.**

The question therefore becomes historically intelligible.

A biblical account may claim:

> **God spoke.**

The historical investigation can then ask:

- What does the account claim happened?
- Who was present?
- Who heard?
- What was said?
- What was the setting?
- Who transmitted the testimony?
- How was the account preserved?
- What corroborating evidence exists?

Those questions do not deny the event.

They define the evidential task.

---

# 23.25 Internal Biblical Claim and External Authentication

The methodology established earlier requires an important distinction.

### Internal claim

The biblical text says:

> **God spoke.**

### Historical claim

The text presents a particular event as having occurred at a particular time and place involving particular people.

### Evidential question

What evidence bears upon the historical reliability of that account?

### Cumulative conclusion

The weight of the evidence is evaluated across multiple independent lines.

Thus Chapter 23 is not required to prove every Sinai or baptismal detail by external evidence before it can identify what the biblical text claims.

Its first task is to establish the biblical category and the content of the claim.

The later authentication movement will evaluate the evidential strength of such historical claims.

---

# 23.26 Why This Is Not Circular Reasoning

There is a difference between saying:

> **“The Bible says God spoke, therefore God spoke.”**

and saying:

> **“The Bible records a claim that God spoke; therefore we must first identify precisely what the claim is before examining its historical warrant.”**

The second is methodologically sound.

The biblical testimony is evidence within the cumulative case.

It is not the entire authentication argument by itself.

The investigation therefore proceeds in stages:

> **identify the claim**
>
> ↓
>
> **understand the claim**
>
> ↓
>
> **identify the witnesses and transmission**
>
> ↓
>
> **examine historical and external evidence**
>
> ↓
>
> **evaluate the cumulative case**

This preserves the anti-circularity protocol.

---

# 23.27 What the Audible Voice Establishes About Divine Communication

The biblical examples establish several important characteristics.

### 1. Divine communication is intentional

God speaks for a purpose.

### 2. Divine communication can be propositional

God communicates identifiable content.

### 3. Divine communication can be personal

God addresses particular people.

### 4. Divine communication can be public

The Sinai and Gospel examples are not presented merely as private inward impressions.

### 5. Divine communication can be covenantal

Sinai demonstrates this especially clearly.

### 6. Divine communication can identify and confirm

Matthew 3 demonstrates this in relation to Christ.

### 7. Divine communication can respond

Exodus 19:19 and John 12:28 demonstrate divine response.

### 8. Divine communication can become testimony

The events are subsequently preserved in the biblical record.

These observations form the foundation for the chapters that follow.

# 23.28 The Sinai Pattern and the Written Record

The Sinai account is especially important because the broader biblical narrative connects God's spoken commandments with written covenant testimony.

The written record can preserve what God has communicated.

---

# 23.29 The Written Record Extends the Reach of the Event

Those present at Sinai could hear the voice directly.

Later generations could not.

Yet later generations could receive the testimony.

This is one of the great functions of writing.

Writing allows a communication associated with a historical event to be transmitted beyond the original audience.

Therefore:

> **The written record extends the historical reach of testimony beyond the generation that first experienced the event.**

This principle will become increasingly important when we examine prophets, apostles, and the production of written Scripture.

---

# 23.30 God Speaking and the Reliability of God's Word

The earlier chapters established the character of God as truthful and unable to lie.

Therefore the reasoning now becomes coherent:

> **God is true.**

> **God cannot lie.**

> **God speaks.**

Therefore:

> **When God genuinely speaks, the content of that communication is trustworthy because its source is trustworthy.**

But one qualification remains essential:

> **The trustworthiness of genuine divine speech does not automatically authenticate every human claim that someone has heard God speak.**

The source and the claim must be distinguished.

This distinction will become increasingly important as we examine prophets, apostles, visions, dreams, and claimed revelation.

---

# 23.31 Genuine Divine Speech and Claims Made in God's Name

There is a necessary difference between:

> **God actually speaking**

and:

> **a person claiming that God spoke.**

The first concerns divine communication.

The second concerns a human claim about divine communication.

Therefore the investigation must distinguish:

> **divine source**

from:

> **human attribution.**

This is not a reason to doubt the biblical record.

It is the reason the later authentication process matters.

A genuine divine message is trustworthy because God is trustworthy.

A claimed divine message must be established as genuine.

---

Chapter 23 therefore establishes the first mode in the sequence.

Each must be examined on its own terms.

# 23.35 The Necessary Conclusion

The evidence leads to a clear conclusion:

> **The Bible presents direct divine speech as a genuine historical mode of God's communication. At Sinai, God answers Moses by a voice; Israel is presented as hearing God's voice; at Jesus' baptism a voice from heaven declares the Father's testimony concerning the Son; and in John 12 a voice from heaven responds to Jesus. These accounts establish that divine communication can originate with God, be received by human beings, and subsequently enter the historical process of testimony and written preservation.**

The conclusion does not require us to collapse:

> **speech**

> **Scripture.**

> **hearing**

into:

> **inspiration.**

# Methodological Guardrails

### 1. Source before inference

The chapter begins with what the biblical texts actually present and then draws conclusions from those presentations.

### 2. Claim → evidence → inference → conclusion

The existence of a biblical claim is distinguished from the historical warrant for that claim.

### 3. Divine source → human reception

The human participant's role does not erase the biblical identification of God as the source of the communication.

### 4. Speech → testimony → writing

These are distinct stages and must not be collapsed.

### 5. Mode → category

Audible speech is a mode of divine communication. Revelation is the broader category.

### 6. No premature compression

This chapter does not attempt to establish the full doctrines of:

- revelation;
- inspiration;
- canon;
- manuscript transmission;
- textual criticism;
- or final authentication.

Those questions have designated places later in the TOC.

# Final Doctrinal Statement

> **The biblical record presents God as speaking directly to human beings through an identifiable voice. This occurs in foundational covenant history at Sinai and in the New Testament testimony concerning Christ. Divine speech therefore constitutes a genuine mode of divine communication through which God's words, commands, declarations, and purposes can be communicated to human recipients and subsequently preserved in historical testimony and written record.**

> **The voice comes first in this chapter because communication precedes the written record. The next task is to examine the other divinely appointed means through which that communication was mediated and transmitted.**
