# I. FINAL TABLE OF CONTENTS

## MOVEMENT I — TRUTH AND THE POSSIBILITY OF KNOWLEDGE

### 1. What Is Truth?
Establish truth as the first question rather than beginning with an institutional claim. Define truth, actuality, fact, and correspondence to reality, and establish the standard against which later claims are evaluated.

### 2. Truth and Reality
Examine the relationship between truth and what actually is. Distinguish reality from assertion, perception, opinion, interpretation, and convention.

### 3. Truth and Error
Establish how truth is distinguished from error and why sincerity, popularity, tradition, institutional status, or repetition cannot by themselves establish truth.

### 4. Knowledge, Evidence, and Rational Warrant
Define knowledge, evidence, testimony, and warrant sufficiently for the investigation. Establish what must be present before a conclusion can rationally be treated as established.

### 5. Reason, Inference, and Ratiocination
Develop the movement from evidence to conclusion. Distinguish assertion from inference and show how valid reasoning permits conclusions to be drawn from established premises.

### 6. Dianoetic Thinking and Sound Judgment
Develop disciplined thinking through analysis, comparison, connection, distinction, inference, and judgment. The treatment should model the intellectual discipline required to investigate Scripture rather than merely describe it.

### 7. Isaiah 1:18 — “Let Us Reason Together”
Use Isaiah 1:18 as a major biblical anchor for reasonability. Show that engaging the Word requires intellectual engagement and sound judgment rather than passive repetition.

### 8. The Problem of Ultimate Authority
Ask what can finally govern judgment when competing claims exist. Establish why an ultimate authority cannot simply be asserted but must itself be grounded and authenticated.

---

## MOVEMENT II — GOD, TRUTH, AND REVELATION

### 9. What Grounds Truth?
Move from the existence of truth to the question of its ultimate ground. Examine whether truth is merely an abstract human convention or has an objective foundation.

### 10. God as the Ground of Truth
Examine the biblical presentation of God in relation to truth, reality, faithfulness, and the nature of what is. Establish the argument rather than merely assume the conclusion.

### 11. The Truthfulness, Faithfulness, and Immutability of God
Develop the biblical attributes relevant to the trustworthiness of divine revelation. Establish why a God who is true, faithful, immutable, and incapable of falsehood can be the ground of trustworthy revelation.

### 12. God Cannot Lie
Examine the biblical claims concerning God's inability to lie and their epistemological significance. The question is not merely whether God communicates true propositions, but whether falsehood is incompatible with His nature.

### 13. Knowing God: Revelation Rather Than Speculation
Distinguish knowledge supplied by revelation from human speculation about God. The Bible's own account must govern the investigation rather than later philosophical or theological constructions.

### 14. The Problem With Human Accounts About God
Examine the difference between what Scripture establishes and what commentators, philosophers, theologians, institutions, or traditions say about Scripture or God. External claims must themselves be tested.

---

## MOVEMENT III — WHAT IS THE BIBLE?

### 15. What Is the Bible?
Define the Bible before making sweeping claims about it. Establish its nature as the body of writings under investigation and ask what the Bible itself claims concerning its origin, contents, authority, and truth.

### 16. The Bible as Divine Library and Written Record
Examine the Bible's collection of writings and its different forms of expression. Establish the importance of understanding what kind of written object is being examined.

### 17. The Bible as Historical Testimony
Develop its historical dimension: events, persons, places, declarations, actions, and recorded encounters. Historical claims must be treated as claims capable of evidential examination.

### 18. The Bible as Eyewitness and Apostolic Testimony
Examine eyewitness testimony explicitly, including 2 Peter 1:16 and related apostolic witness. Distinguish direct testimony from conjecture or later philosophical reconstruction.

### 19. The Bible's Own Claims About Its Origin
Gather and reason through the Bible's own claims concerning divine communication, inspiration, prophetic speech, apostolic testimony, and Scripture.

### 20. What Is γραφή (*graphē*)?
Introduce the Greek term γραφή, transliterated *graphē*, Strong's G1124. Establish its lexical meaning of writing and its biblical use for Scripture/Holy Writ.

### 21. G1124 — Writing, Holy Writ, and Scripture
Develop why a writing can be designated Holy Writ and how biblical usage identifies authoritative written material. Include lexical evidence and relevant biblical usage.

### 22. Graphē, Holy Writ, Scripture, Bible, and Written Word
Clarify the relationship among these designations without flattening their distinctions. Establish the written authority being investigated.

Develop evidence as a rational basis for conclusions concerning historical claims. Establish how testimony functions as evidence.

Examine the Bible's unity across writers, periods, circumstances, and forms of expression.

### 39. Historical and External Corroboration
Examine external evidence relevant to historical claims while keeping external corroboration subordinate to the central question of what the biblical record itself claims.

### 41. The Cumulative Evidential Case
Bring independent lines of evidence together and examine cumulative convergence rather than relying on one isolated argument.

# II. MASTER KNOWLEDGE FLOW

The book must not read as a collection of interesting subjects. It must read as a cumulative investigation:

> **Truth → Reality → Knowledge → Evidence → Reason and rational warrant → Ultimate authority → God and the ground of truth → Divine revelation → What the Bible is → Graphē/Holy Writ → How revelation was given → Witness and testimony → Writing and inspiration → Authentication → Christ → Apostolic testimony → Canon → Inspiration and infallibility → Christ's treatment of Scripture → “The scripture cannot be broken” → “Thy word is truth” → Graphē as primary written authority → Human interpretation and error → Tergiversation, accepted heresies, aridity, unskillfulness → Scholarly labour, reasonability, skill, rightly dividing → Readiness for the Six First Principles.**

**No major conclusion should be introduced before the premises required for it have been established.**

---

# III. FOUR QUESTIONS THAT MUST REMAIN DISTINCT

## 1. Ontological Question
> **What is true/reality?**

This concerns what actually is.

## 2. Epistemological Question
> **How can we know what is true?**

This concerns knowledge: perception, testimony, evidence, reasoning, inference, revelation, and warranted conclusion.

## 3. Evidential Question
> **What warrants believing that something is true?**

This asks what evidence supports a conclusion and whether the inference from evidence to conclusion is rationally justified.

## 4. Hermeneutical Question
> **Once we have an authoritative truthful text, how do we correctly understand it?**

This is distinct from establishing the authority of the text. The authority of Scripture does not make every interpretation of Scripture authoritative.

---

# IV. CORE CONCEPTUAL DISTINCTIONS

The work must repeatedly distinguish:

- **Truth ≠ belief**
- **Truth ≠ popularity**
- **Truth ≠ institutional acceptance**
- **Truth ≠ repetition**
- **Scriptural authority ≠ interpretive infallibility of the reader**
- **Evidence ≠ authority**
- **Quotation ≠ ignorance**
- **Quotation ≠ complete understanding**
- **Understanding ≠ authority over the text**
- **Biblical revelation ≠ human commentary about biblical revelation**
- **God's Word ≠ every human claim made about God's Word**

---

# V. THE MASTER REASONING DISCIPLINE

Major claims should, where appropriate, be processed through:

> **Claim → Definition → Evidence → Analysis → Inference → Conclusion → Consequence**

For each significant conclusion, ask:

- What premise supports this conclusion?
- What evidence supports that premise?
- Is the inference valid?
- Is the conclusion stronger than the evidence permits?
- Has an external assumption been introduced?
- Has a biblical claim been distinguished from a human claim about the Bible?

---

# VI. SOURCE AND BIBLICAL DISCIPLINE

The underlying source material must be **studied, not merely cited**.

Do not repeatedly write “the source says.” Instead:

- identify the proposition;
- understand its reasoning;
- preserve its terminology where appropriate;
- integrate its evidence;
- compare it with Scripture;
- expose its implications;
- identify what follows logically;
- identify what does not follow;
- write the resulting argument directly.

When the source does not support a point, do not silently manufacture support.

Biblical passages must likewise be treated as arguments and evidence in context, not as decorative quotations.

---

# VII. BIBLE-FIRST METHODOLOGY

Do not use “the Bible” as an undefined authority while simultaneously attempting to establish why the Bible is authoritative.

First establish:

- what the Bible is;
- what its writings are;
- what kind of record it constitutes;
- what it claims concerning its origin;
- how revelation was given;
- how witnesses function;
- what inspiration means;
- what constitutes Scripture;
- how the canonical corpus is recognized;
- what evidence authenticates the record.

Only then should the conclusion concerning primary written authority be fully drawn.

---

# VIII. GOD MUST NOT BE REDUCED TO “A SUBJECT”

Avoid formulations that make God sound merely like an object about whom a book happens to speak.

The architecture concerns:

> **God, truth, revelation, and the written record.**

God is presented in the biblical account as Creator, revealer, speaker, truthful, faithful, immutable, and incapable of lying.

The investigation therefore asks:

> **What does the biblical record establish concerning God, and why is that record itself trustworthy?**

Do not substitute theology, philosophy, commentary, or institutional claims for the biblical revelation itself.

---

# IX. GRAPHĒ / HOLY WRIT GUIDANCE

Establish precisely:

**Greek:** γραφή  
**Transliteration:** *graphē*  
**Strong's:** G1124  
**Basic lexical idea:** writing.

Then establish its biblical usage in relation to:

- Scripture;
- Holy Writ;
- the written Word.

The reader must understand why a writing is called Holy Writ and why Scripture itself assigns decisive authority to what is written.

---

# X. JOHN 10:34–36 GUIDANCE

John 10 must be an argument, not a proof-text display.

1. Give the context.
2. Give the actual quotation.
3. Identify Psalm 82:6.
4. Give the relevant Psalm text.
5. Follow Christ's reasoning.
6. Identify His appeal to what is written.
7. Explain “the scripture cannot be broken.”
8. Ask **why** it cannot be broken.
9. Connect the answer to the established doctrine of truth and divine revelation.
10. Connect it with John 17:17: **“thy word is truth.”**

The conclusion must be earned through the preceding argument.

# XII. REVELATION, INSPIRATION, CANON, AND INFALLIBILITY

Keep these concepts distinct:

**Revelation:** what God makes known.

**Communication:** how revelation is conveyed.

**Witness:** those who receive, see, hear, or encounter.

**Testimony:** what witnesses report.

**Writing:** the committed written record.

**Inspiration:** the divine operation concerning Scripture.

**Canon:** the recognized authoritative scriptural corpus.

**Transmission:** historical preservation and copying.

**Authentication:** grounds for recognizing the record as genuine and authoritative.

**Infallibility:** the conclusion concerning the truthfulness/reliability of the inspired record.

---

# XIII. CHRIST'S SPECIAL PLACE

Christ is not merely one evidence category among miracles, prophecy, archaeology, and manuscripts.

The argument should progressively establish:

- Christ's historical place;
- His claims;
- His works;
- His relationship to truth;
- His treatment of Scripture;
- His use of Scripture;
- His reasoning from Scripture;
- His confirmation of earlier Scripture;
- His relationship to the apostles;
- the apostles' eyewitness testimony.

This makes Christ a central authentication and interpretive axis.

# XVI. WHAT THE BOOK IS NOT

It must not become:

- a philosophical apologetics book;
- a theology textbook;
- a Bible commentary;
- a history of the Bible;
- a collection of proof texts.

It may engage philosophy, theology, history, scholarship, and commentary where useful, but none may replace the biblical record or become an unquestioned primary authority.

---

# XVII. WHAT THE BOOK IS

> **A rigorous investigation of truth, its ultimate ground, divine revelation, the nature and authentication of the Holy Writ/Graphē, its authority and infallibility, and the intellectual discipline required to know and rightly handle it.**

Its governing movement is:

> **Truth → Ground → Revelation → Scripture → Authentication → Christ → Graphē → Authority → Interpretation → Skill → First Principles**

---

# XVIII. AUTHORING QUALITY CONTROL

Every chapter must pass five tests:

### 1. Substance
Does it actually teach something?

### 2. Dependency
Has everything required to understand it already been established?

### 3. Reasoning
Does it demonstrate why its conclusion follows?

### 4. Biblical grounding
Are biblical claims established against Scripture rather than merely attributed to a source?

### 5. Forward movement
Does it answer the question created by the preceding argument and prepare the next question?

If not, revise before proceeding.

---

# XIX. FINAL MANUSCRIPT AUDIT

Before publication, audit for:

- omitted source arguments;
- duplicated arguments;
- premature conclusions;
- circular reasoning;
- undefined terminology;
- unsupported assertions;
- misplaced topics;
- weak transitions;
- biblical context;
- lexical accuracy;
- historical claims;
- evidential claims;
- philosophical assumptions;
- theological assumptions;
- conflation of Scripture with interpretation;
- reduction of truth to belief;
- reduction of knowledge to quotation;
- reduction of Scripture to proof texts;
- failure to answer questions raised by the text;
- failure to distinguish God's Word from human commentary about God's Word.

The manuscript is not complete because it contains many facts. It is complete when the reader can follow the argument and see **why each major conclusion follows**.
