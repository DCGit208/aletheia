## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 43 — Jesus Christ in the Historical Record

> **Movement VI — Christ as the Central Witness**

> **Central objective:** Establish the historical question concerning Jesus Christ before developing theological implications. Show, with full force and by ordinary historical method, that Jesus of Nazareth is a real historical figure of first-century Judea and Galilee under Roman administration, attested by early written testimony and by independent non-biblical notices, and that this historical baseline is the necessary opening of Movement VI.

## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 42 closed Movement V with a warrant verdict. The investigator is rationally warranted in treating the Graphē as authentic written testimony of the claims under investigation: a genuine, recoverable, historically embedded, internally coherent record of claimed divine communication and historical acts, carried by positioned witnesses and marked by signs and predictive correspondences belonging to that testimony. That warrant is a right of rational acceptance at a stated level. It is not canon, not infallibility, and not primary written authority in the later sense of the book.

Chapter 42 ended by naming the next necessary question, not a new curiosity:

> **Who is Jesus Christ in the historical record, before theological implications are developed from His person, and how does that historical question open the investigation of Him as the central witness of the authenticated Graphē?**

That is this chapter’s assignment.

The locked Table of Contents states the work in one sentence:

> **Establish the historical question concerning Jesus Christ before developing theological implications.**

The chapter does not re-prove Movement V. It cites that movement as established. It does not consummate resurrection apologetics. It does not develop John 14:6 as a full doctrine of truth and revelation. It does not try the claims-and-works authentication axis as a finished case. It does not treat apostolic authority, Christ’s confirmation of Scripture, canon, or infallibility. It performs the historical opening without which those later investigations would be investigations of an unlocated name.

Christ is not merely one evidence class among archaeology and manuscripts. Movement V already used His concentration in the later writings as a coherence-claim, and already used named public officials of His setting as external traces. Those uses were authenticating the written record. This chapter asks a prior and distinct question about the Person toward whom that record is aimed:

> **Did this Jesus of Nazareth exist as a public historical figure, and what does the surviving record actually establish concerning His place in first-century history?**

The remaining questions of Movement VI follow by sequence. Sequence continues the investigation. It does not convert the present historical conclusion into hesitation.

## Central Objective

> **To define the historical question concerning Jesus Christ; to distinguish historical existence and public ministry from later doctrinal development and from legendary accretion; to examine the Gospels, Acts, and Pauline letters as sources for the historical person; to examine early non-biblical notices for what each actually establishes; to locate Jesus within Judea and Galilee under Roman administration and within Second Temple Judaism and its Scriptures; and to state, with full force, that Jesus of Nazareth is a real historical figure within ordinary historical method.**

The governing movement is:

> **Warranted written testimony → historical question concerning its central figure → definition and distinctions → biblical writings as historical sources → chronology and setting → external notices → competing accounts → inspectable inference → historical baseline → sequence to truth and revelation.**

The chapter’s conclusion is affirmative:

> **Jesus of Nazareth is a real historical figure of the early first century: a Jewish man of Galilee and Judea, publicly active in the closing years of the Second Temple, associated with John the Baptist, gathered as a teacher with disciples, brought into conflict with portions of the Jewish leadership, and executed by crucifixion under Pontius Pilate in the reign of Tiberius. That conclusion is warranted by ordinary historical method applied to early written testimony and independent notices. It is not the product of later ecclesiastical decree. It is not a myth invented centuries after the events. It is not yet the full Christology of truth, the authentication of His claims and works, or the evidential case for His resurrection.**

The remaining questions follow by sequence. The historical baseline is already in force.

# 43.1 The Question Created by the Preceding Chapter

Movement V authenticated a written testimony. It did not yet investigate the Person in whom that testimony concentrates.

Chapter 38 established Christological concentration as a coherence-claim of the later writings. Chapter 26 examined Christ as the decisive mode of divine communication in the giving of the record: God, who spoke formerly by the prophets, has in these last days spoken by His Son (Hebrews 1:1–2). Revelation 1:5 names Jesus Christ “the faithful witness.” John 5 says the writings testify of Him.

Those identifications are in the record. The record is now warranted as authentic written testimony. The next operation is not to quote those identifications as if they were already the finished doctrine of His person. It is to ask the historical question that any such identification presupposes:

> **Is the figure named Jesus Christ a figure of history, or a later literary construction?**

If He were only a later construction, Movement VI would be an investigation of a symbol. If He is a figure of history, Movement VI is an investigation of a Person who acted, spoke, suffered, and was proclaimed within a recoverable public world.

The question is therefore not:

> **Has a religious tradition used the name Jesus?**

That question is trivial. Traditions use names.

The question is:

> **Does ordinary historical method warrant concluding that Jesus of Nazareth lived, taught, and died as a public figure in first-century Judea and Galilee under Roman administration?**

That is a different operation from piety. It is the operation with which Movement VI must begin.

# 43.3 Define the Historical Question

A **historical question** is a question about what occurred, who existed, and what can be known of persons and events in a recoverable past.

The historical question concerning Jesus Christ is not first a question about the metaphysical constitution of His person. It is not first a question about the later vocabulary of councils. It is not first a question about the religious use of His name by later institutions. It is a question of this form:

> **Did a particular Jewish man, known as Jesus, associated with Nazareth, live and act publicly in Galilee and Judea in the early first century, and was He executed under Pontius Pilate?**

Several elements belong to the question as a historical question.

First, **particularity**. The claim is not that “a savior-figure” exists in religious imagination. The claim is that a named person occupied a named land in a named political period.

Second, **publicity**. The Gospels and Acts do not present a private vision granted to one isolated recipient. They present teaching, controversy, crowds, opponents, a trial, and an execution. Public events are, in principle, historically examinable in a way that a purely inward event is not.

Third, **temporality**. The figure is placed in the reign of Tiberius, in the prefecture of Pilate, in the days of Herod Antipas, in the high-priesthood associated with Caiaphas, and in a Temple still standing. Those are dated coordinates.

Fourth, **examinability**. A historical question admits evidence: writings close to the events, independent notices, material traces of the setting, and the testing of rival explanations.

Thus:

> **The historical question concerning Jesus Christ is the question of His existence and public career as a figure of first-century history, asked under the same kind of method by which other ancient persons are known.**

---

# 43.4 Distinguish Four Objects That Must Not Be Collapsed

The investigation fails at the first step if it treats four different objects as one.

## 43.4.1 Historical existence and public ministry

This is the object of the present chapter. It includes: a real person; a Jewish setting; Galilee and Judea; association with John the Baptist; public teaching; disciples; conflict; and execution under Pilate. These are historical claims about a career in the world.

## 43.4.2 Later doctrinal development

Later centuries formulated, in technical vocabulary, what the churches confessed concerning the Person already proclaimed in the first-century writings. Councils, creeds, and theological systems are historical facts of later reception. They are not the same object as the first-century figure. A later formula may be faithful to earlier testimony, or it may distort it. Either way, the formula is not the existence of the man. This chapter does not treat Nicaea, Chalcedon, or later systematic Christology as premises of His historicity. Sequence forbids that collapse.

## 43.4.3 Legendary accretion

Later writings, some of them pious and some of them fantastic, attached stories to the name of Jesus that the first-century Gospels do not contain. Infancy romances, fabricated dialogues, and later apocryphal narratives are a real literary class. Their existence is not evidence that the earlier, more restrained, publicly located narratives are of the same class. Accretion is a historical process that can occur around a real person. It is not identical with the invention of the person.

## 43.4.4 The full theological and evidential case of Movement VI

That Jesus is the truth; that His works authenticate His claims; that He rose from the dead; that eyewitnesses of His majesty are decisive; that He confirms Scripture; that apostolic testimony carries His authority — these are the assignments of Chapters 44–50. They presuppose a historical figure. They are not established by proving that the figure existed.

The governing distinction is therefore:

> **Existence and public ministry ≠ later doctrinal formulation ≠ legendary accretion ≠ the remaining Christological case of this movement.**

The present chapter establishes the first. It names the others in order not to treat them as already finished.

# 43.5 Ordinary Historical Method

The method required here is ordinary historical method, not a special religious method invented for this Person, and not a Euclidean method invented to make ancient history impossible.

Ordinary historical method, as already developed in Chapters 4, 5, 17, 18, 34, and 35, includes at least the following operations.

**Identify the claim.** What proposition is being examined? Here: that Jesus of Nazareth existed and acted publicly as described in the relevant respects.

**Identify the sources.** What writings or traces bear upon that proposition? Here: the Pauline letters; the Gospels and Acts; independent Jewish and Roman notices; and the already established material setting of the period.

**Classify the sources.** Early or late; positioned or remote; friendly, hostile, or indifferent; independent or derivative; narrative, epistolary, or incidental.

**Ask what each source is in a position to know.** A letter written within a generation of the events by a man who knew the Lord’s brother is a different kind of source from a romance written two centuries later.

**Ask what would be expected if the claim were true, and what would be expected if it were false.** A real public figure executed under a named prefect leaves a different evidential residue from a myth coined in the third or fourth century.

**Test rivals.** Myth invented centuries later; purely legendary construction without a historical core; reduction of the figure to a later doctrinal cipher.

**Draw a conclusion proportional to the grounds.** Do not upgrade existence into resurrection. Do not downgrade a well-attested public figure into a maybe.

Ancient history rarely possesses the autograph diary of its subjects. It possesses writings, inscriptions, later historians, and material context. Tiberius, Pilate, Caiaphas, John the Baptist, and Herod Antipas are known by such means. Jesus of Nazareth is to be examined by the same means. A demand that His existence be proved by a surviving Roman court transcript, or by an artifact inscribed with His signature, is not historical rigor. It is a unique veto applied to one figure.

Chapter 34 already established that historical reasoning can produce rational warrant without mathematical deduction. That conclusion is in force. This chapter uses it.

Using “Jesus is God, therefore He existed” would be circular and is not the argument.

# 43.7 What Would Count as a Myth Invented Centuries Later

Clarity about the rival is part of the definition of the question.

A **myth invented centuries later** would be a figure who first appears in writings long after the alleged lifetime, without earlier positioned testimony, without attachment to named public officials of a recoverable administration, without a community that can be located in the intervening decades, and without hostile notice of a founder executed under a named prefect.

If Jesus of Nazareth were such a figure, the investigator would expect:

- silence in the first century;
- a first appearance of the story in the third or fourth century, or at earliest in a period too late for living memory;
- mobility of setting — an unlocated savior, or a savior whose geography and politics are theatrical;
- absence of costly, checkable particulars;
- absence of family names that later embarrassment would rather omit;
- absence of a crucified founder in hostile Roman memory.

That is the profile. The profile will be compared with the evidence. Comparison is not a hedge. It is the test by which the rival is to be failed if it fails.

A weaker rival must also be named now, so that it is not confused with the first:

> **Legendary accretion around a real person is not the same as the invention of the person.**

The first rival, if it fails, leaves the second to be examined in its proper measure. The second, even if partly true of later literature, does not cancel a first-century figure.

# 43.8 The Biblical Writings as Historical Sources

The Gospels, Acts, and Pauline letters are biblical writings. In this chapter they are examined as **sources for the historical person**, not yet as the Graphē in the later sense of primary written authority.

That examination is legitimate for two independent reasons.

First, they are ancient writings. Any ancient writing that makes particular historical claims is a historical source until shown to be otherwise. The investigator does not need a completed doctrine of inspiration in order to read a first-century letter as a first-century letter.

Second, Movement V has already warranted them as authentic written testimony of the claims they make, at the level of genuineness, recoverability, embeddedness, and testimonial character. That warrant does not yet mean that every sentence has been independently corroborated. It means the investigator is entitled to treat them as testimony rather than as folklore, and to examine their historical claims as historical claims.

The supplied research material insisted that statements of fact and historical accounts are to be taken as historical accounts, and that the four Gospels are, in form, history of the Messiah. That insistence is here given a disciplined form. Genre is relevant. A writing that names rulers, places, families, itineraries, disputes, and an execution is not, by that form, a timeless myth. Form does not by itself prove every detail. It does establish the proper category of inquiry.

Thus:

> **The biblical writings concerning Jesus are historical sources for a historical person. They are to be read as such. They are not to be silently reclassified as myth because they also make theological claims.**

Writings may be both testimony concerning events and confession concerning the meaning of those events. The two functions must be distinguished. They must not be used to cancel each other. A source that says “this happened, and this is what it means” is still a source for the happening.

# 43.9 The Pauline Letters as the Earliest Written Christian Documents

The earliest surviving Christian writings are not the four Gospels. They are letters of Paul.

The undisputed letters — including Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon — belong to the middle of the first century, within roughly two to three decades of the crucifixion. 1 Thessalonians is commonly placed near the beginning of that window; Romans and the Corinthian correspondence occupy the 50s. Exact year-dates of individual letters may be argued. The window is not in serious doubt. These are not fourth-century documents. They are not even late-second-century documents.

That chronological fact has immediate historical force.

A writer in the 50s cannot be inventing a figure who first existed as literature in the 300s. He can be mistaken. He can be partisan. He cannot occupy a century that has not yet arrived.

Paul is not a neutral Roman annalist. He is a former persecutor of the assembly who became its most vigorous advocate. Partisanship is a fact about him. It is not a solvent of his chronological position, nor of his named contacts with persons who had known Jesus in the flesh.

The letters are therefore the first written stratum of the Christian testimony, and they already presuppose a recent historical figure, not a remote myth.

---

# 43.10 What Paul Knows of the Historical Person

Paul does not write a Gospel. He writes occasional letters to assemblies. The historical person appears in them not as a full biography but as a set of costly, particular claims that a myth of the later centuries would not need and that a contemporary community could check.

He knows that Jesus was a man of Israel, “made of the seed of David according to the flesh” (Romans 1:3), “made of a woman, made under the law” (Galatians 4:4). These are not the predicates of a timeless aeon. They are predicates of birth, lineage, and Torah-observant Jewish life.

He knows that Jesus had brothers, and that one of them, James, was a leading figure in the Jerusalem assembly. “Other of the apostles saw I none, save James the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19). He distinguishes this James from the rest. He also refers to “the brethren of the Lord” as a known class (1 Corinthians 9:5). A mythical construct does not ordinarily acquire a named brother whom the writer has met.

He knows a night of betrayal and a supper: “I have received of the Lord that which also I delivered unto you, That the Lord Jesus the same night in which he was betrayed took bread” (1 Corinthians 11:23). Betrayal is a particular. A night is a particular. Bread and a cup as instituted memory are particulars.

He knows a death by crucifixion as the public scandal of the message: “we preach Christ crucified” (1 Corinthians 1:23); “Jesus Christ, and him crucified” (1 Corinthians 2:2). Crucifixion is a Roman penalty, public and shameful. It is not the death a later myth-maker would most naturally invent for a glorious heavenly being, unless the death were already a stubborn historical given.

He knows that this death occurred in recent memory relative to his own career. He persecuted the assembly that already existed because of this Jesus (Galatians 1:13, 23; 1 Corinthians 15:9). His own conversion is not dated to a remote age. It is dated to the same generation.

He knows Cephas and John as “pillars” in Jerusalem, and he knows that his gospel was compared with theirs (Galatians 2:1–10). The Jerusalem assembly is not a literary device. It is a body of persons he visited, disputed with, and in some matters agreed with.

These are historical claims inside the earliest Christian documents. They do not yet prove the resurrection. They do prove that the writer is talking about a recent Jewish man, born, related, betrayed, crucified, and remembered by named persons whom the writer knew.

---

# 43.11 The Received Tradition of 1 Corinthians 15:3–8

1 Corinthians 15:3–8 must be handled as a source, not as a slogan.

Paul writes that he delivered “first of all that which I also received”: that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; that He was buried; that He rose again the third day according to the scriptures; that He was seen of Cephas, then of the twelve; that He was seen of above five hundred brethren at once, of whom the greater part remain unto this present, but some are fallen asleep; that He was seen of James, then of all the apostles; last of all, of Paul himself.

Several features of this passage are historically relevant before any resurrection apologetic is attempted.

First, **reception language**. Paul distinguishes what he received from what he now delivers. The wording is the wording of tradition, not of a private invention of the 50s.

Second, **earliness**. Paul writes to Corinth in the 50s. He had already delivered this tradition when he founded the assembly, still earlier. He himself had received it earlier still, in the period after his conversion, in contact with the Jerusalem figures named in Galatians 1–2. The tradition therefore belongs to the first years of the movement, not to a later legendary century.

Third, **named persons**. Cephas, the twelve, James, the apostles, and Paul are not anonymous mythic types. They are the same names that appear as living leaders in the letters.

Fourth, **checkability**. “The greater part remain unto this present” is an invitation to inquiry, not a sealed myth. Living witnesses are a different evidential class from departed ancestors.

Fifth, **the death-and-burial sequence**. Whatever Chapter 46 will make of the resurrection clause, the tradition already treats death and burial as prior public facts. Burial is not a mythic flourish. It is a claim that a body was disposed of in the ordinary world.

This chapter does not convert 1 Corinthians 15 into a completed proof that God raised Jesus from the dead. It converts the passage into what it is at this stage: early, named, checkable testimony that the movement, from its first years, was about a particular man who had died, been buried, and was proclaimed as seen alive by persons still known.

The distinction is mandatory:

> **Early tradition that a man died and was buried is historical evidence that the man existed and died. Early tradition that He was seen alive is a further claim, reserved for its chapter.**

# 43.12 The Gospels as Sources for the Historical Person

The four Gospels are writings that present themselves as accounts of Jesus’ public career, teaching, conflict, death, and — in their closing movements — resurrection appearances. In the architecture of the biblical library they are, as the research material rightly classified them, history of the Messiah: not novels, not timeless oracles detached from land and year, not later ecclesiastical minutes.

Matthew opens with a genealogy that places Jesus in the line of Abraham and David, and with a birth in the days of Herod the king. Mark opens with the preaching of John, the baptism of Jesus, a public ministry in Galilee, and a rapid movement toward Jerusalem and death. Luke opens with a historiographical preface and a synchronism of rulers. John opens with the Word, then immediately locates the Word’s historical manifestation in a ministry of named places, named feasts, named opponents, and a named Roman prefect.

These are different writings. They have different selections, different immediate purposes, and different emphases. Chapter 38 already treated overlapping testimony with distinct selection as a coherence-fact, not as a scandal. The present point is narrower:

> **All four locate a particular man in a particular land under particular authorities, and all four make His public death under Pilate a central event.**

A source that is also a confession is still a source. The investigator who discards the Gospels because they believe what they narrate would, by the same rule, discard most of ancient biography. Partisanship is to be weighed. It is not a license for non-existence.

Luke 1:1–4 is especially important as a statement of method inside the corpus. Luke distinguishes many earlier attempts, eyewitnesses and ministers of the word, his own careful following of things from the beginning, and an orderly account written so that Theophilus may know the certainty of what he has been taught. That is the language of historical investigation, not of myth-making. It does not by itself prove that Luke succeeded. It does prove that Luke presents his work as success of that kind, and that the community already possessed a body of testimony he could examine.

John 21:24 and John 19:35 likewise present the writing as testimony of one who saw. 1 John 1:1–3, already treated as witness-language in Chapter 18, joins hearing, seeing, beholding, and handling with proclamation. These texts are claims of proximity. Chapter 47 will examine them as the eyewitness file. This chapter requires only that they be recognized as first-century claims of proximity to a historical person, not as later metaphysical poetry about an idea.

---

# 43.13 Acts as Continuation into Public History

Acts continues the story into the public life of the assemblies in Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, and the cities of the eastern empire.

Two features of Acts are historically relevant here.

First, the opening speeches in Jerusalem treat Jesus of Nazareth as a man whose works were public knowledge. Peter says:

> **“Ye men of Israel, hear these words; Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved of God among you by miracles and wonders and signs, which God did by him in the midst of you, as ye yourselves also know.”**  
> — Acts 2:22

The appeal is not to a secret. It is to what the hearers themselves know of a recent public career. Whether every hearer granted the interpretation “approved of God” is a different question from whether a public career was being alleged in the city where it was said to have occurred. A myth coined far away and long after does not begin by appealing to the local memory of the audience.

Second, Acts repeatedly names the same political world already independently attested: Herod, Pilate, Caiaphas, Gallio, named civic titles, synagogues, the Temple, and Roman custody. Chapter 39 has already shown that this civic texture meets the stones. The present chapter uses that meeting as a premise of setting, not as a second trial of Gallio.

Paul, in Acts 26:26, tells Agrippa that the king knows of these things, “for this thing was not done in a corner.” The sentence is a claim of publicity. It belongs to the historical file because it is the opposite of the claim a mystery-cult myth would most naturally make.

Acts is not, in this chapter, a completed corroboration of every miracle in the Gospel narratives. It is evidence that the movement, from its first urban proclamations, presented Jesus as a recent public figure of Israel, executed under known authorities, and not as a private allegory.

# 43.14 Names, Places, Families, and Institutions

Historical particularity is not decoration. It is the difference between a figure who can be sought in a land and a figure who cannot.

The writings name **Nazareth**, **Capernaum**, **Bethsaida**, **Chorazin**, **the Sea of Galilee**, **Caesarea Philippi**, **Jericho**, **Bethany**, **Bethlehem**, **Jerusalem**, **the Temple**, **the Mount of Olives**, **Gethsemane**, **Golgotha**. These are not interchangeable story-places. Nazareth was an obscure Galilean village; its very obscurity is a poor invention if the aim were grandeur. “Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth?” (John 1:46) is the opposite of mythic embellishment of origin.

They name **persons**: Mary, Joseph, John the Baptist, Simon Peter, Andrew, James, John, Philip, Thomas, Judas Iscariot, Mary Magdalene, Martha, Lazarus, Caiaphas, Annas, Herod Antipas, Herodias, Pontius Pilate, Barabbas. Some of these names are independently recoverable in the political file. Others are the ordinary names of a Jewish village world. A late myth tends toward types. These writings tend toward names.

They name **families**: the brothers of Jesus — James, Joses, Simon, Judas (Mark 6:3) — and sisters left unnamed in the summary. They name a mother who remains in the story after the public ministry has begun. They record unbelief among His own (John 7:5). Unbelief in the family is not the sort of detail a later encomiast is eager to create.

They name **institutions**: synagogue, Temple, Sanhedrin, priesthood, Pharisees, Sadducees, scribes, Roman prefecture, crucifixion as a Roman penalty, the Passover as a calendar marker. These are the institutions of Second Temple Judaism under Rome. They are independently known. The narrative is at home in them.

Chapter 17 already established that a dense network of historical relationships is more than a list of names. The present application is:

> **The Jesus of the Gospels is a figure inside a known social, geographic, and political network, not a floating savior-name.**

# 43.15 Chronology: The Window of His Life

Exact day-dates of birth and death are not required for historical existence. A window is.

Several coordinates fix the window.

**Herod the Great.** Matthew places Jesus’ birth in the days of Herod the king, and the subsequent danger under that king. Herod’s death is independently placed in 4 BC. The birth therefore falls shortly before that death, in the closing years of Herod’s reign. The common era’s numbering of years is a later convention and is slightly offset from this window. The offset is a fact of calendar history, not a confusion in the sources.

**The census notice in Luke 2.** Luke associates the birth with a decree of Caesar Augustus and a taxing when Cyrenius (Quirinius) was governor of Syria. Quirinius’ well-attested census of Judea as a Roman province belongs to AD 6, after Archelaus’ removal. The relation between Luke’s notice and that later census is a local chronological question of long standing. Proposed reconciliations exist; none is required here as a premise. What is not warranted is the leap from a disputed synchronism of the birth-narrative to the non-existence of the man whose public career is independently placed decades later under Pilate. Local chronological friction is not a global rival.

**The fifteenth year of Tiberius.** Luke 3:1–2 anchors the beginning of John the Baptist’s ministry in a multiple synchronism: the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar; Pontius Pilate governor of Judaea; Herod tetrarch of Galilee; Philip tetrarch of Ituraea and Trachonitis; Lysanias tetrarch of Abilene; Annas and Caiaphas the high priests. The fifteenth year of Tiberius, counted from his accession in AD 14, falls in AD 28–29; if counted from an earlier association with Augustus, a year or two earlier is possible. Either way, John’s public appearance, and therefore Jesus’ baptism and the opening of His ministry, belong to the late 20s of the first century.

**Pilate’s prefecture.** Independently, Pontius Pilate was prefect of Judea from AD 26 to 36. The passion narratives place the execution inside that prefecture. Tacitus, treated below, independently places the execution of Christus under Pilate in the reign of Tiberius. The death therefore falls between AD 26 and 36, and more narrowly in the early 30s by the combined Gospel and Pauline chronology. The two years most often argued are AD 30 and AD 33. The chapter does not need to decide between them. Both are inside the prefecture. Both are inside living memory of the letter-writers of the 50s.

**The Temple still standing.** The public ministry is set while the Second Temple stands. The Temple was destroyed in AD 70. The narrative world is pre-70 in its institutions, calendar, and priestly conflict. That is the world of Jesus’ career.

Thus:

> **Jesus of Nazareth belongs to the reign of Tiberius, the prefecture of Pilate, the tetrarchy of Antipas, and the last generation of the Second Temple. The window is the early first century. It is not the third. It is not the fourth.**

---

# 43.16 Luke 3:1–2 as a Historical Synchronism

Luke 3:1–2 deserves isolation because it is the New Testament’s most explicit political dating of the opening of the public story.

A synchronism of this kind is a historiographical device. Thucydides and Polybius date by offices and years. Luke dates by emperor, prefect, tetrarchs, and high priests. The device is a claim to occupy real time.

The named offices are the right offices for the eastern Roman world of the period. Judea is under a prefect, not under a client king, which matches the settlement after Archelaus. Galilee is under a tetrarch, which matches Antipas. The high-priestly names match the house of Annas and the tenure of Caiaphas.

Chapter 39 already noted that an inscription from Abila attests a Lysanias as tetrarch, meeting Luke at a point once charged with confusion. The present chapter does not re-try that inscription. It records that Luke’s synchronism is the synchronism of a writer who knows the political map.

A myth invented centuries later, in a different civic idiom, is the writer most likely to get this map wrong. Luke gets the structure right. Right structure is not proof of every subsequent sentence. It is proof that the public career is being dated inside a real administration.

---

# 43.17 Judea and Galilee under Roman Administration

The land of Jesus is not a sacred nowhere. It is a double landscape under Rome.

**Judea**, with Samaria and Idumea, had been the ethnarchy of Archelaus after Herod’s death. In AD 6 it became a Roman province governed by a prefect of equestrian rank, resident at Caesarea on the sea, coming up to Jerusalem at the feasts with military force. Taxation, auxiliary troops, the *ius gladii* in capital matters, and the prefect’s right to appoint and depose high priests belong to that settlement. Pontius Pilate is one such prefect. The trial and crucifixion narratives occupy this legal world: a Jewish leadership without the unfettered right of Roman execution, a prefect who alone can authorize a cross, a public penalty designed for slaves and rebels.

**Galilee** remained under Herod Antipas, tetrarch until AD 39. Jesus’ northern ministry is therefore not under direct prefectural government. It is under a client ruler who had executed John, who feared the rumor of Jesus as John risen, and who later appears in the passion account in Luke 23. The distinction between Galilee and Judea is a political distinction, not a narrative convenience.

**The wider east** is the world of Philip the tetrarch, of the Decapolis, of Syria as the neighboring consular province, and of the emperor in Rome. Luke’s census-language, however it is related to Quirinius, is language of that imperial world. So is the later appeal to Caesar in Acts.

Rome is therefore not a later colorist’s addition. It is the condition under which a Galilean teacher could be crucified at Jerusalem during a feast.

The supplied research material noted that the common Greek of the empire was the commercial language of the period and the language of the New Testament writings. That observation belongs here as setting, not as a linguistic lecture. Jesus Himself is presented as speaking the Aramaic of the land — *Talitha cumi*, *Ephphatha*, *Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani* — while the writings that report Him do so in the Greek in which a Mediterranean testimony could travel. Bilingual reality is the reality of the eastern provinces. It is not an anachronism.

---

# 43.18 Second Temple Judaism as the Immediate World

Jesus is a figure of **Second Temple Judaism**, not of later Gentile religion first and Jewish memory afterwards.

The Temple stands. Sacrifice is offered. Pilgrimage feasts — Passover, Pentecost, Tabernacles — structure the year. Priests and Levites serve. The high priest is a political as well as a cultic figure under Rome. The Sanhedrin is a real council. Synagogues are the local sites of reading and dispute. Pharisees, Sadducees, and scribes are named parties and professions of that Judaism, independently known from Josephus and from the later rabbinic memory of the period. Essene and related groups belong to the same century, even where the Gospels do not name them.

Jesus teaches in synagogues. He reads from Isaiah in Nazareth (Luke 4:16–21). He disputes about Sabbath, purity, divorce, and the greatest commandment. He is addressed as rabbi. He is asked for a sign. He is accused in categories available to that Judaism: blasphemy, leading the people astray, threat to the Temple. These are intra-Jewish conflicts. They are unintelligible as the first layer of a Gentile mystery-myth.

He is circumcised on the eighth day (Luke 2:21). He is presented at the Temple. He attends the feasts. His first audiences are Israelites. The earliest assembly in Acts is an assembly in Jerusalem, at the Temple hours, among Jews and proselytes.

This setting is independently recoverable. Josephus describes the same Temple, the same parties, the same Roman pressure, the same high-priestly families, the same feasts, and the same fragility of public order. The Dead Sea Scrolls, whatever their precise communal identity, exhibit a Judaism of Scripture, purity, eschatological hope, and intense dispute in the same centuries. The world is not invented by the evangelists.

Thus:

> **The historical Jesus is unintelligible apart from the Judaism of the Second Temple. He is a figure inside that Judaism, not a later foreign myth draped in Jewish costume.**

---

# 43.19 Relation to Jewish Scripture

The same world is a world of **written Scripture**.

By the time of Jesus, the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings function as public holy writings in Israel. Jesus is presented as hearing them, reading them, citing them, and arguing from what is written. Matthew 4, Mark 12, Luke 4, and John 10 are later to be examined for His treatment of the Graphē. This chapter requires a narrower conclusion:

> **The historical figure is located inside a people whose life is already ordered by a received body of writings.**

He is not the founder of literacy in Israel. He is not the first to treat Moses as written. He enters a story already shaped by promise, law, prophecy, and hope. The genealogies that open Matthew and Luke are claims to occupy that story as history, not as a fresh mythology.

The research material noted that the Old Testament canon was a settled subject in the days of Christ and the apostles, and that the Gospels present the history of the Messiah in continuity with that earlier corpus. Continuity is a historical claim of belonging. It is not yet Chapter 48’s full treatment of His confirmation of Scripture. It is the observation that a first-century Jewish teacher who argues from Moses and the Prophets is a historically ordinary kind of figure in that land, even if the claims He makes from those writings are extraordinary.

A myth invented in a later Gentile church, ignorant of synagogue argument, is the figure least likely to be at home in the texture of halakhic dispute, feast-calendar, and written citation that the Gospels actually display. The texture is Jewish before it is ecclesiastical.

---

# 43.20 Public Ministry: What the Sources Claim as Public

The ministry presented in the Gospels is public in several inspectable respects.

It is **geographic**. He moves through Galilean towns, along the lake, into the villages of Caesarea Philippi, through Samaria in John 4, into Perea and Judea, and finally into Jerusalem at the feast. Itinerary is a historical form.

It is **audible**. He teaches in synagogues on the Sabbath, from a boat, on a mountain, in the Temple courts. John 18:20 records His own claim before the high priest: “I spake openly to the world; I ever taught in the synagogue, and in the temple, whither the Jews always resort; and in secret have I said nothing.” Open speech is the opposite of a mystery whispered to one recipient.

It is **social**. He calls disciples who leave nets and a tax table. He eats with publicans. He is criticized for the company He keeps. He is followed by crowds and abandoned by many. He has supporters in a household at Bethany and opponents in the priestly council.

It is **controversial**. The conflict with scribes and Pharisees, and later with the chief priests, is not a later ecclesiastical projection of Gentile-Jewish debate onto a placid sage. It is the plot of the ministry. A teacher who never collided with anyone would be easier to invent. The Gospels invent the collision only if one has already decided that the career is fiction.

It is **bounded**. The public career, from baptism to death, occupies a short span — a few years at most. John structures it by Passovers. The Synoptics concentrate the final week. A short public life ending in execution is a historical shape. It is not the shape of a cyclic dying-and-rising year-god.

This chapter does not convert public ministry into a completed catalogue of authentic sayings and works. Chapter 45 examines claims and works as an authentication axis. The present conclusion is:

> **The sources present a public career, and a public career is the kind of object historical method is competent to locate.**

---

# 43.21 John the Baptist as an Independent Historical Marker

John the Baptist functions as a chronological and social marker of unusual value, because he is attested both in the Gospels and independently in Josephus.

The Gospels present John as a public preacher of repentance, baptizing in the Jordan, attracting crowds, confronting Herod Antipas, and baptizing Jesus. Jesus’ first public act in the Synoptic outline is to enter John’s movement and then to depart from it into His own. The relationship is not one of later ecclesiastical convenience. It is, if anything, an embarrassment to a later church that might have wished its Lord to have no predecessor.

Josephus, in *Antiquities* 18.5.2, records John as a good man who exhorted the Jews to virtue and to baptism, who attracted such crowds that Herod Antipas, fearing sedition, put him to death at Machaerus. Josephus’ account is not a transcript of the Gospels. It has its own interest — political fear rather than the Synoptic dance of Herodias. The independence is the point. A Jewish historian of the late first century knows John as a real public figure executed by Antipas.

If John is historical, the man who entered his movement in the same window occupies the same historical field. The baptism of Jesus by John is among the most firmly placed public acts of the career precisely because later theology would not have invented a superior submitting to an inferior. Criterion of embarrassment is not a magic rule. It is ordinary historical sense. People do not ordinarily create, as the opening of their hero’s public life, an act that requires explanation.

John therefore does two things for this chapter:

> **He independently dates a public religious crisis in the late 20s under Antipas, and he attaches Jesus’ opening ministry to that crisis rather than to a vacuum.**

---

# 43.22 Crucifixion under Pilate as a Public Historical Claim

The death of Jesus by crucifixion under Pontius Pilate is a historical claim of a particularly public kind.

Crucifixion is a Roman penalty. It is inflicted by the prefect, not by a village mob as its legal source. It is inflicted at Jerusalem at a feast, according to the Gospels, with a charge related to kingship (the inscription on the cross). It is inflicted in a manner meant to be seen.

Several independent streams report it.

The Pauline letters, in the 50s, treat “Christ crucified” as the already-standard message, scandalous to Jews and foolish to Greeks (1 Corinthians 1:23). The scandal is evidence. A later community inventing a Lord from nothing would not need this particular shame as its center, unless the shame were already given.

The Gospels, in four writings, make the passion the longest connected public sequence.

Acts proclaims the killing in Jerusalem to Jerusalem.

Josephus, in the James passage treated below, knows Jesus who was called Christ as a figure already identifiable, and the *Testimonium*, even in a reconstructed modest form, places a condemnation under Pilate.

Tacitus, independently and hostilely, places the execution of Christus under Pontius Pilate in the reign of Tiberius.

Chapter 39 has already established Pilate as an inscribed prefect of Judea. The execution of Jesus is therefore an event claimed to have occurred under a real official of a real province in a real emperor’s reign.

This chapter names that death as a historical claim of high public character. It does not here develop the theology of atonement. It does not here try the resurrection. It does not here reconstruct every hour of the passion. Chapter 46 is reserved for the central historical claims of passion and resurrection as a full case. What is warranted now is more limited and still decisive:

> **That a Jewish man called Jesus, known as Christ, was executed by crucifixion under Pontius Pilate is among the most secure public facts of the whole file. A figure so executed existed.**

Existence is not a meager conclusion when the rival is non-existence.

---

# 43.23 External Notices: What Independence Means

External notices are writings that are not the Graphē and are not composed as Christian confession.

Independence is not omniscience. A Roman historian writing in the early second century is independent of the evangelists as composition even if he knows of Christians as a public class. A Jewish historian writing in the 90s is independent of the New Testament as a corpus even if he has heard the name Christ. Hostility is a form of independence. Indifference is another.

Chapter 39 already classified these writers as later literary traces of a public movement and of named public officials, and reserved Movement VI’s investigation of Christ. That reservation is now due. The notices are to be examined one by one for **what each actually establishes**, not for what later apologetic catalogues have wished them to establish.

The rule is the evidence protocol:

> **Evidence of what? What proposition? How strong? What does it not establish?**

No notice will be invented. No notice will be asked to carry a weight it does not have. The conjunction of modest independent traces with early Christian testimony is the argument, not any single pagan sentence treated as a substitute Gospel.

---

# 43.24 Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44

Tacitus, a Roman senator and historian, writing in the early second century, records the fire of Rome under Nero in AD 64 and Nero’s attempt to shift the odium onto a class already hated, called Christians by the populace.

He then explains the name. Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of the Roman procurators, Pontius Pilatus. A pernicious superstition, checked for a moment, broke out again not only in Judaea, the first source of the evil, but also in Rome.

Several features of this notice must be kept distinct.

**What Tacitus is doing.** He is not writing a life of Jesus. He is explaining to Roman readers why a despised class in Rome is called Christian, and why Nero could use them as scapegoats. The explanation requires a founder, an execution, a place of origin, and a subsequent spread.

**Tone.** The tone is hostile. Christians are associated with abomination and evil. Christus is not praised. Hostility is evidentially useful. Tacitus has no interest in supplying the church with a founder if the class could be explained as a nameless riot.

**The political coordinates.** Reign of Tiberius. Execution under Pontius Pilate. Origin in Judaea. Spread to Rome by the 60s. These are the same coordinates as the Gospels and Acts. Tacitus uses “procurator,” the later common term for the provincial governor of this type; the Caesarea inscription uses the earlier title “prefect.” The terminological shift is a known feature of later Roman usage. It does not relocate the event.

**What the notice establishes.** It establishes that a distinguished Roman historian, with access to senatorial tradition and no love for the movement, treated as fact that the Christian name derived from a Christus executed under Pilate in Tiberius’ reign, and that the movement originated in Judaea and was present in Rome by Nero’s time.

**What the notice does not establish.** It does not describe the ministry. It does not confirm a single parable. It does not prove the resurrection. It does not prove deity. It does not prove that Tacitus had read Mark. It is not an eyewitness deposition. It is a hostile Roman memory of a public execution and of a movement that followed.

That is already a great deal against the claim that Jesus is a myth invented centuries later. Tacitus is not a Christian. He is not writing in the fourth century. He places the death where the first-century testimony places it.

---

# 43.25 Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.9.1 — James the Brother of Jesus

Josephus, a Jewish priest of aristocratic family, a participant in the war of AD 66–70, and a historian writing in Rome in the 90s, records in *Antiquities* 20.9.1 the high-handed action of the high priest Ananus after the death of the procurator Festus and before the arrival of Albinus.

Ananus assembled the Sanhedrin and brought before it “the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, whose name was James,” and others, and delivered them to be stoned.

This passage is the firmer of the two principal Josephan notices concerning Jesus.

**Why it is firm.** The passage is not a hymn. It is a political anecdote about a high priest, a vacancy in the Roman succession, and a judicial stoning that cost Ananus his office. Jesus is named only to identify James. The phrase “who was called Christ” is identification, not confession. Josephus does not say that Jesus was the Christ. He says that He was called Christ — the designation by which this Jesus was known, as distinct from other men named Jesus in Josephus’ crowded narrative.

**What it establishes.** By the 90s, a Jewish historian who is not a Christian can identify James, a Jerusalem figure executed around AD 62, as the brother of Jesus who was called Christ. The identification presupposes that Jesus was a known person, that “Christ” was a known designation of that person, and that the family relation was a usable public marker.

**What it does not establish.** It does not narrate the ministry. It does not describe the crucifixion in this sentence. It does not prove the resurrection. It does not make Josephus a witness of the events of AD 30. It makes him a witness of how the name and family of Jesus stood in Jewish memory a generation later, among people who had every reason not to confess Him.

Paul’s “James the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19) and Josephus’ “brother of Jesus who was called Christ” meet. The meeting is independent enough to matter. A mythical Jesus does not have a brother stoned in Jerusalem under a named high priest in a named procuratorial interval.

---

# 43.26 Josephus, the *Testimonium Flavianum*

Josephus, in *Antiquities* 18.3.3, in the context of Pilate’s prefecture, includes a paragraph concerning Jesus. The received Greek text, as it stands in the extant manuscripts, includes clauses that a non-Christian Jewish historian is most unlikely to have written in that form: that Jesus was the Christ; that He appeared on the third day as the prophets had foretold; and the reverent aside “if it be lawful to call him a man.”

Those clauses have a history of interpolation. The problem is a real textual problem. It must be handled as such, not denied and not used as a solvent of the whole paragraph.

What remains, when the clearly Christian clauses are treated as later expansion, is a more modest Josephan notice whose core is widely judged to be authentic in substance: that there was a Jesus, a wise man, a doer of startling deeds, a teacher who won followers among Jews and also among Greeks; that Pilate, at the accusation of leading men, condemned Him to the cross; that those who had loved Him did not cease; and that the tribe of Christians, named from Him, had not disappeared.

A later Arabic citation preserved by Agapius offers a more restrained wording to the same effect, without the full Christian confession of the received Greek. The citation is late. It is useful as a control on the kind of notice Josephus could have written, not as an independent first-century source.

**What a reconstructed core would establish.** A Jewish historian of the 90s, describing Pilate’s time, knew of Jesus as a teacher and wonder-worker who was crucified under Pilate and whose followers continued.

**What even a fully skeptical handling still leaves.** Even the investigator who declines to use any reconstructed core still has *Antiquities* 20.9.1. The *Testimonium* is therefore not the sole Josephan pillar. It is a second, textually complicated notice in the same author, in the right book, in the right prefecture, naming the right penalty.

**What neither form establishes.** Neither the received text nor a reconstructed core is an eyewitness account of the resurrection. Josephus is not converted into an evangelist by being quoted. The value of the passage, to the extent that a pre-Christian core is present, is independent Jewish acknowledgment of the man, the ministry’s public character, the death under Pilate, and the survival of the movement.

Honesty about interpolation is part of warrant. It is not a retreat from the historical conclusion. The James passage does not depend on the *Testimonium*. Tacitus does not depend on Josephus. Paul does not depend on either.

---

# 43.27 Pliny the Younger to Trajan

Pliny the Younger, imperial governor of Bithynia-Pontus, wrote to the emperor Trajan about AD 111–113 concerning persons accused as Christians (*Letters* 10.96). Trajan’s reply is preserved (*Letters* 10.97).

Pliny reports that he has never been present at trials of Christians; that he has interrogated them; that he has executed those who persisted after warning, excepting Roman citizens sent to the city; that an anonymous pamphlet named many; that some denied they were or had been Christians, and proved it by invoking the gods, offering wine and incense to Trajan’s image, and cursing Christ — none of which, Pliny says, those who are truly Christians can be compelled to do.

He reports of their practice, as obtained from those who had been Christians and from two slave women called deaconesses examined under torture: that they met on a stated day before dawn, sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, bound themselves by oath not to some crime but against theft, robbery, adultery, breach of trust, and denial of a deposit; that they then separated and met again to take food, ordinary and harmless; that they had ceased this after Pliny’s edict against associations.

Trajan replies that Christians are not to be hunted, that those accused and convicted are to be punished if they persist, and that anonymous accusations are not to be admitted.

**What the correspondence establishes.** By Trajan’s reign, Christians are a recognizable class in a northern Anatolian province, numerous enough to affect temples and meat-markets, organized enough to have a stated day, hymns, oaths, and a meal, and defined by allegiance to Christ such that cursing Christ is a test of apostasy. Roman administration treats this as a public problem. Christ is the object of worship in their assembly — “as to a god.”

**What it does not establish.** Pliny does not narrate the ministry in Galilee. He does not name Pilate. He does not prove the resurrection. He does not give a biography. He establishes the movement as a public imperial fact, and Christ as its defining name and cultic center, within eighty years of the crucifixion and far from Judea.

A myth invented centuries later cannot be the object of Pliny’s hearings. The hearings are themselves in the early second century. The movement they police is already old enough that some had been Christians and left, some had been so twenty years before, and some were still young. Pliny is evidence of spread and of worship. He is corroboration that the name Christus denotes a real founder of a real movement, already treated as more than a man by His followers. The further question of whether that worship is true is Chapter 44’s question. The existence of the founder whom they worship is this chapter’s question. Pliny knows the worshippers. Tacitus and Josephus know the execution. Paul knows the brother. The lines meet at a person.

# 43.28 Suetonius — What May and May Not Be Used

Suetonius, writing in the early second century, supplies two notices that are often placed beside Tacitus and Pliny. They are not of equal quality.

In *Claudius* 25.4 he records that Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome because they were constantly making disturbances at the instigation of *Chrestus*. Acts 18:2 records that Aquila and Priscilla had come from Italy because Claudius had commanded all Jews to depart from Rome. The expulsion is a real event of the late 40s. Whether *Chrestus* is a misspelling or variant of *Christus*, referring to disputes in the Jewish community about Jesus, or whether it is an otherwise unknown agitator actually present in Rome, cannot be settled from Suetonius’ sentence alone. *Chrestus* is a known slave-name. Tacitus’ *Christus* is unambiguous. Suetonius’ *Chrestus* is not. This chapter therefore does **not** treat *Claudius* 25.4 as an independent proof of Jesus’ existence. It treats it as a possible, not certain, echo of conflict over Christ in the Roman Jewish community, consistent with Acts but not demonstrative by itself.

In *Nero* 16.2 Suetonius lists, among Nero’s acts, punishments inflicted on the Christians, a class of men given to a new and mischievous superstition. That notice is clearer. It independently attests Christians as a known class in Rome under Nero, in agreement with Tacitus, without naming the founder’s death.

The discipline is the same throughout:

> **Use what the sentence will bear. Do not upgrade a possible identification into a proof. Do not discard a clear class-notice because a neighboring sentence is ambiguous.**

Suetonius, taken strictly, supports the public existence of Christians in Rome by the 60s. He does not, on the *Chrestus* sentence, independently narrate Jesus of Nazareth. Tacitus remains the Roman historian who names the execution.

# 43.29 What the External Notices Jointly Establish

Taken together, without forcing any one of them beyond its sentence, the non-biblical notices establish a modest and sufficient conjunction.

**Josephus** knows Jesus who was called Christ, knows His brother James as a Jerusalem figure of the early 60s, and — in a notice whose Christian expansions must be discounted — places a teacher crucified under Pilate whose followers continued.

**Tacitus** knows Christus executed under Pontius Pilate in the reign of Tiberius, origin in Judaea, spread to Rome, a class called Christians hated in the city by the 60s.

**Pliny** knows Christians in Bithynia under Trajan, a cultic center named Christ, a stated day, hymns, a meal, and a test of cursing Christ.

**Suetonius** knows Christians as a punished class under Nero.

These writers are not a church. They are not a single workshop. They are a Jewish aristocrat, a Roman senator-historian, a provincial governor, and a court biographer. Their interests diverge. Their agreement is not on the creed. It is on the public fact of a movement named from a Christus associated with Judea and, in the two most important notices, with death under Pilate.

What they jointly establish is therefore this:

> **By the end of the first century and the beginning of the second, independent Jewish and Roman observers treated Jesus called Christ as a real figure of the recent Jewish past, executed under Pilate, whose followers formed a named, spread, and — to Rome — troublesome class.**

That conjunction is fatal to the claim of a myth invented centuries later. It is not yet the Gospel. It is the external outline of the same figure the Gospel writings fill.

The research material observed that secular history includes pagan as well as Jewish and Christian writers who confirm facts of the biblical narrative. That observation is here given its proper measure. Confirmation is of public facts: a man, an execution, a movement, a prefect, an emperor’s reign. Confirmation is not of every sign, every saying, or the resurrection. Measure is not unbelief. It is the refusal to make Tacitus preach Romans.

# 43.30 The Material and Civic Setting Already Established

Chapter 39 is not to be retried. It is to be used.

The Caesarea inscription names Pontius Pilate prefect of Judea in connection with a Tiberieum. The passion is therefore set under an inscribed official, not under a literary governor.

The Herodian building program — the Temple platform, Caesarea Maritima, Herodium, Masada — is the monumental public world of the Gospel and Acts setting. The Pool of Bethesda, described in John 5 with five porches, has been recovered in a form matching that description. The Pool of Siloam associated with John 9 has been brought to light. Nazareth, Capernaum, and the Galilean lake are real. Jerusalem’s topography is real.

Civic titles in Acts meet inscriptions. Gallio meets Delphi. These traces do not mention Jesus by name. They do something historically precise: they show that the writings which do mention Him occupy a world that stones also occupy.

A figure can be mythical in a real city. The combination of a real city, a real prefect, a real tetrarch, a real Temple, early letters naming a brother, and hostile notice of an execution is not the combination typical of myth. It is the combination typical of a public provincial career.

# 43.31 Competing Claim: The Christ-Myth

The strongest rival to this chapter’s conclusion is the claim that Jesus of Nazareth never existed: that Christ is a myth, a heavenly being later historicized, or a literary construct assembled from Scripture, pagan dying-and-rising patterns, and community need.

The claim has had advocates. Advocacy is not warrant.

The myth-account must explain, together:

- Pauline letters of the 50s that treat a recent crucifixion as the movement’s scandal, name a brother whom the writer has met, and cite a received tradition of death, burial, and appearances to known persons, many still alive;
- Gospel and Acts narratives that locate the man in a checkable political map and appeal, in Jerusalem, to public knowledge;
- Josephus’ identification of James as brother of Jesus who was called Christ, in a narrative about a high priest and a procuratorial interval;
- Tacitus’ hostile placement of the execution under Pilate in Tiberius’ reign;
- a movement already in Rome by the 60s, already in Bithynia as a public problem by Trajan, already defined by allegiance to this Christ.

A myth invented in the third or fourth century explains none of this. The documents and notices are too early.

A myth historicized in the first century, before Paul, would have to have been historicized within a few years of the alleged lifetime, in the land of the alleged events, among persons who could name brothers and pillars, and then passed to a former persecutor who met those persons. That is not a myth in the relevant sense. That is a claim that a historical figure was immediately interpreted. Interpretation of a figure presupposes a figure.

Pagan dying-and-rising patterns, even where they exist in the form alleged, do not create a crucified Jew under Pilate with a brother named James in Jerusalem. Scripture-fulfillment patterns in the Gospels raise questions of interpretation that Chapter 37 has already begun and Chapter 48 will continue. They do not erase the political coordinates.

The Christ-myth therefore fails as an explanation of the conjunction. It is not a live option at the level of ordinary historical method. Failure of the myth is not yet the full Gospel. It is the failure of non-existence.

---

# 43.32 Competing Claim: Legendary Accretion without a Historical Core

A more moderate rival concedes some early tradition but treats the Gospels as uncontrolled legend: stories grown in the telling until the original, if any, is unrecoverable.

Legend exists. The research material noted that in the early centuries many were writing novels and fantastic stories about Jesus, His life, and His parents, and that the assemblies had to distinguish such writings from the authentic testimony. That distinction is historically real. Infancy romances, later apocryphal gospels, and fabricated acts of apostles are a class. Their existence proves that accretion occurred. It does not prove that Mark is an infancy romance.

Several controls distinguish the first-century core from later accretion.

**Time.** Legend grows with distance. Paul’s letters are inside a generation. The received tradition of 1 Corinthians 15 is earlier still. There is not enough time for a purely legendary career to be invented, offered to living brothers and pillars, and accepted by a former enemy who can check.

**Costly particulars.** Nazareth, crucifixion, family unbelief, baptism by John, and the scandal of the cross are poor legendary ornaments. Later accretion adds grandeur — marvelous infancies, detached discourses, cosmic voyages. The earliest stratum is more spare and more politically located.

**Multiple streams.** Paul, Markan narrative, Johannine narrative, and Jerusalem tradition in Acts are not a single overnight tale. They overlap on the identity of the man, the death under the prefect, and the existence of a named circle. Overlap of that kind is the residue of a public career more readily than of a single legend-shop.

**Hostile and independent notice.** Legend internal to a community does not by itself create Tacitus’ procurator and Josephus’ brother.

Accretion around a real person is therefore admissible as a description of later literature and even of some later churchly expansion. It is not admissible as a global explanation of the first-century conjunction. The historical core is recoverable as existence, Jewish setting, public ministry, and death under Pilate. Recoverability of that core is this chapter’s conclusion. Recoverability of every later pious story is not.

---

# 43.33 Competing Claim: Jesus as Only a Later Doctrinal Construct

A third rival grants a name in early texts but treats “Jesus Christ” as a cipher for later doctrine: the churches first had a theology, then projected a biography.

The order of the sources is against the rival.

Paul, the earliest writer, already has a man born of a woman, born under the law, of David’s seed according to the flesh, with brothers, betrayed at night, crucified, buried. That is biography in outline, however brief. It is not a council’s metaphysical formula seeking a narrative.

The Gospels, whatever their confessional aim, are narratives of a career before they are treatises. They can be used theologically because they first present a life.

Later doctrinal development is real. Vocabulary thickens. Disputes about how to speak of the Person occupy later centuries. That thickening is Movement VI’s later problem only insofar as Chapters 44 and following must not read later technical terms back into the historical baseline as if the baseline were already those terms. It is not evidence that the baseline is empty.

The distinction of §43.4 is the answer to this rival:

> **Later doctrine develops from testimony concerning a person. It does not, in this file, create the person out of doctrine.**

---

# 43.34 Reason through the Evidence

The reasoning can now be exhibited.

If a Jewish man of Galilee was publicly active in the late 20s and early 30s, was associated with John, gathered disciples, came to Jerusalem, and was crucified under Pilate, the investigator would expect early testimony from the movement that followed Him, named contacts with His family and first disciples, a political setting that matches the known administration of Judea and Galilee, and, in time, notice from outsiders who had no reason to confess Him.

That is what the file contains.

If there were no such man, the investigator would expect the name to appear late, the political map to be theatrical or wrong, the family names to be absent, the death to be unlocated, and hostile Roman history to explain Christians without a crucified founder of Tiberius’ reign.

That is not what the file contains.

The positive bearing is therefore the match between the expected residue of a public provincial career and the actual residue. The negative bearing is the failure of myth, of coreless legend, and of pure doctrinal projection to account for the same residue.

The form of reasoning is abductive historical warrant, as Chapter 42 defined it for this kind of object. It is not Euclidean demonstration. It is not institutional decree. It is not circular appeal to later authority. It is the same form by which Pilate, Antipas, Caiaphas, and John the Baptist are themselves known.

Proportionality remains. The reasoning warrants existence, Jewish identity, Galilean-Judean setting, public ministry, and execution under Pilate. It does not, in this chapter, warrant the further conclusions reserved to Chapters 44–50. Sufficiency is met for this level. Remaining local questions — the precise year of death, the relation of Luke’s census notice to AD 6, the exact wording of Josephus’ *Testimonium* — are local. They are not a global rival.

---

# 43.35 What Is Explicit and What Is Inferred

Intellectual honesty requires the distinction.

**Explicit in the biblical writings:** Jesus is a man of Nazareth and Galilee; He is Jewish, under the law, of Davidic claim; He is baptized by John; He teaches publicly; He has disciples and brothers; He comes into conflict with portions of the leadership; He is arrested, tried in connection with the prefect, and crucified; the movement that follows Him proclaims these things in Jerusalem and beyond.

**Explicit in Paul:** birth, law, Davidic flesh, brothers, betrayal, crucifixion, burial, received tradition, named witnesses, the writer’s own former persecution of the assembly.

**Explicit in Tacitus:** Christus executed under Pilate in Tiberius’ reign; Judaea as origin; Christians in Rome by Nero.

**Explicit in Josephus 20.9.1:** James, brother of Jesus who was called Christ, stoned in Jerusalem under Ananus in the procuratorial interval after Festus.

**Explicit in Pliny:** Christians in Bithynia who worship Christ, cannot be made to curse Him, and are a public administrative problem under Trajan.

**Inferred, with warrant:** that these sources refer to one and the same historical person, not to a cluster of unrelated Christs; that the political coordinates agree because they describe one career; that the movement Pliny polices is the movement Tacitus explains by the execution under Pilate; that the Jesus whose brother Josephus names is the Jesus Paul met in that brother’s person.

**Inferred, and not required for this chapter’s conclusion:** the precise calendar date of the crucifixion; a full harmony of every Gospel day-count; a reconstructed original Greek of the *Testimonium* word for word; the identification of Suetonius’ *Chrestus* with Jesus.

**Not established here, though named in the sources:** that the works authenticate the claims; that the resurrection occurred; that Jesus is the truth; that Scripture cannot be broken because He said so; that the Graphē is primary written authority.

The inference from the explicit notices to a single historical career is an inference. It is a short inference. Multiple independent identifications of a crucified Christus under Pilate, with a brother James in Jerusalem, are not plausibly identifications of different men. The warranted conclusion uses that inference and names it.

---

# 43.36 The Warranted Conclusion

The investigator is therefore rationally warranted, with full force, in concluding:

> **Jesus of Nazareth existed. He was a Jewish man of the early first century, associated with Galilee and with Nazareth, active publicly in the last years of the Second Temple, related to John the Baptist’s movement, accompanied by disciples, possessed of known family including James, brought to Jerusalem, and executed by crucifixion under Pontius Pilate in the reign of Tiberius. The movement that bore His name was public in Judea and, within a generation, in Rome. Independent Jewish and Roman writers, who did not confess Him, nevertheless treated Him as a real figure of that recent past.**

That conclusion is ordinary historical knowledge. It is not a church’s gift to history. It is not a concession extracted from unbelief. It is the residue of testimony and notice.

A real historical figure is the necessary object of Movement VI. Without Him, “Christ as the central witness” would be a phrase about a symbol. With Him, the phrase is about a Person who can be further examined: in relation to truth and revelation; in His claims and works; in His death and resurrection; in the eyewitnesses; in His treatment of Scripture; in the apostles He commissioned.

The baseline is not thin. It is sufficient. Sufficiency for existence is not sufficiency for every later chapter. It is sufficiency for this one.

---

# 43.37 Why This Is Not Circular on Movement V

A charge of circularity would run:

> **The book first uses the writings to authenticate the writings, then uses the authenticated writings to prove Jesus, then will use Jesus to prove the writings.**

The charge misunderstands the levels.

Movement V authenticated the writings as genuine recoverable testimony of claims, historically embedded, not as already infallible primary authority. That is a conclusion about documents.

This chapter uses those documents as historical sources, together with independent notices that do not depend on Movement V at all. Tacitus is not warranted by Chapter 42. Josephus is not warranted by Chapter 42. Pliny is not warranted by Chapter 42. An investigator who declined every conclusion of Movement V would still have to explain Paul, Josephus, and Tacitus. The historical existence of Jesus is therefore not hostage to the Graphē-gate. It is over-determined.

What Movement V contributes is not a circular proof of existence. It contributes the right to treat the Gospels and letters as authentic testimony rather than as late folklore, which strengthens the interior of the career — places, disputes, public teaching — beyond the bare external outline. The outline stands without that right. The interior is clearer with it.

Movement VIII will ask why the Graphē is primary written authority, and will use Christ’s verdict on what is written. That later use requires a historical Christ who actually reasoned from Scripture. It does not require this chapter to assume the later verdict. The order is:

> **Historical person (Ch 43) → His relation to truth and revelation (Ch 44) → claims and works (Ch 45) → death and resurrection (Ch 46) → eyewitnesses (Ch 47) → His confirmation of Scripture (Ch 48) → apostolic witness and its authority (Ch 49–50) → later, His verdict that the scripture cannot be broken (Chs 59–63).**

Each step uses the prior. None silently imports the last.

---

# 43.38 Why Christ Is Not Merely One Evidence Class

Archaeology, manuscripts, prophecy, miracles, and unity are evidence classes in Movement V. Christ is not a sixth class of the same kind.

Those classes authenticate a written record. Christ is the Person toward whom the record is aimed, the One the record names as the faithful witness, the Son through whom God has spoken, the center of the later writings’ coherence.

The architecture of the book therefore changes at this movement. The question is no longer only “Is this writing authentic testimony?” The question becomes “Who is the central witness of the testimony, and what follows from His place in history, His relation to truth, His works, His death and resurrection, and His treatment of the writings?”

Chapter 43 supplies the historical condition of that change. A central witness must be a witness in history, or the title is metaphor. Revelation 1:5 will be developed as faithful witness in its proper place. The title is already in the record. The record is already warranted as testimony. The Person named by the title has now been located as a figure of the reign of Tiberius.

That is why Movement VI opens with history rather than with John 14:6. Truth-claims about a Person who did not exist would be a different investigation. Truth-claims about a Person who did exist can be examined as claims.

---

# 43.39 The Historical Baseline and the Interior of the Career

What, more precisely, belongs to the baseline as history, and what belongs to later examination as claim?

**To the baseline:** existence; Jewishness; Galilee and Nazareth; the window of Tiberius and Pilate; association with John; disciples; brothers including James; public teaching and controversy in synagogue and Temple; movement to Jerusalem; crucifixion under the prefect; a following that survived in Judea and spread.

**To Chapter 45 as claims and works:** the particular miracles as signs; the particular sayings as authenticating His identity; the authority with which He spoke; the relation of works to claims.

**To Chapter 46:** the full passion sequence as evidential case; burial; the empty tomb; the appearances; the resurrection as historical claim of the highest consequence.

**To Chapter 47:** the epistemology of the eyewitnesses of His majesty, including 2 Peter 1:16 as a dedicated file.

The interior of the career is not therefore unknown. It is known in outline. Outline is what historical method first yields for almost every ancient person. Alexander, Socrates, and Hillel are known in outline plus disputed detail. Jesus is known in outline plus an unusually dense first-century dossier. Density is not a reason to postpone existence. It is a reason to proceed, in order, through the dossier.

---

# 43.40 The Scope of This Stage

This is the scope statement for the historical baseline. It is sequence and limitation of level, not a withdrawal of force.

By sequence, later chapters examine:

- Christ’s relationship to truth and divine revelation, including John 14:6 (Chapter 44);
- the claims and works of Christ as an authentication axis (Chapter 45);
- the death and resurrection as the central historical claims of the passion and the apostolic proclamation (Chapter 46);
- the eyewitness testimony, including 2 Peter 1:16 and related apostolic testimony (Chapter 47);
- Christ’s confirmation of the Scriptures He received, quoted, interpreted, and appealed to (Chapter 48);
- Christ’s relationship to the apostles and the authority of their testimony (Chapters 49–50);
- the scriptural corpus, canonical recognition, and infallibility (Movement VII);
- John 10:34–36, “the scripture cannot be broken,” “Thy word is truth,” and why the Graphē is the primary written authority (Movement VIII);
- mishandling, tergiversation, and ἄπειρος (*apeiros*, G552) (Movement IX);
- the scholarly labour of the Graphē, including σπουδάζω (*spoudazō*, G4704), and readiness for the Six First Principles (Movement X).

Those questions remain later because they are different questions. The present conclusion is already in force for its level.

This chapter has not established that Jesus is the truth. It has not authenticated His works as a finished case. It has not proved the resurrection. It has not treated the eyewitness file as complete. It has not established His confirmation of Scripture as a doctrine of the Graphē. It has not established apostolic authority as a finished argument. It has not claimed that the Graphē is primary written authority in the Movement VIII sense. It has not treated John 10 as finished. It has not entered the First Principles.

It has established that Jesus of Nazareth is a real historical figure of the early first century, publicly active in Judea and Galilee under Rome, executed under Pontius Pilate, and attested by early Christian testimony and by independent notices.

Local chronological questions, the interpolation-history of the *Testimonium*, and the ambiguity of Suetonius’ *Chrestus* remain as local questions. They are not a global rival. They do not reopen existence. They do not convert the baseline into a maybe.

One scope statement is enough. The force of the historical conclusion is not diminished by the existence of later chapters.

---

# 43.41 Apply the Standard

The reader should now be able to take any proposed sentence about “Jesus” and ask:

> **Is this sentence a claim of historical existence, a claim of public ministry, a later doctrinal formula, a legendary accretion, or a reserved evidential case of Movement VI?**

Then:

> **If it is a claim of existence, what sources bear upon it, and are they early, independent, and particular?**

Then:

> **Does the proposed rival explain Paul, James in Josephus, and Tacitus together, or only a preferred strand?**

Then:

> **Is the conclusion being postponed because later chapters remain, or is it being drawn at the level the grounds support?**

If the grounds are the first-century writings plus the independent notices, existence and public execution under Pilate are to be granted with full force.

If a later conclusion is desired — that He is the truth, that His works authenticate Him, that God raised Him, that He confirms Scripture — it is to be sought in its chapter, not smuggled into this baseline and not used to postpone this baseline.

If a catalogue of “secular proofs” mixes Tacitus’ sentence with later pious legend and with the virtue of the churches, the catalogue is to be classified before it is allowed to speak of history. Tacitus can warrant a death under Pilate. An infancy romance cannot. Classification is the discipline of the whole book.

The governing maxim remains:

> **Do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture. Teach the reader to reason through Scripture.**

Reasoning through the writings at this stage, together with the notices that stand outside them, yields a historical Jesus of Nazareth. That is not the abandonment of faith. It is the refusal to call an unlocated name by the title of central witness.

---

# 43.42 From Historical Existence to Truth and Revelation

A real man can still be misapprehended. Existence does not settle identity.

The writings that have now been used as historical sources do not stop at existence. They present this man as the Son through whom God has spoken, as the Word made flesh, as the faithful witness, as the One who says, “I am the way, the truth, and the life.” Those are further claims. They are the claims for which a historical baseline exists.

Chapter 26 already recorded them as the record’s own presentation. Chapter 44 must examine them as the next necessary question:

> **What is Jesus Christ’s relationship to truth and to divine revelation, and what follows from His own identification of Himself with the truth?**

That question is not a retreat from history. It is the first theological and revelatory question that history now makes responsible. A myth cannot be the truth in the sense John 14:6 claims. A historical person can be examined as to whether He is merely a teacher of true sentences, or the One in whom truth and revelation are personally present.

The investigation therefore moves from:

> **He was there.**

to:

> **Who, in relation to truth, is the One who was there?**

That is the proper open of the remainder of Movement VI. History was never meant to be the last word. It was meant to be a word sufficient to justify taking the next one.

# What Has Been Established

This chapter has established:

1. **The historical question concerning Jesus Christ is the question of His existence and public career as a figure of first-century history, asked under ordinary historical method.**
2. **Historical existence and public ministry are distinct from later doctrinal development, from legendary accretion, and from the remaining evidential cases of Movement VI.**
3. **Ordinary historical method is competent here. A unique veto demanding autograph court transcripts is not historical rigor.**
4. **The Gospels, Acts, and Pauline letters may be examined as historical sources without treating the Graphē as already primary written authority in the Movement VIII sense.**
5. **Paul’s letters of the 50s are the earliest Christian documents and already presuppose a recent Jewish man, born, related, betrayed, crucified, and remembered by named persons the writer knew.**
6. **1 Corinthians 15:3–8 is early received tradition. It warrants, at this stage, a real death and burial of a known man, and names living witnesses; the resurrection clause is reserved.**
7. **The Gospels present history of a public career in a checkable land under named authorities. Luke 1:1–4 is historiographical method inside the corpus.**
8. **Acts treats the career as public knowledge in Jerusalem and continues it into a civic world already corroborated.**
9. **Names, places, families, and institutions locate Jesus inside a known network, not in a floating sacred landscape.**
10. **The chronological window is the reign of Tiberius and the prefecture of Pilate, the last generation of the Second Temple. Local friction in Luke 2 does not cancel the window.**
11. **Judea under a prefect and Galilee under Antipas are the political conditions of a crucifixion at Jerusalem.**
12. **The immediate world is Second Temple Judaism, ordered by Temple, synagogue, feast, party, and received Scripture.**
13. **John the Baptist is an independent historical marker attaching Jesus’ opening ministry to a public crisis under Antipas.**
14. **Crucifixion under Pilate is among the most secure public facts of the file.**
15. **Tacitus establishes a hostile Roman notice of Christus executed under Pilate in Tiberius’ reign, origin in Judaea, presence in Rome by Nero.**
16. **Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.9.1, establishes James as brother of Jesus who was called Christ. The *Testimonium* is textually complicated and is not required as the sole Josephan pillar.**
17. **Pliny establishes Christians in Bithynia under Trajan who worship Christ and cannot be made to curse Him. Suetonius’ *Chrestus* is not used beyond its uncertainty; his Nero notice attests the class.**
18. **The external notices jointly destroy the claim of a myth invented centuries later.**
19. **Coreless legendary accretion and pure doctrinal projection fail as explanations of the first-century conjunction.**
20. **The investigator is rationally warranted, with full force, in treating Jesus of Nazareth as a real historical figure of the early first century.**
21. **This baseline does not circularly assume Movement VIII. Independent notices would remain even if Movement V were declined. Movement V clarifies the interior of the career.**
22. **Christ is not merely one evidence class among stones and manuscripts. He is the central witness whose historical place Movement VI now investigates.**
23. **What is reserved is Chapters 44–50 in full; canon; infallibility; John 10 / “cannot be broken”; First Principles; *spoudazō*; and the Graphē as primary written authority in the Movement VIII sense.**

# Chapter Summary

> **The chapter’s central conclusion:** Jesus of Nazareth is a real historical figure of the early first century, a Jewish man of Galilee and Judea under Roman administration, publicly active in the last years of the Second Temple, and executed by crucifixion under Pontius Pilate in the reign of Tiberius. The conclusion follows from ordinary historical method applied to the Pauline letters, the Gospels and Acts as historical sources, and independent notices in Josephus, Tacitus, and Pliny. He is not a myth invented centuries later. Later doctrine, legendary accretion, and the remaining cases of Movement VI are distinct objects. The historical baseline is full force for this stage. The investigation now turns to His relationship to truth and divine revelation.

The essential architecture is:

> **Warranted written testimony → historical question → sources and setting → external notices → failure of myth and coreless legend → existence and public career warranted → sequence to truth and revelation.**

The governing distinctions are:

- **Existence and public ministry** are this chapter’s object.
- **Later doctrine** is reception, not the man.
- **Legendary accretion** is a later literary class, not the first-century core.
- **Death under Pilate** is a public historical fact named here; **resurrection** is reserved.
- **Biblical writings as historical sources** are not yet the Graphē as primary written authority.
- **Scope** is sequence of levels, not hesitation about this level.

The decisive principle is:

> **Establish the historical person before developing the theological implications. Draw the conclusion the premises support. Do not draw the conclusions that still require later premises. Then ask what this Person has to do with truth.**
