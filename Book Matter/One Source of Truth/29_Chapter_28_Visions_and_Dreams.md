# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 28 — Visions and Dreams

> **Movement IV — How God Communicates Revelation**

---

## Chapter Assignment

The preceding chapters have established a progressive chain.

God is the ultimate source of revelation.

The prophets are commissioned recipients and communicators.

Jesus Christ is presented as the decisive revelation of God in the Son.

The apostles are Christ-commissioned witnesses whose testimony stands immediately behind the New Testament proclamation and written record.

The present chapter examines another major biblical category of divine communication:

> **visions and dreams.**

The Bible does not treat every dream as revelation, nor does it treat every human perception as a divine vision.

It nevertheless repeatedly records particular dreams and visions as occasions in which God makes something known.

The question is therefore not:

> **“Do people dream?”**

Nor merely:

> **“Can people have unusual experiences?”**

The precise question is:

> **How does the Bible itself identify, describe, distinguish, interpret, authenticate, and transmit visions and dreams that it presents as divine communication?**

That question must be examined carefully because the biblical record contains both genuine revelatory experiences and explicit warnings about false dreams and false visions.

The distinction is therefore not between:

> **dreams = revelation**

and:

> **dreams = imagination.**

The biblical category is more precise.

The issue is whether a particular experience is presented as **God-given revelation**, what message it conveys, whether its interpretation is given, whether its claim is authenticated, and how that revelation enters the prophetic or written record.

---

# Central Objective

> **To establish from the Bible's own testimony how God communicates through visions and dreams, how these experiences are distinguished from ordinary human dreams and imagination, how divine revelations are received and interpreted, how false or self-generated claims are identified, and how genuine revelatory experiences become testimony and, where God so directs, part of the written record.**

The chapter will establish:

1. the biblical vocabulary of vision and dream;
2. the relationship between seeing, perceiving, dreaming, and revelation;
3. the distinction between ordinary dreams and revelatory dreams;
4. the distinction between waking visions, dream visions, and other revelatory experiences;
5. Numbers 12:6–8 as a foundational biblical statement;
6. the patriarchal examples;
7. Joseph's dreams;
8. Pharaoh's dreams and Joseph's interpretation;
9. Samuel and the prophetic word;
10. Daniel's visions and dreams;
11. Ezekiel's visions;
12. Isaiah's vision;
13. Amos and the minor prophets;
14. Joel's prophecy concerning dreams and visions;
15. New Testament dreams associated with Joseph and the infancy narratives;
16. Peter's vision in Acts 10;
17. Paul's visionary experiences;
18. John's visions in Revelation;
19. the distinction between receiving a revelation and interpreting it;
20. divine interpretation and human interpretation;
21. the Bible's warnings about false dreams and false visions;
22. the importance of testing claims;
23. the relationship between vision, dream, prophetic speech, testimony, and writing;
24. and the place of visions and dreams within the larger doctrine of revelation and the Graphē.

# 28.1 The Question of Divine Communication

A revelation must first be distinguished from ordinary knowledge.

If a person discovers something through ordinary observation, that knowledge does not necessarily constitute revelation.

If a person reasons from known facts, that reasoning does not automatically constitute revelation.

If a person dreams while asleep, the mere occurrence of the dream does not establish divine origin.

The Bible itself makes these distinctions necessary.

Yet the same Bible records specific instances in which God communicates through dreams and visions.

Therefore the correct proposition is:

> **Dreams and visions can function as instruments of divine revelation, but the existence of a dream or vision does not by itself establish divine revelation.**

This distinction is essential to the biblical evidence.

---

# 28.2 Numbers 12:6 — A Foundational Statement

One of the clearest biblical statements occurs in Numbers 12:6.

The LORD declares:

> **“If there be a prophet among you, I the LORD will make myself known unto him in a vision, and will speak unto him in a dream.”**

The verse is important because the speaker is God Himself.

The subject is divine self-disclosure.

The means identified are:

> **vision**

and:

> **dream.**

The structure is therefore:

> **God**
>
> ↓
>
> **makes Himself known**
>
> ↓
>
> **vision / dream**
>
> ↓
>
> **prophetic recipient**

This is not an inference imposed upon the Bible from outside.

It is the Bible's own description of one mode by which God makes Himself known.

---

# 28.3 The Hebrew Vocabulary of Vision

Numbers 12:6 uses the Hebrew noun **מַרְאָה (*marʾâh*)**, commonly translated “vision” or “appearance.”

It is related to the verbal root **ראה (*rāʾâh*)**, “to see.”

The semantic relationship is important.

The category of vision is connected to perception or seeing.

This does not mean that every biblical use of a word related to “seeing” refers to supernatural revelation.

Lexical relationship is not theological proof.

But it does establish why the biblical language naturally describes a revelatory vision in terms of something perceived or shown.

Numbers 12:6 then places this vision alongside the dream as a mode through which God communicates.

---

# 28.4 The Hebrew Vocabulary of Dream

The Hebrew noun **חֲלוֹם (*ḥălôm*)** means “dream.”

It is the ordinary biblical term for a dream.

The corresponding verb **חָלַם (*ḥālam*)** means “to dream.”

The word itself therefore does not automatically mean:

> **divine revelation.**

This is an important lexical distinction.

The same vocabulary can occur in contexts involving ordinary dreams and in contexts where a dream carries prophetic significance.

The context determines the function.

This is why lexical study must not be turned into a shortcut to theology.

---

# 28.5 A Word Does Not Automatically Determine the Event

Consider the logic.

If *ḥălôm* means “dream,” then:

> **dream = dream**

It does not follow that:

> **every dream = divine revelation.**

Likewise, if *marʾâh* means “vision,” it does not follow that:

> **every visual experience = divine revelation.**

The Bible itself requires contextual discrimination.

The important question is:

> **What does God do with this dream or vision in this particular passage?**

---

# 28.6 Numbers 12:6–8 Establishes a Distinction

Numbers 12 does more than mention dreams and visions.

It compares God's communication with prophets to His communication with Moses.

The passage states that God makes Himself known to a prophet in a vision and speaks in a dream.

But concerning Moses, the LORD says:

> **“With him will I speak mouth to mouth.”**

The comparison is significant.

The Bible therefore recognizes different modes or degrees of revelatory communication.

The existence of visions and dreams does not mean that every prophet receives revelation in exactly the same form.

---

# 28.7 Revelation Is Not Reduced to One Mode

The Bible's revelation is therefore not technologically uniform.

God speaks through:

- direct speech;
- prophetic proclamation;
- visions;
- dreams;
- symbolic acts;
- angels;
- written commandments;
- historical acts;
- and ultimately through the Son.

The variety does not create contradiction.

It demonstrates that revelation concerns the **divine source and message**, not merely one mechanical delivery mechanism.

---

# 28.8 Vision and Dream Are Not the Same Experience

The biblical record often distinguishes visions and dreams.

A dream normally occurs in the context of sleep.

A vision can occur while the recipient is awake.

Daniel's experience is especially useful because the language can place dream and vision together.

Daniel 7:1 says that Daniel had a dream and visions of his head upon his bed.

Thus:

> **dream**

and:

> **vision**

can overlap in a single revelatory experience.

The categories should therefore be distinguished without being artificially separated.

---

# 28.9 The Broader Biblical Category: Seeing

The Old Testament contains several terms associated with seeing, appearance, vision, and revelation.

Among the important Hebrew terms are:

- **מַרְאָה (*marʾâh*)** — appearance, vision;
- **חָזוֹן (*ḥāzôn*)** — vision, revelation;
- **מַחֲזֶה (*maḥăzeh*)** — vision;
- related verbal forms from roots involving seeing or beholding.

Their semantic ranges overlap but are not identical.

The proper method is therefore not:

> “One Hebrew word means one theological concept everywhere.”

The proper method is:

> **examine the word, grammar, context, literary setting, and function in the passage.**

---

# 28.10 The Greek Vocabulary of Vision

The New Testament likewise employs several terms.

One important noun is:

> **ὅραμα (*horama*)**

meaning a vision or something seen.

It is related to the Greek verbal field of seeing.

Another term is:

> **ὅρασις (*horasis*)**

which can refer to sight, appearance, or vision.

The term:

> **ὀπτασία (*optasia*)**

is also used for a vision or appearance.

Again, the lexical data establishes the language used to describe the experience.

The theological conclusion must be established from the context in which the experience occurs.

---

# 28.11 Revelation Is the Larger Category

A vision or dream is therefore not identical to revelation as a whole.

Rather:

> **revelation is the larger category.**

A vision or dream may be one means through which revelation is communicated.

The relationship can be expressed:

> **Divine revelation**
>
> ↓
>
> **possible modes**
>
> → speech  
> → vision  
> → dream  
> → angelic communication  
> → prophetic action  
> → historical manifestation  
> → written command  
> → Christ

This prevents the chapter from making visions and dreams larger than Scripture makes them.

---

# 28.12 Jacob's Dream at Bethel

Genesis 28 records Jacob's dream at Bethel.

Jacob sees a ladder or stairway associated with heaven and earth and receives a divine communication concerning God's covenant promises.

The dream therefore contains:

1. an experience during sleep;
2. symbolic imagery;
3. divine communication;
4. a covenantal message;
5. and a response from the recipient.

The dream is not merely an emotional episode.

The content communicates something concerning God's purposes.

---

# 28.13 Jacob's Later Dream

Genesis 31 records another dream in which God speaks to Jacob concerning the events involving Laban.

Again, the dream functions as a means of divine communication.

The significance is not that Jacob happened to dream.

The significance is that the narrative identifies the communication as coming from God.

Thus:

> **the biblical text identifies the source and function of the dream.**

---

# 28.14 Joseph's Dreams

Genesis 37 records Joseph's dreams.

Joseph dreams of sheaves bowing down to his sheaf and later of the sun, moon, and stars bowing before him.

The dreams are presented within the developing narrative of Joseph's life.

Their meaning becomes increasingly significant as later events unfold.

The dreams therefore illustrate an important feature of biblical revelation:

> **the meaning of a revelation may become clearer through subsequent history.**

The event is experienced first.

Its significance may be understood more fully later.

---

# 28.15 Revelation and Fulfillment

Joseph's dreams become especially important because the narrative later presents events corresponding to their content.

This creates a relationship between:

> **revelation**

and:

> **historical development.**

The dream is not authenticated merely because Joseph says he had it.

Its meaning becomes intelligible in relation to what subsequently happens.

This establishes an important biblical pattern:

> **revelatory claim → interpretation → historical development.**

---

# 28.16 Pharaoh's Dreams

Genesis 40–41 expands the category.

Pharaoh dreams.

His officials dream.

The dreams trouble them.

The problem is not merely experiencing the dream.

The problem is understanding it.

This introduces a crucial distinction:

> **receiving a dream is not identical to interpreting a dream.**

A revelation may be received by one person while its meaning is given to another.

---

# 28.17 Joseph as Interpreter

Joseph tells Pharaoh:

> **“Do not interpretations belong to God?”**

This is a decisive statement.

Joseph does not claim autonomous interpretive authority.

He attributes the interpretation to God.

The structure becomes:

> **God gives the revelation**
>
> ↓
>
> **human recipient receives it**
>
> ↓
>
> **God gives or enables interpretation**
>
> ↓
>
> **meaning is communicated**

This is one of the clearest biblical examples of the distinction between revelation and interpretation.

---

# 28.18 Daniel and Nebuchadnezzar

Daniel 2 provides another major example.

Nebuchadnezzar has a disturbing dream.

His court interpreters cannot provide the required answer.

Daniel seeks God.

The mystery is then revealed to Daniel in a night vision.

Daniel does not claim that his own intellect independently discovered the dream's hidden meaning.

He gives glory to God.

The narrative explicitly connects the revelation to God.

---

# 28.19 Revelation of the Hidden Thing

Daniel 2 is particularly important because the issue is a **mystery**.

The king possesses the dream.

Daniel receives the revelation of what the king dreamed and its interpretation.

The biblical pattern is therefore:

> **hidden information**
>
> ↓
>
> **God reveals**
>
> ↓
>
> **Daniel receives**
>
> ↓
>
> **Daniel interprets**
>
> ↓
>
> **the king receives the message**

This is a sophisticated revelatory structure.

---

# 28.20 Human Intelligence and Divine Revelation

Daniel is an intelligent man.

He reasons.

He speaks.

He interprets.

He organizes the information.

Yet the narrative does not attribute the hidden knowledge merely to Daniel's intelligence.

Daniel explicitly attributes the revelation to God.

This is an important distinction:

> **human intelligence can participate in receiving, organizing, communicating, and interpreting revelation without becoming its ultimate source.**

---

# 28.21 Daniel's Visions

Daniel 7 begins:

> **“Daniel had a dream and visions of his head upon his bed.”**

The chapter then presents symbolic beasts, kingdoms, judgment, and the kingdom of God.

The revelatory experience is visual and symbolic.

Daniel sees.

Daniel is troubled.

Daniel seeks understanding.

The interpretation is subsequently provided.

This makes Daniel a major biblical case study in the movement:

> **vision → observation → inquiry → interpretation → written record.**

---

# 28.22 Daniel Writes the Vision

Daniel 7:1 also says:

> **“Then he wrote the dream, and told the sum of the matters.”**

This is crucial for the Doctrine of the Graphē.

The sequence is explicit:

> **revelatory experience**
>
> ↓
>
> **reception**
>
> ↓
>
> **writing**

The vision is not left permanently as a private experience.

It becomes written testimony.

This is one of the clearest biblical examples of revelation entering the written record.

---

# 28.23 Daniel 8 and the Problem of Understanding

Daniel 8 again presents a vision.

Daniel sees symbolic imagery.

He then seeks its meaning.

The angelic messenger provides interpretation.

The passage therefore reinforces a central distinction:

> **seeing does not automatically equal understanding.**

The recipient may genuinely receive the revelation while requiring interpretation.

---

# 28.24 Daniel 10

Daniel 10 describes another extraordinary experience.

Daniel sees a figure and experiences physical effects.

The vision is associated with a message concerning future events.

Again, the experience is not merely an emotion.

The narrative connects:

> **vision**

with:

> **message**

and:

> **understanding.**

---

# 28.25 Ezekiel's Opening Vision

Ezekiel 1 begins with:

> **“the heavens were opened, and I saw visions of God.”**

The wording is exceptionally direct.

Ezekiel does not merely say:

> “I imagined something.”

He identifies the experience as:

> **visions of God.**

The chapter then gives an elaborate description of what he saw.

The vision becomes the setting for Ezekiel's prophetic commission.

---

# 28.26 Ezekiel's Repeated Visions

Ezekiel repeatedly describes visionary experiences.

Ezekiel 8 records the prophet being shown things concerning Jerusalem.

Ezekiel 40 begins another major visionary experience concerning the temple.

The pattern is repeated:

> **God shows**
>
> ↓
>
> **the prophet sees**
>
> ↓
>
> **the prophet receives explanation**
>
> ↓
>
> **the prophet records or communicates**

The visionary experience therefore functions within a larger prophetic ministry.

---

# 28.27 Isaiah's Vision

Isaiah 6 begins:

> **“I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up.”**

The vision is followed by:

- heavenly worship;
- Isaiah's recognition of his condition;
- purification;
- God's question;
- Isaiah's response;
- and commissioning.

The vision therefore does not exist merely for spectacle.

It accomplishes revelation and commission.

The experience results in:

> **“Here am I; send me.”**

---

# 28.28 Vision and Commission

Isaiah's example shows that a divine vision can have vocational consequences.

The structure is:

> **God reveals Himself**
>
> ↓
>
> **the recipient perceives**
>
> ↓
>
> **the recipient understands something of God's holiness**
>
> ↓
>
> **the recipient is commissioned**
>
> ↓
>
> **the recipient speaks**

Thus revelation can produce prophetic mission.

---

# 28.29 Amos

Amos repeatedly records visions.

Amos 7 presents visions involving:

- locusts;
- fire;
- a plumb line.

Amos 8 presents a basket of summer fruit.

Amos 9 presents another visionary scene.

These visions are symbolic.

They communicate judgment.

The prophet does not merely observe the symbols.

He receives their meaning and communicates God's message.

---

# 28.30 Zechariah

Zechariah provides one of the richest collections of symbolic visions in the minor prophets.

The book contains multiple visions involving:

- horsemen;
- horns;
- craftsmen;
- a measuring line;
- the high priest Joshua;
- the lampstand and olive trees;
- the flying scroll;
- the woman in the ephah;
- and chariots.

The repeated question:

> **“What are these?”**

is itself instructive.

Zechariah sees.

Zechariah asks.

The angel explains.

The revelation therefore involves both:

> **vision**

and:

> **interpretation.**

---

# 28.31 Habakkuk

Habakkuk 2:2 says:

> **“Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables.”**

This verse is exceptionally important for the written-record question.

The vision is to be:

> **written**

and:

> **made plain.**

The movement is therefore explicit:

> **vision**
>
> ↓
>
> **writing**
>
> ↓
>
> **communication to others**

This is precisely the transition this chapter is designed to examine.

---

# 28.32 Writing Is Part of the Revelatory Process

A vision can be genuinely revelatory without remaining a private experience.

When the prophet is commanded to write, the revelation is transformed into a durable communicative form.

This does not mean the paper or ink is itself the original experience.

It means the written record becomes the authorized vehicle for communicating what was revealed.

That distinction becomes essential later when the doctrine of inspiration is examined.

---

# 28.33 Joel's Prophecy

Joel 2:28 declares:

> **“your old men shall dream dreams, your young men shall see visions.”**

The prophecy associates:

> dreams

and:

> visions

with the outpouring of God's Spirit.

Acts 2 later quotes this passage in Peter's Pentecost sermon.

The New Testament therefore explicitly interprets Joel's language within the events surrounding Pentecost.

---

# 28.34 Acts 2 and the Spirit

Peter's use of Joel establishes a major New Testament connection:

> **Spirit**
>
> ↓
>
> **prophetic communication**
>
> ↓
>
> **dreams and visions**

This does not mean that every Christian dream is automatically prophetic.

The biblical text does not make that equation.

It means that dreams and visions belong to the biblical category of revelatory activity associated with God's Spirit.

---

# 28.35 New Testament Dreams — Joseph

Matthew's infancy narrative records dreams received by Joseph.

Matthew 1:20 describes an angel of the Lord appearing to Joseph in a dream.

Matthew 2 contains additional dreams warning Joseph and directing his actions.

The structure is clear:

> **dream**
>
> ↓
>
> **divine message**
>
> ↓
>
> **instruction**
>
> ↓
>
> **obedient action**

These dreams are therefore functional revelations.

They communicate specific information necessary for action.

---

# 28.36 The Magi

Matthew 2:12 records the Magi being warned in a dream not to return to Herod.

Again:

> **dream**
>
> ↓
>
> **warning**
>
> ↓
>
> **decision**

The dream is not treated as an ordinary psychological event within the narrative.

It is explicitly presented as a means of divine direction.

---

# 28.37 Peter's Vision in Acts 10

Acts 10 provides one of the most important New Testament visions.

Peter becomes hungry and falls into a trance.

He sees heaven opened and a vessel descending containing animals.

A voice instructs him.

Peter initially struggles with the meaning.

The experience is subsequently interpreted through the arrival of Cornelius's messengers and the Spirit's direction.

The narrative therefore combines:

> **vision**
>
> +
>
> **divine speech**
>
> +
>
> **interpretation**
>
> +
>
> **historical confirmation.**

---

# 28.38 Vision and Historical Confirmation

Acts 10 is especially valuable because Peter's understanding develops through subsequent events.

The vision occurs.

Then messengers arrive.

Peter goes with them.

The Holy Spirit falls upon Gentiles.

Peter recognizes the significance.

The meaning of the vision is therefore illuminated by historical events occurring after the vision.

The pattern is:

> **revelation**
>
> ↓
>
> **initial perception**
>
> ↓
>
> **interpretive clarification**
>
> ↓
>
> **historical confirmation**

This is an important biblical model for understanding revelatory experience.

---

# 28.39 Paul's Vision at Troas

Acts 16 records Paul's vision of a man of Macedonia saying:

> **“Come over into Macedonia, and help us.”**

Paul and his companions conclude that God had called them to preach the gospel there.

Again, the vision results in action.

It is not merely private religious experience.

The message directs missionary movement.

---

# 28.40 Paul's Night Vision at Corinth

Acts 18:9 records the Lord speaking to Paul in a night vision.

Paul is told:

> **“Be not afraid, but speak, and hold not thy peace.”**

The vision therefore communicates:

- reassurance;
- instruction;
- and encouragement.

Paul's response is continued ministry.

---

# 28.41 Paul's Vision of the Man of Macedonia and the Corinthian Vision

These examples show that New Testament visionary communication can be practical.

The revelation can answer:

> **Where should the messenger go?**

or:

> **Should the messenger continue speaking?**

The biblical category is therefore not limited to symbolic prophecy about distant future events.

God may communicate immediate direction.

---

# 28.42 Paul's Experience in Acts 22

Paul recounts an experience in which he says:

> **“when I was come again to Jerusalem, even while I prayed in the temple, I was in a trance.”**

He describes seeing the Lord and receiving instruction.

This is another example of a visionary experience occurring while Paul is awake.

It therefore demonstrates that the New Testament category of vision is broader than ordinary sleep dreams.

---

# 28.43 Paul's Vision in 2 Corinthians 12

Paul speaks of:

> **“visions and revelations of the Lord.”**

He describes being caught up to the third heaven and hearing unspeakable words.

Paul is deliberately restrained in discussing the experience.

This is important.

A genuine revelatory experience does not automatically become a license for uncontrolled speculation.

Paul does not build his ministry around sensational description.

He remains focused on the gospel and Christ.

---

# 28.44 John on Patmos

The Book of Revelation presents the most extensive New Testament visionary record.

John states:

> **“I was in the Spirit on the Lord's day.”**

He then receives a series of visions concerning Christ, the churches, heavenly realities, judgment, conflict, and the consummation.

The experience is repeatedly described in terms of:

> seeing;

> hearing;

> being shown;

> being told;

> and writing.

---

# 28.45 “Write What You See”

Revelation repeatedly commands John to write.

Revelation 1:11 says:

> **“What thou seest, write in a book.”**

This is one of the clearest biblical examples of the movement:

> **vision**
>
> ↓
>
> **written record**

The visionary experience becomes Scripture through written testimony.

The command to write is therefore central to the Book of Revelation.

---

# 28.46 Revelation as a Written Prophecy

Revelation 1:3 calls the book:

> **“the words of this prophecy.”**

This combines three categories:

> **revelation**
>
> **prophecy**
>
> **writing**

The book therefore provides an important model of how a visionary revelation becomes a written prophetic document.

---

# 28.47 Seeing and Writing

Revelation repeatedly uses the pattern:

> **I saw**

followed by:

> **I heard**

and:

> **I was told**

and:

> **write.**

This is not accidental literary decoration.

It is the narrative mechanism by which John communicates what he received.

The written record functions as the enduring testimony to the revelation.

---

# 28.48 The Vision Is Not the Interpretation

A recurring pattern across Scripture is:

> **revelation received**

followed by:

> **meaning explained.**

Daniel sees.

An angel explains.

Zechariah sees.

An angel explains.

Peter sees.

Historical events clarify.

John sees.

He is repeatedly told what particular symbols mean.

Therefore:

> **the recipient's experience and the interpretation of that experience are distinguishable stages.**

This distinction is crucial.

---

# 28.49 Interpretation May Be Given by God

Daniel repeatedly receives interpretation from heavenly messengers.

Joseph attributes dream interpretation to God.

Zechariah receives explanations through the angelic messenger.

Revelation includes angelic explanations.

These examples establish a principle:

> **when God gives symbolic revelation, He can also provide its interpretation.**

The recipient is not necessarily left to invent meaning.

---

# 28.50 Human Interpretation Is Not Automatically Divine Interpretation

This produces an equally important conclusion.

If a person receives an experience and then personally assigns meaning to it, the interpretation is not automatically divine.

The biblical record itself distinguishes:

> **what was shown**

from:

> **what it means.**

Therefore the interpreter must not claim divine authority merely because an experience occurred.

The meaning requires warrant.

---

# 28.51 False Dreams

The Bible explicitly warns about false dreams.

Deuteronomy 13 describes a prophet or dreamer who announces a sign or wonder and attempts to lead the people away from the LORD.

Jeremiah 23 condemns prophets who speak:

> **“a vision of their own heart.”**

The Bible therefore recognizes a category of:

> **self-generated visionary claim.**

This is crucial.

The biblical doctrine of revelation contains its own authentication problem.

---

# 28.52 “Their Own Heart”

Jeremiah 23:16 warns against prophets who:

> **“speak a vision of their own heart, and not out of the mouth of the LORD.”**

The distinction is precise:

> **vision from God**

versus:

> **vision from one's own heart.**

The experience may be sincerely reported.

But sincerity does not transform a humanly generated message into divine revelation.

---

# 28.53 False Dreams in Jeremiah 23

Jeremiah 23:25–32 speaks explicitly about prophets who say:

> **“I have dreamed, I have dreamed.”**

The problem is not the word “dream.”

The problem is claiming divine authority for a message that did not come from God.

The LORD contrasts His word with the dreams of those who prophesy falsely.

This establishes a critical rule:

> **the claim “I had a dream” is not equivalent to the claim “God spoke.”**

---

# 28.54 The Bible Tests the Claim

The biblical record therefore does not ask people to suspend judgment whenever someone claims a vision.

It provides criteria.

Deuteronomy 13 tests whether the message leads away from the LORD.

Deuteronomy 18 provides a test concerning prophetic claims and their fulfillment.

Jeremiah condemns visions that originate in the prophet's own heart.

The biblical system therefore contains:

> **revelation**

and:

> **authentication of claimed revelation.**

This distinction is essential for the broader architecture of the book.

---

# 28.55 A Genuine Revelation Is Not Created by Emotion

Emotion can accompany revelation.

Fear can accompany revelation.

Awe can accompany revelation.

Confusion can accompany revelation.

But none of these emotional responses creates revelation.

The source must remain God.

Therefore:

> **intensity of experience is not proof of divine origin.**

The Bible itself provides this principle by recording powerful experiences while simultaneously condemning false visionary claims.

---

# 28.56 A Genuine Revelation Is Not Created by Imagination

Likewise:

> imagination ≠ revelation.

A person can imagine a scene.

A person can construct a symbolic narrative.

A person can dream from ordinary psychological causes.

A person can misremember.

A person can interpret an experience incorrectly.

The biblical category of divine revelation requires something more:

> **God is the source of the communication.**

---

# 28.57 A Dream Can Be Ordinary

The Bible knows ordinary dreams.

Ecclesiastes discusses dreams in the context of ordinary human life.

Job uses dream imagery.

Psalm 73 uses dream language.

Isaiah 29 uses dream imagery in a context that does not turn every dream into revelation.

Therefore the Bible does not teach:

> **all dreams are divine messages.**

That proposition is inconsistent with the biblical record.

---

# 28.58 A Dream Can Be Revelatory

At the same time, Genesis, Daniel, Matthew, and other passages clearly present particular dreams as divinely given.

Therefore the opposite proposition is also false:

> **no dream can be divine revelation.**

The biblical evidence requires a third category:

> **some dreams are ordinary; some dreams are presented as divine communication.**

The distinction is established by context, source, message, interpretation, and biblical authentication.

---

# 28.59 The Same Principle Applies to Visions

Likewise:

> **vision ≠ automatically divine revelation.**

The Bible records false visions.

Ezekiel 13 condemns prophets who claim:

> **“They have seen vanity and lying divination.”**

The existence of visionary language therefore does not settle the source.

The biblical text itself insists on discrimination.

---

# 28.60 Divine Source Is the Decisive Category

The fundamental distinction is:

> **Where did the message originate?**

If it originates from God, it belongs to divine revelation.

If it originates in the human mind, it does not become divine revelation simply because it is presented in religious language.

If the source is uncertain, the claim remains unestablished.

This is why biblical revelation and biblical authentication must be studied together.

---

# 28.61 The Prophetic Office Does Not Remove the Need for Authentication

A person being called a prophet does not mean every statement attributed to that person is automatically true.

The Bible records false prophets.

Therefore the title itself is not sufficient.

The message must correspond to God's revelation and the biblical standard.

This is one reason the Bible's own warnings are so important.

---

# 28.62 Revelation and Covenant Fidelity

Deuteronomy 13 provides a particularly strong principle.

Even if a sign or wonder occurs, the people are not to follow a prophet who leads them after other gods.

The lesson is profound:

> **a supernatural-looking event does not override revealed truth.**

The message must remain faithful to God.

Therefore:

> **experience is subordinate to truth.**

This is a major epistemological principle.

---

# 28.63 Fulfillment as Authentication

Deuteronomy 18 also gives a criterion involving whether a prophetic word comes to pass.

This creates another pattern:

> **claimed revelation**
>
> ↓
>
> **historical prediction**
>
> ↓
>
> **historical outcome**
>
> ↓
>
> **authentication**

This does not mean every biblical revelation is predictive.

It means predictive revelation can contain an evidential test.

---

# 28.64 Vision, Prophecy, and History

The prophetic visions of Daniel, Isaiah, Ezekiel, Zechariah, and others frequently concern historical realities.

Kingdoms rise.

Kingdoms fall.

Judgment comes.

People are commanded.

Events occur.

The vision therefore connects the supernatural experience to history.

The prophetic claim can subsequently be investigated through the historical record where the relevant prediction is sufficiently specific.

This belongs to the broader evidential architecture of the book.

---

# 28.65 The Written Record Preserves the Revelation

Once a prophetic revelation is written, later readers are no longer dependent upon the prophet's continued presence.

Habakkuk is told to write the vision.

Daniel writes the dream.

John is repeatedly commanded to write what he sees.

The written record therefore performs a preservation function.

It carries the testimony of the revelation beyond the immediate moment of reception.

---

# 28.66 Writing Preserves Both Message and Context

A written prophetic record can preserve:

- the revelation;
- its imagery;
- the spoken interpretation;
- the prophetic response;
- historical setting;
- and sometimes the circumstances of reception.

This gives later readers a much more substantial object for examination than an isolated modern claim:

> “I had a vision.”

The biblical reader receives a recorded prophetic testimony embedded in a historical and literary context.

The process can therefore be described:

This is the central bridge from visionary experience to Scripture.

---

# 28.68 The Recipient Is a Witness

The prophet who receives a vision becomes a witness to what was shown.

This does not mean the prophet independently created the revelation.

The witness function is:

> **I received this from God.**

The prophetic role therefore resembles the apostolic role in one important respect:

> both involve testimony.

But the source and form of their testimony can differ.

---

# 28.69 Prophetic Witness and Apostolic Witness

The book can now distinguish:

### Prophetic witness

> **God reveals → prophet receives → prophet proclaims**

### Apostolic witness

> **Christ acts → apostle observes → apostle proclaims**

The categories overlap in their testimonial function but differ in the immediate mode of reception.

This distinction is important.

A prophet may testify to what God revealed.

An apostle may testify to what he witnessed in Christ's historical ministry.

Both can become part of the written record.

---

# 28.70 Christ as the Fulfillment of the Pattern

Hebrews 1:1–2 provides a decisive architectural transition:

> God spoke in many portions and many ways through the prophets, and has spoken in these last days by His Son.

The progression is:

> **many modes**
>
> ↓
>
> **prophets**
>
> ↓
>
> **Son**

The visions and dreams therefore belong within the earlier and continuing history of God's varied modes of communication.

Christ is not merely another dream or vision.

He is the Son through whom God has spoken.

---

# 28.71 Visions Point Beyond Themselves

A biblical vision is therefore not normally an end in itself.

Isaiah's vision reveals God and commissions Isaiah.

Daniel's visions reveal God's purposes concerning kingdoms and His kingdom.

Zechariah's visions communicate God's purposes for His people.

John's visions reveal Christ and the consummation of God's purposes.

The revelation points beyond the experience to the truth communicated.

---

# 28.72 The Experience Is Not the Object of Faith

This distinction is essential.

The biblical faith is not:

> **“I believe because someone had an extraordinary experience.”**

The object is God and what God has revealed.

The experience is the medium.

The message is the content.

The written testimony preserves the content.

Thus:

> **experience → message → testimony → written record**

The experience itself is not the final authority.

---

# 28.73 Why This Matters for Modern Claims

The biblical framework also provides a disciplined way to evaluate contemporary claims.

Suppose someone says:

> “I had a dream from God.”

The biblical response cannot simply be:

> “Dreams are impossible.”

Nor can it be:

> “Therefore God definitely spoke.”

The biblical process requires examination.

Questions include:

1. Does the message agree with what God has already revealed?
2. Does the claimant distinguish experience from interpretation?
3. Is the message actually identifiable?
4. Is there evidence of divine origin?
5. Does the claim contradict established revelation?
6. If it predicts something, does the prediction occur?
7. Is the claimant using the experience to establish authority over others?
8. Does the Bible provide a category or test relevant to the claim?

This is not skepticism toward Scripture.

It is the Bible's own discipline applied to a claim.

---

# 28.74 The Bible Does Not Teach Blind Acceptance of Revelatory Claims

The warnings against false prophets establish this.

A person cannot simply say:

> **“God told me.”**

and thereby make the statement true.

The Bible repeatedly distinguishes God's word from human religious speech.

This is why revelation must be authenticated.

---

# 28.75 Nor Does the Bible Teach Blind Rejection

The opposite error is also unwarranted.

Because false visions exist, it does not follow that genuine visions never occur.

The Bible itself records genuine revelations through dreams and visions.

Therefore the biblical posture is neither:

> **credulity**

nor:

> **automatic denial.**

It is:

> **discernment according to God's revealed standard.**

---

# 28.76 The Importance of Context

A dream in Genesis 37 cannot be interpreted independently of the Joseph narrative.

Daniel's visions cannot be understood without Daniel's historical and literary context.

Peter's vision in Acts 10 cannot be understood without the Cornelius narrative.

John's visions cannot be understood without the structure of Revelation.

Context therefore governs interpretation.

A symbolic image is not a free-floating object to which any desired meaning can be assigned.

---

# 28.77 Symbolism Does Not Mean Meaninglessness

Biblical visions frequently use symbols.

But symbols communicate.

A beast can represent a kingdom.

A lampstand can carry symbolic meaning.

A measuring line can signify something.

A scroll can signify judgment.

The presence of symbolism therefore does not make the vision arbitrary.

The biblical text frequently provides the interpretive key.

---

# 28.78 God Can Explain His Own Symbols

Daniel's angels explain symbols.

Zechariah receives explanations.

Revelation interprets several symbols.

This is important because it establishes a methodological principle:

> **where Scripture provides an interpretation, that interpretation governs the meaning.**

The interpreter should not replace God's stated meaning with private speculation.

---

# 28.79 Where Interpretation Is Not Explicit

Not every symbolic detail receives a separate explanation.

In such cases, interpretation must remain controlled.

The reader should examine:

- immediate context;
- wider biblical usage;
- repeated imagery;
- literary structure;
- historical setting;
- and explicit interpretive statements.

What should not happen is arbitrary symbolism.

The fact that a passage is visionary does not suspend rational interpretation.

---

# 28.80 Visionary Language Can Communicate Concrete Claims

A vision may use symbols while still communicating claims about real events.

Daniel's symbolic kingdoms concern actual historical powers.

Peter's symbolic animals concern a real historical question concerning Gentile inclusion.

John's visions concern real theological and eschatological claims.

Therefore:

> **symbolic form does not imply unreal referent.**

A symbol can communicate a claim about reality.

---

# 28.81 Dreams Can Communicate Concrete Instructions

Joseph's dreams in Matthew lead to concrete actions.

The Magi's dream changes their route.

Paul's night vision redirects missionary movement.

The dream is therefore not merely metaphorical.

A revelatory dream can communicate actionable information.

---

# 28.82 The Revelation May Be Immediate or Future-Oriented

The Bible records dreams and visions concerning:

- immediate action;
- personal direction;
- current judgment;
- national events;
- future kingdoms;
- messianic expectations;
- and final consummation.

Therefore the temporal range is broad.

The important question is not whether the revelation concerns the present or future.

The important question is whether God is the source and what the revelation actually communicates.

---

# 28.83 Visions and Dreams Are Subordinate to God

A recurring principle is:

> **God is the source; the experience is the instrument.**

The dream does not become God.

The vision does not become God.

The prophet does not become God.

The interpreter does not become God.

All remain subordinate to the divine source.

This protects the doctrine of revelation from turning experiences into objects of worship or independent authority.

---

# 28.84 Visions and Dreams Do Not Create New Gods

Deuteronomy 13's warning is decisive.

Even extraordinary phenomena cannot authorize departure from the LORD.

The standard is covenantal truth.

Therefore:

> **a claimed revelation cannot overthrow the God who is already revealed.**

The Bible's doctrine of revelation is internally ordered.

Later claims cannot simply invalidate the established identity of God.

---

# 28.85 The Relationship Between Revelation and Scripture

A crucial distinction must now be made.

The Bible records many revelations.

But not every revelation experienced by every biblical person is preserved as a separate biblical book.

Some dreams are narrated.

Some visions are summarized.

Some prophetic words are recorded.

Some are referred to only briefly.

Therefore:

> **revelation and Scripture are related but not numerically identical categories.**

The doctrine of inspiration concerns the authoritative written Scripture.

This chapter concerns one important means by which revelation can be received.

---

# 28.86 Not Every Divine Communication Is a Book

God may give a person a specific instruction without that instruction becoming a new book of Scripture.

Joseph is directed to flee.

Paul is directed to remain in Corinth.

Peter is directed to go with the messengers.

These communications serve their immediate divine purpose.

The biblical record then preserves selected testimony concerning them.

Therefore the movement from:

> **revelation**

to:

> **Scripture**

is not automatic in every individual instance.

It is governed by God's purpose in the written record.

# 28.87 The Graphē Receives the Written Testimony

When a prophet is commanded:

Nor does the reader need to have stood on Patmos to investigate John's written record.

The relevant epistemological question becomes:

The Bible's revelatory structure is cumulative.

Daniel's visions build upon themes of kingdoms and God's rule.

The New Testament writers read earlier revelation in light of Christ.

The recorded communication through which the revelation is preserved for others.

# 28.101 The Chapter's Necessary Conclusion

The Bible presents visions and dreams as genuine means through which God can communicate revelation.

It does not, however, identify every dream or vision as divine.

Instead, the biblical record establishes a disciplined distinction:

> **ordinary human experience**

versus:

> **divinely given revelation.**

Where revelation occurs through a dream or vision, the recipient may receive the experience without immediately understanding its meaning. The Bible therefore distinguishes **reception from interpretation**, and it repeatedly shows God providing or authorizing interpretation.

The Bible also explicitly recognizes false dreams, false visions, and messages arising from human imagination or the prophet's own heart. Consequently, the claim that an experience came from God cannot be established merely by the intensity, unusualness, or religious character of the experience.

>
> ↓
>
> **the written record becomes available to later generations**

Visions and dreams therefore occupy a genuine and identifiable place within the Bible's doctrine of divine communication, while remaining subordinate to God as the source and to the truth of His revelation as the standard.

- **The Bible explicitly presents visions and dreams as means through which God can communicate.**

A noun associated with seeing, appearance, or vision. In Numbers 12:6 it is used for the revelatory vision through which God makes Himself known to the prophet.

### חֲלוֹם — *ḥălôm*

The ordinary Hebrew noun for “dream.” Its occurrences include ordinary dreams and dreams presented in Scripture as carrying prophetic significance.

### חָזוֹן — *ḥāzôn*

A Hebrew term associated with vision and prophetic revelation, particularly prominent in prophetic literature.

### מַחֲזֶה — *maḥăzeh*

A Hebrew term for vision or visionary experience, occurring in contexts of revelation.

## Greek

### ὅραμα — *horama*

A term used for a vision or something seen, including visionary experiences in Acts and other New Testament contexts.

### ὅρασις — *horasis*

A term associated with sight, appearance, or vision.

### ὀπτασία — *optasia*

A term used for a vision or appearance, including Paul's reference to visions and revelations.

### ὄναρ — *onar*

The Greek term for “dream,” particularly visible in Matthew's infancy narrative and the account concerning Pilate's wife.

Lexical evidence establishes the language of the biblical categories; the theological determination that a particular dream or vision is divine must come from the passage's context, source attribution, message, interpretation, and canonical testimony.

# Methodological Guardrails

### 1. Do not equate every dream with revelation.

The Bible records ordinary dreams as well as revelatory dreams.

### 2. Do not equate every vision with divine revelation.

The Bible explicitly condemns false visions.

### 3. Do not derive theology from etymology alone.

Word origins and lexical relationships illuminate biblical language but do not independently establish doctrine.

### 4. Distinguish reception from interpretation.

Seeing or dreaming something does not necessarily mean the recipient immediately understands its meaning.

### 5. Distinguish revelation from Scripture.

Not every divine communication recorded in Scripture constitutes a separate canonical book or new body of Scripture.

### 6. Distinguish experience from authentication.

The intensity of an experience cannot by itself establish divine origin.

### 7. Preserve the biblical testing principle.

A claimed revelation must conform to God's truth and, where applicable, withstand historical or prophetic testing.

### 8. Preserve the divine-human structure.

God is the source; human recipients genuinely receive, perceive, communicate, interpret, and write.

### 9. Do not make symbolism arbitrary.

Where Scripture supplies an interpretation, that interpretation governs.

### 10. Do not make later interpretation replace the biblical record.

The task is to understand what the biblical text presents before constructing theological systems around it.

---

# Final Doctrinal Statement

> **The Bible presents visions and dreams as genuine means through which God can communicate revelation. Numbers 12:6 explicitly identifies vision and dream as modes by which God makes Himself known to prophets. The biblical record, however, distinguishes genuine divine communication from ordinary dreams, human imagination, false visions, and deceptive prophetic claims. Joseph, Daniel, Isaiah, Ezekiel, Amos, Zechariah, Peter, Paul, and John demonstrate that revelation may be received through dreams or visions, that its meaning may require interpretation, and that God may provide or authorize that interpretation. The Bible further demonstrates that revelatory experience can become prophetic testimony and, where God directs the message into written form, part of the written record. Visions and dreams therefore constitute a genuine pathway of divine communication within the Doctrine of the Graphē, while remaining subordinate to God as the source, truth as the standard, interpretation as a distinct task, and Scripture as the written record through which the revelation is preserved for later generations.**
