# 59.3 Define the Question: John 10:34–36 in Argumentative Context

**John 10:34–36 in argumentative context**, in this chapter, is the complete three-verse answer Christ gives to the blasphemy-charge in the Dedication controversy of John 10:22–39, read as an argument from what is written, not as an isolated clause, not as a later slogan, and not as the whole of Movement VIII.

The phrase is easily abused. In ordinary speech “John 10:35” may mean a proof-text for unbreakable Scripture, a curiosity about the word “gods,” a weapon in later debates about deity, or a license to treat every later Graphē-doctrine as already settled because three verses exist. None of those senses is this chapter’s object.

Four elements belong to the definition.

First, **the complete passage**. The unit is verses 34, 35, and 36 together. Verse 34 asks whether it is written and cites the wording. Verse 35 names those to whom the word of God came, and states that the scripture cannot be broken. Verse 36 applies the written case to the present charge against Him whom the Father sanctified and sent. A fragment is not the passage. “Cannot be broken” without the citation is not the passage. “Ye are gods” without the charge is not the passage.

Second, **in its argumentative context**. The three verses are an answer. An answer has a question, a charge, a setting, and neighbouring appeals. The Dedication scene supplies those: the demand for plain speech, the works as witness, the claim of oneness with the Father, the taking up of stones, the named blasphemy, and the return to works after the Scripture-appeal. Context is not ornament. Context is what makes the three verses an argument rather than a slogan.

Third, **as the opening of Christ’s verdict on the Graphē**. The object of this movement is His verdict upon the written Holy Writ. The opening of that verdict is this: He treats what is written as the decisive court in a capital religious charge. Opening is not completion. The later files will examine the cited psalm, the reasoning, the statement that Scripture cannot be broken, the why of that statement, the relation of Word and truth, and the Graphē as primary written authority.

Fourth, **not those other claims**. The definition excludes, as not this object: finished Psalm 82 exposition; the dedicated reasoning-file; the finished sense of “broken”; the dedicated why; John 17:17 as finished Graphē-doctrine; the Graphē-gate; a retry of Chapter 45’s works-argument; a retry of Chapter 48’s confirmation-file; a retry of Chapter 58’s synthesis. Those exclusions are part of the definition. They are not a later apology.

Thus:

> **The question of this chapter is not whether Scripture is the written Word, whether Christ confirmed received Scripture, or why Scripture cannot be broken. Those questions have been examined or remain later. The question is what John 10:34–36, as a complete argument in its controversy, establishes concerning Christ’s appeal to the Graphē as decisive written Word.**

# 59.7 The Passage Defined as a Unit of Argument

A passage, in this investigation, is not a numbered interval on a later page. It is a unit of speech with a beginning, a middle, and an end, performing a stated work in a stated dispute.

John 10:34–36 is such a unit. It begins with Jesus answering them. The “them” are those who have taken up stones and named blasphemy. It proceeds through a question about what is written, a citation of written wording, a characterization of those to whom the word of God came, a statement about the scripture, and an application to the present charge. It ends when the application has been put: do you say of Him whom the Father sanctified and sent, Thou blasphemest, because I said, I am the Son of God?

The unit is therefore an answer, not a motto. It has a question-form: “Is it not written…?” It has a cited wording: “I said, Ye are gods.” It has a condition: “If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came.” It has a parenthetical of force: “and the scripture cannot be broken.” It has a charge reversed: “Say ye of him… Thou blasphemest.” It has a because-clause: “because I said, I am the Son of God.”

To isolate any one of those members is to destroy the unit. The citation without the charge is a lemma. The charge without the citation is an assertion. The parenthetical without the citation is a slogan. The because-clause without the written wording is a claim without the court to which He has appealed.

The later English verse-numbers are useful as locators. They are not the definition of the unit. The unit is Christ’s answer. The locators name where that answer stands in the received chapter.

Thus:

> **John 10:34–36 is a unit of argument: question, citation, characterization, statement of the writing’s standing, and application to the blasphemy-charge. A fragment of the unit is not the passage.**

# 59.8 Argumentative Context Defined

**Argumentative context**, in this chapter, is the dispute that makes the unit an answer: speaker, audience, occasion, preceding claim, named accusation, neighbouring appeals, and the work the answer is performing.

The speaker is Jesus. The audience is “the Jews” of the scene — those who have asked for plain speech, have not believed, have taken up stones, and have named blasphemy. The occasion, as the record states it, is Jerusalem, the feast of the dedication, winter, the Temple, Solomon’s porch (John 10:22–23). The preceding claim is “I and my Father are one” (John 10:30), after works offered as witness (John 10:25). The named accusation is blasphemy: “because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God” (John 10:33). The neighbouring appeals are the works before the Scripture-answer and the works after it (John 10:25, 32, 37–38). The work the answer is performing is to force the charge onto the ground of their own received writing.

Context so defined is not a historical novel. It is what the record itself supplies as the scene of the speech. The investigation does not invent the mood of the crowd, the precise architectural reconstruction of the porch, or unverifiable details of festival practice beyond the record’s naming of the feast, the season, the place, and the acts.

Two false substitutes must be refused.

**Immediate verbal neighbourhood without the charge.** One may quote verses 34–36 and call the neighbouring verses “context” while still treating the unit as a slogan about Scripture in general. Neighbourhood of words is not yet argumentative context. Argumentative context is the dispute.

**Historical colour without the argument.** One may describe winter and a porch and a feast and still leave the charge unexamined. Colour is not context. The feast, the porch, and the season locate the scene. The charge makes the scene a controversy. The controversy makes the three verses an argument.

Thus:

> **Argumentative context is the Dedication controversy in which Christ answers a blasphemy-charge by appealing to what is written. It is not a slogan’s caption, and it is not invented historical colour.**

# 59.9 Appeal to What Is Written Defined

**Appeal to what is written**, in this chapter, is Christ’s act of treating a received writing as the decisive court of the dispute: asking whether it is written, citing the written wording, and reasoning from that wording rather than from mere assertion of His own opinion.

The definition has four elements.

First, **a received writing**. He says “written in your law.” The writing is not a private oracle invented for the occasion. It is their own received Scripture. The appeal works only if both parties already treat the writing as standing. A man does not ask “Is it not written in your law?” if the law is in dispute as law. The dispute is not whether the writing is Scripture. The dispute is what follows from the writing for the present charge.

Second, **as written**. The force is the written character of the wording. He does not say, “Have you not heard a rumour?” He does not say, “I now reveal a new sentence.” He says it is written. The Graphē’s standing as writing, already established in Chapters 20–22 and joined in Chapter 58, is the category He uses.

Third, **as the court of the dispute**. Decisive, here, does not mean that the later Graphē-gate has been drawn. It means that in this controversy He places the charge under the writing. The opponents have named blasphemy. He names what is written. The writing is not illustration. It is the ground to which He moves the argument.

Fourth, **not mere opinion**. Opinion is a speaker’s view, revisable as view, possessing whatever force the speaker’s prestige supplies. An appeal to what is written treats the wording as already standing, independent of the present speaker’s preference. He could have answered, “I am the Son of God; accept it.” He answers, “Is it not written…?” The form is the refusal of mere opinion.

Thus:

> **Appeal to what is written is the act of placing the dispute under a received writing’s wording. It is not a new oracle, not an illustration, and not the speaker’s mere opinion.**

# 59.10 Christ's Verdict on the Graphē: Opening, Not Completion

Movement VIII is titled **Christ’s Verdict on the Graphē**. A verdict is a judgment. The Graphē is the written Holy Writ already identified. Christ’s verdict is His judgment upon that writing’s standing and force.

The movement as a whole will include the citation, the reasoning, the statement that the scripture cannot be broken, the why of that statement, the identification of Word and truth, and the conclusion that the Graphē is the primary written authority. Those are seven files. This is the first.

The opening of a verdict is not a weak form of the later files. It is the act without which the later files would have no controversy to examine. The opening is this: in a capital charge concerning God, the Son does not treat Scripture as optional decoration. He treats it as the writing whose wording governs the argument. That is already a verdict upon the Graphē: it stands as the court. Later files will say what that standing includes, why it cannot be broken, and what follows for primary written authority. They will not create the court. They will examine the court He has already entered.

The Bible Introduction’s observation that John 10:35 belongs among New Testament texts showing that the received Old Testament writings were not, in that scene, an open controversy about identity of corpus, is reconstructible without treating that observation as a second canon-file. The appeal functions because the writing’s identity as Scripture is common ground. Chapter 52 already examined recognition. This chapter uses that common ground as the condition of the appeal. It does not retry how a corpus is recognized.

The Aletheia material’s identification of John 10:35’s γραφή as Holy Writ — writing of standing authority — is likewise reconstructible without treating a leaflet as a premise. The passage itself names ἡ γραφή. Biblical usage already established in Chapters 20–22 identifies that naming as Scripture. The present chapter’s contribution is not a new lexicon. It is the location of that named writing as the object to which Christ appeals in this dispute.

Thus:

> **Christ’s verdict on the Graphē opens here: He treats the written Scripture as the decisive court of the blasphemy-dispute. Opening is not the finished why, and it is not the Graphē-gate.**

# 59.11 Lexical Control: γεγραμμένον, νόμος, γραφή

The passage’s writing-terms must be controlled, not inflated.

**γεγραμμένον** is the perfect passive participle of γράφω (*graphō*, G1125): having been written, standing written. John 10:34: οὐκ ἔστιν γεγραμμένον ἐν τῷ νόμῳ ὑμῶν — “Is it not written in your law.” The perfect marks a completed writing that remains in force as written. The question is not whether a sentence might be composed. The question is whether a writing already exists in their law.

**νόμος** (*nomos*, G3551) is law. In many biblical uses it designates the Mosaic Law, the Torah. In this verse Christ cites wording that is not a sentence of the Pentateuch. He calls that wording written in “your law.” The usage is not an error to be repaired by a later theologian. It is the passage’s own designation: the received Scripture, as their law, includes this written wording. John 15:25 exhibits a related usage: “that the word might be fulfilled that is written in their law,” citing a psalm. The present chapter needs only this: “your law” here names the received written Scripture to which He appeals, not a private code, and not only a Pentateuch isolated from the Writings. The precise relation of Law, Prophets, and Psalms as a received body remains Chapter 48’s already-established confirmation, used rather than retried.

**γραφή** (*graphē*, G1124) appears in verse 35: ἡ γραφή. Chapters 20–22 established the lexical field as writing, and the biblical usage as Holy Writ / Scripture. The articular singular here names the Scripture as the written thing whose standing is asserted in the parenthetical. The term is not “the Bible” as a later English nickname. It is the writing. In this argument the writing is the cited Scripture, treated as Scripture.

Three refusals follow.

The terms do not, by themselves, finish “cannot be broken.” A named writing may be appealed to. Why it cannot be broken remains later.

The terms do not, by themselves, finish the Graphē-gate. A named writing may be the court of one dispute. Primary written authority in the Movement VIII sense remains Chapter 65.

The terms do not license a claim that every later use of “law” means the whole corpus. Context controls. Here the context is a cited psalm called written in their law.

Thus:

> **The passage’s writing-terms name a received written Scripture already standing: γεγραμμένον, νόμος as their law including this wording, γραφή as the Scripture. Lexical control is not later doctrine under another name.**

# 59.12 Lexical Control of Neighbouring Terms Named, Not Exhausted

Other terms in the unit must be named accurately and left, where the TOC requires, to later files.

**βλασφημεῖς** is from βλασφημέω (*blasphēmeō*, G987): to blaspheme, to speak injuriously of the divine. The opponents’ charge in verse 33 is blasphemy, because, being a man, He makes Himself God. Verse 36 quotes their speech: “Thou blasphemest.” The term locates the dispute as a charge concerning God, not as a literary seminar about a psalm. The later systematic analysis of blasphemy as a legal category is not this chapter’s object. The record’s own naming is sufficient: they charge blasphemy; He answers from what is written.

**ἁγιάζω** (*hagiazō*, G37) in verse 36: “whom the Father hath sanctified.” The verb’s field includes setting apart, consecrating. He names Himself as the one the Father set apart and sent into the world. The term belongs to the application. It does not, here, become a finished consecration-doctrine.

**ἀποστέλλω** (*apostellō*, G649): “sent into the world.” Sending has already been examined as a claim-class in Chapter 45 and as apostolic sending in Chapters 49–50. Here it is Christ’s identification of Himself in the application: the Father sanctified and sent Him. The term is used, not retried as the apostolic-office file.

**λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ** in verse 35: “the word of God” came to those who were called gods. The phrase belongs to the characterization of the cited case. Its precise force in relation to Psalm 82 remains Chapter 60 and Chapter 61. This chapter names the phrase as part of the unit.

**λυθῆναι** is the aorist passive infinitive of λύω (*lyō*, G3089): to loose, undo, dissolve, break. “Cannot be broken” renders οὐ δύναται λυθῆναι ἡ γραφή. The verb will be the object of Chapters 62–63. This chapter records that the unit contains the statement. It does not define “broken.” It does not answer why. Naming the Greek form is lexical honesty. Exhausting it would be theft of the later files.

Thus:

> **Neighbouring terms are named so the unit is not mistranslated. Blasphemy locates the charge. Sanctified and sent locate the application. “Broken” is quoted as belonging to the unit and reserved as a dedicated object.**

# 59.13 The Setting of John 10: Feast, Temple, Demand

The record locates the scene before it locates the answer.

> **“And it was at Jerusalem the feast of the dedication, and it was winter. And Jesus walked in the temple in Solomon’s porch.”**
> — John 10:22–23

The feast, the season, the city, the Temple, and the porch are the record’s own locators. The investigation takes them as locators. It does not reconstruct an unwritten liturgy, invent a crowd-count, or supply architectural measurements the text does not give. The Dedication is named. Winter is named. Solomon’s porch is named. Those names suffice to show that the controversy is public, in the Temple, at a feast, not a private aside.

Then the demand:

> **“Then came the Jews round about him, and said unto him, How long dost thou make us to doubt? If thou be the Christ, tell us plainly.”**
> — John 10:24

The demand is for plain speech concerning messianic identity. It is not, at this moment, a demand for a lecture on Psalm 82. The Scripture-argument will be His answer to the charge that follows His plainness, not a change of subject invented by a later preacher.

He answers first by recalling prior speech and by naming the works:

> **“I told you, and ye believed not: the works that I do in my Father’s name, they bear witness of me.”**
> — John 10:25

The works-argument is already in force from Chapter 45. It is used here as the first member of the scene, not as a second works-file.

The sheep-sayings of John 10:26–29 belong to the same answer: they believe not because they are not of His sheep; His sheep hear; He gives them eternal life; no man snatches them from His hand or from the Father’s hand. Those sayings interpret unbelief. They are not this chapter’s object. They are named as belonging to the scene so that verse 30 is not torn from its place.

Then the sentence that prompts the stones:

> **“I and my Father are one.”**
> — John 10:30

The setting is therefore not a classroom. It is a public Temple controversy, at a named feast, begun by a demand for plain messianic speech, answered by works as witness and by a claim of oneness with the Father.

John 10:1–21, the Good Shepherd discourse, stands earlier in the received chapter. The Dedication scene begins at verse 22. The investigation does not treat the two as one indistinguishable occasion. It also does not pretend they are unrelated writings. They stand in one Gospel testimony. The Scripture-argument belongs to the Dedication controversy. That is the context required here.

Thus:

> **The record locates John 10:34–36 in a public Temple controversy at the feast of the dedication: a demand for plain speech, works as witness, and the claim of oneness with the Father. Locators are taken from the text, not invented.**

# 59.14 The Works-Argument Already Established, Used Not Retried

Chapter 45 established that in this discourse the works in the Father’s name bear witness of Him; that oneness with the Father is claimed; and that the works are offered as the path to knowing that the Father is in Him and He in the Father (John 10:25, 30, 37–38). That file remains in force.

The present chapter needs the works-argument as a member of the context, not as its object.

Without the works, the Scripture-appeal would appear as a sudden leap from identity-claim to psalm. With the works, the scene has already offered public authentication. He has not refused evidence. He has named the works as witness. The opponents do not answer the works with a counter-catalogue. They take up stones after the oneness-claim. When He asks for which work they stone Him, they distinguish: not for a good work, but for blasphemy.

The works therefore do two jobs in this file, and only two.

First, they show that the controversy is not a word-game detached from public acts. The same discourse that will appeal to Scripture has already appealed to works.

Second, they show that the Scripture-argument is not a substitute invented because works failed. After verses 34–36 He returns to the works (John 10:37–38). Scripture and works are neighbouring appeals in one dispute. They are not rivals. They are not identical.

What this section does not do is rewrite Chapter 45. It does not recatalogue healings. It does not retry Mark 2, Matthew 11, or John 5. It does not treat John 10:37–38 as a new authentication-axis. Those remain established. The new work is to locate the works as the context that makes the Scripture-appeal an appeal in a dispute already public, not a detached lemma.

Thus:

> **The works-argument of John 10 remains Chapter 45’s object, used here as context. The Scripture-argument is this chapter’s object. Same discourse; two files.**

# 59.15 “I and My Father Are One” as the Claim That Prompts the Charge

The sentence that turns the demand into a capital charge is not a later creed. It is John 10:30.

> **“I and my Father are one.”**

Chapter 45 already classified this among the public claims of unique relation to the Father. Chapter 44 already identified Him as the Truth and the revealer of the Father. Those conclusions remain premises. The present section’s job is narrower: to show that this claim is what the opponents treat as the ground of blasphemy, and therefore what the Scripture-argument is answering.

The narrator’s next sentence is not commentary invented by a later church. It is the record of an act:

> **“Then the Jews took up stones again to stone him.”**
> — John 10:31

“Again” points to prior attempts in the Gospel’s testimony (John 8:59 is the neighbouring prior act of taking up stones). The investigation does not build a novel of repeated riots. It notes what the word “again” itself marks: this is not the first such act in the testimony.

He answers the stones by returning to works:

> **“Many good works have I shewed you from my Father; for which of those works do ye stone me?”**
> — John 10:32

They refuse that framing:

> **“For a good work we stone thee not; but for blasphemy; and because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God.”**
> — John 10:33

The opponents’ interpretation of verse 30 is therefore explicit. Oneness with the Father, spoken by a man, is received as making Himself God. They name the height of the claim. As in John 5:18, where the narrator states that He said God was His Father, making Himself equal with God, the opposition is not portrayed as failing to hear. It is portrayed as hearing a claim it judges blasphemous.

The Scripture-argument of verses 34–36 is the answer to that judgment. If the investigation treats verse 30 as a later metaphysical slogan, it will not understand why a psalm is cited. If it treats verse 30 as the whole of the Graphē-verdict, it will not understand why He does not rest in the claim but moves to what is written. The claim prompts the charge. The charge prompts the appeal. The appeal is this chapter’s object.

Thus:

> **“I and my Father are one” is the claim the opponents treat as making Himself God. The Scripture-argument answers that charge. The claim is context; the appeal is the assignment.**

# 59.16 The Accusation of Blasphemy

Blasphemy, in this scene, is not a vague insult. It is the named charge on which they take up stones.

The elements of the charge, as they state it, are three.

**A good work is not the ground.** They distinguish work from speech. The distinction is their own. It prevents a later reader from saying they stoned Him for healing. They say they do not.

**The ground is blasphemy.** They use the word. The dispute is located in speech concerning God.

**The specification is: being a man, He makes Himself God.** The charge is not that He performed a work. The charge is that a man has claimed what belongs to God. Verse 30 is the speech they have in view. Verse 36 will show that they also hear “I am the Son of God” as the same offence.

The accusation therefore has a logic. If God alone is God, and if a man claims oneness with the Father in the sense they hear, then the speech is blasphemy. The logic is not this chapter’s later Christology. It is the opponents’ stated reason for stones.

Two errors must be refused.

**Dismissal of the charge as a misunderstanding too trivial to answer.** He does not treat the charge as trivial. He answers it. An answer grants that the charge, as a charge, requires a reply.

**Inflation of the charge into a finished doctrine of the incarnation as this file’s object.** The charge is the context of the Graphē-appeal. The later full treatment of His person is not this chapter. Chapters 44–46 have already examined identity, claims, and the hinge of death and raising. This file uses those as premises. It does not retry them under the name of blasphemy.

The accusation matters for the Graphē because of what He does with it. He does not flee the Temple. He does not answer with a new private revelation unconnected to their writings. He answers with their law. The court He chooses is the writing they already have.

Thus:

> **The accusation is blasphemy: a man making Himself God. It is the charge that makes verses 34–36 an answer, not a detached lecture.**

# 59.17 Stones, Charge, and the Turn to Scripture

The movement from stones to writing is the hinge of the scene.

Stones are an act. The charge is a sentence. The turn to Scripture is another sentence. The three are not the same.

The act shows the seriousness of the dispute. This is not a polite seminar. They take up stones.

The charge shows the issue. The issue is God, a man, and claimed oneness.

The turn shows the court. Jesus answered them. The answer begins: “Is it not written in your law?”

The form of the turn is a question. A question of this kind is not ignorance. It is a forensic question. It places the hearers under a writing they themselves receive. If it is written, the writing is in force as written. If it is in their law, they cannot dismiss it as His invention. If the wording is “I said, Ye are gods,” then a written sentence of their own Scripture has already used that language of those to whom the word of God came.

The hinge is therefore this:

> **The dispute is moved from stones as the opponents’ court to Scripture as Christ’s court.**

That movement is the opening of the verdict on the Graphē. A man who, under stones, appeals to what is written, has judged the writing more decisive than the stones. He has also judged the writing more decisive than a mere restatement of His claim. He has a claim. He has works. He still appeals to the writing.

The turn is not a retreat. A retreat would abandon the claim. He will apply the writing to the claim: the Father sanctified and sent Him; He said, I am the Son of God. The writing is brought to the claim. The claim is not abandoned for the writing. The writing is not abandoned for the claim.

Thus:

> **The hinge of the scene is the turn from stones and a blasphemy-charge to what is written in their law. That turn is the opening of Christ’s verdict on the Graphē.**

# 59.18 The Complete Quotation: John 10:34–36

The passage must be given as a complete argument, not as a proof-text fragment.

> **“Jesus answered them, Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods? If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came, and the scripture cannot be broken; Say ye of him, whom the Father hath sanctified, and sent into the world, Thou blasphemest; because I said, I am the Son of God?”**
> — John 10:34–36

The three verses are one answer. The first sentence is a question about what is written, containing a citation. The second sentence is a condition and a statement about the scripture. The third sentence is an application in the form of a question about their charge.

The argumentative force of the unit, at this stage, can be stated without stealing later files.

He answers. He does not ignore the charge.

He appeals to writing. He does not appeal to a new private sentence.

He locates the writing in their law. He does not locate it in a foreign corpus.

He cites wording. He does not cite a vague theme.

He characterizes the persons of the citation as those to whom the word of God came. He does not leave “gods” as an unexplained shock.

He states that the scripture cannot be broken. The statement belongs to the unit. Its dedicated sense remains later.

He applies the case to Himself as the one the Father sanctified and sent. He does not leave the citation unused.

He restates their charge and His own sentence: “I am the Son of God.” He does not hide the claim behind the psalm.

That is the complete passage in full argumentative force. A later preacher may quote only “the scripture cannot be broken.” A later controversy may quote only “Ye are gods.” Neither quotation is the argument. The argument is the answer as given.

Thus:

> **The complete passage is John 10:34–36 as one answer: written law, cited wording, characterization, statement of the writing’s standing, application to the sanctified and sent Son, and the restated charge. Fragmentation is not exposition.**

# 59.19 Verse 34: “Is It Not Written in Your Law”

Verse 34 is the opening move of the answer.

> **“Jesus answered them, Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?”**

Three features of the move must be seen.

**It is an answer.** “Jesus answered them” is not a narrator’s filler. The speech is a reply to verse 33. Any reading that treats verse 34 as a sudden new topic has already lost the argument.

**It is a question about writing.** “Is it not written” expects the answer yes. The form is the form of forcing an admission. He does not ask whether they like the sentence. He asks whether it is written. The category is the Graphē’s category: writing.

**It is located in their law.** “Your law” is not a sneer that the writing is merely theirs and not His. It is a placement: the writing they already acknowledge as law. The appeal binds them by their own received Scripture. If they deny that it is written, they deny their law. If they admit that it is written, they admit a wording that His argument will use.

The cited wording inside the question is “I said, Ye are gods.” The wording will be identified as Psalm 82:6 in the next sections. Verse 34 itself presents it as written in their law. The identification of the source-text is required so that “written” is not an empty claim. The exposition of that source-text is Chapter 60.

The verse, taken alone, already establishes the form of the verdict’s opening: the first word of the answer to blasphemy is a question about what is written. That fact does not finish the later why. It does finish the claim that He argues from the written Word’s standing rather than from mere opinion.

Thus:

> **Verse 34 opens the answer by forcing the dispute onto a received writing: Is it not written in your law? The form is the appeal. The cited wording is the material of the appeal.**

# 59.20 “Your Law” as Their Own Received Writing

“Your law” requires a precise sense, neither too narrow nor too broad.

Too narrow: “law” must mean only the five books of Moses, and therefore a psalm cannot be in view, and therefore Christ has mis-cited. The passage itself forbids this. He cites this wording as written in their law. The usage of νόμος for received Scripture, already noted, includes this case. Chapter 48 already showed that He receives Law, Prophets, and Psalms as the Scriptures. A psalm called “your law” is not a contradiction of that confirmation. It is an instance of treating the received writings as the law they already have.

Too broad: “your law” means whatever later community custom they happen to prefer, including oral tradition as equal to the writing. The verse says γεγραμμένον — written. Mark 7, already available as a distinction in the book’s later mishandling-files and as a known contrast in His practice, distinguishes commandment of God from tradition of men. This chapter does not write Chapter 69. It notes that the appeal here is to what is written, not to an unwritten custom.

The possessive “your” has a forensic function. It is the law they claim. It is the writing they cannot disown without disowning their own standing as hearers of Scripture. He does not say “my new law.” He says “your law.” The Graphē already in their hands is the court.

This is why the appeal is decisive in the dispute as a dispute between Him and them. Decisive here means: they are not in a position to say, “That writing is irrelevant because it is yours, not ours.” The writing is theirs. The later conclusion that the Graphē is the primary written authority for all later testing remains Chapter 65. The present point is narrower and already full: in this dispute, the received writing is the common court, and He is the one who opens it.

Thus:

> **“Your law” names the received written Scripture they already acknowledge. The possessive binds them to the writing. It does not reduce the writing to custom, and it does not finish the Graphē-gate.**

# 59.21 The Cited Wording Named: Psalm 82:6

The wording He cites — “I said, Ye are gods” — is the wording of Psalm 82:6.

> **“I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.”**
> — Psalm 82:6

The identification is required by the investigation’s own rule: a citation must be located. Location is not exposition.

What this chapter may say, with full force, is limited and real.

The cited text is a writing already standing among the Psalms. It is not a sentence composed in the Temple on that winter day. It is not an oral proverb of uncertain origin. It is Scripture He treats as written in their law.

The cited line includes “I have said” / “I said,” and “Ye are gods.” Verse 35 will speak of His having “called them gods.” The wording is the material. Chapter 60 will examine why this wording, in this psalm, in this psalm’s own context, is the wording He uses.

Psalm 82 as a whole addresses a divine assembly, judges, and the Most High. That context is Chapter 60’s object. This chapter names the psalm so that the reader knows which writing is cited, and so that “Ye are gods” is not left as a floating shock.

Two failures of identification must be refused.

**Refusal to name the psalm**, as if naming it were already exposition. Naming is the minimum of honesty about a citation.

**Exposition of the psalm here**, as if this chapter were Chapter 60. The judges of Psalm 82, the meaning of אֱלֹהִים (*elohim*) in that psalm, the psalm’s indictment, and the precise correspondence of each clause to Christ’s use, remain the next file.

The Bible Introduction’s larger argument that Christ and the apostles treat the received writings as already Scripture is used here only as already-established correspondence: He cites a psalm as written law. Correspondence is not a second confirmation-trial.

Thus:

> **The cited wording is Psalm 82:6. The psalm is named so the citation is located. The psalm’s own context and the reason He uses this wording remain Chapter 60.**

# 59.22 Why the Citation Is Named Here and Examined in Chapter 60

A citation has two questions.

The first: **which writing is cited?** That question belongs to the complete passage. If the passage cites wording, the wording’s location in the received Scriptures is part of giving the actual quotation in full argumentative force. An unidentified quotation is a fragment.

The second: **what is that writing in its own context, and why is this the wording He uses?** That question is the locked assignment of Chapter 60: examine the actual citation and its Psalm 82 context; reason through why Christ uses the written wording.

The two questions are neighbours. They are not the same assignment. Neighbourhood is why the psalm must be named here. Difference of assignment is why the psalm must not be rewritten here.

If this chapter performed Chapter 60, Movement VIII would have no next necessary question about the citation. If this chapter refused to name Psalm 82, the reader would be asked to take “written in your law” as a gesture without an object. The architecture requires both the naming and the reservation.

The reservation is sequence. It is not a suspicion that Psalm 82 is weak. It is the refusal to steal the next why.

Thus:

> **Psalm 82:6 is identified here because a citation must be located. It is examined in Chapter 60 because location is not the psalm’s own context and is not the dedicated account of why this wording is used.**

# 59.23 Verse 35a: “If He Called Them Gods, Unto Whom the Word of God Came”

Verse 35 continues the unit. Its first member is a condition:

> **“If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came…”**

The condition is not a hypothetical invented to evade. It is the consequence of admitting verse 34. If it is written, “I said, Ye are gods,” then He — the Speaker of that written wording — called them gods. The “them” are characterized: those unto whom the word of God came.

At this stage, three things are explicit and one thing is reserved.

**Explicit:** the written wording is treated as a calling. Someone, in the writing, called persons gods.

**Explicit:** those persons are identified as those to whom the word of God came. The writing is not being used as a random vocabulary-list. It is being used as a case: word of God, persons addressed, the name given in that address.

**Explicit:** the condition is the premise of the application that verse 36 will make. “If” here is the logic of the argument, not a doubt that the writing exists.

**Reserved:** who, precisely, those persons are in Psalm 82; what אֱלֹהִים means in that psalm; how “the word of God came” maps onto the psalm’s own sentences; and how the a fortiori toward the sanctified and sent Son is to be drawn in full. Those are Chapter 60 and Chapter 61.

This chapter’s job is to see that verse 35a is not a new topic. It is the interpretation of the citation as a written case. The Graphē supplies not only a phrase but a characterization. He argues from the writing as writing that has already named and already described.

Thus:

> **Verse 35a treats the citation as a written case: persons to whom the word of God came were called gods. The case is located here. Its full mapping onto Psalm 82 remains later.**

# 59.24 Verse 35b: “And the Scripture Cannot Be Broken” Named, Not Finished

The unit contains the clause:

> **“…and the scripture cannot be broken…”**

The clause is not optional ornament. It stands between the characterization of the cited persons and the application to the present charge. In the argument’s structure it functions as a parenthetical of force: the writing that has called them gods is Scripture, and that Scripture cannot be broken.

What this chapter must do with the clause is strictly limited and still full.

It must **include** the clause in the complete quotation. Omitting it would mutilate the passage.

It must **locate** the clause as a statement about ἡ γραφή, the writing already named as Scripture.

It must **refuse** to treat the clause as a slogan detachable from the citation and the charge.

It must **refuse** to define “broken,” to answer why, and to connect the why to John 17:17 as finished Graphē-doctrine. Those are Chapters 62–64.

The positive force available at this stage is nevertheless real. He does not treat the cited wording as a negotiable illustration. He treats it as Scripture, and He states that this Scripture cannot be broken, as a step in an argument that will apply the wording to the charge. The statement is His. The statement is about the Graphē. The statement belongs to the opening of the verdict. The dedicated doctrine of the statement remains the dedicated doctrine.

A stacked hedge would say: perhaps the clause means something, but we cannot know until later, so it has no force now. Sequence does not require that evacuation. The clause has force as His assertion of the writing’s standing in the dispute. The later files will examine that standing’s meaning and ground. They will not create the assertion.

Thus:

> **“The scripture cannot be broken” belongs to the complete passage as Christ’s assertion of the writing’s standing in the dispute. The dedicated sense and the dedicated why remain Chapters 62–63. Quotation is not those chapters.**

# 59.25 Verse 36: From Written Wording to the Present Charge

Verse 36 is the application.

> **“Say ye of him, whom the Father hath sanctified, and sent into the world, Thou blasphemest; because I said, I am the Son of God?”**

The verse does four things.

It **identifies the subject** of their charge as the one the Father sanctified and sent into the world. The identification is not a new biography. It is the claim-class already in force: sent by the Father, doer of the Father’s works.

It **quotes their charge**: “Thou blasphemest.” The application does not change the subject. It returns their word to them.

It **states His own sentence** as the because-clause: “because I said, I am the Son of God.” The oneness-claim of verse 30 and the Son-claim of verse 36 stand together in the dispute. The opponents’ “makest thyself God” and His “I am the Son of God” are the controversy’s two namings of the same offence as they hear it.

It **puts the application as a question**. The question is not ignorance. It is the forcing of a comparison. If the writing called those to whom the word of God came “gods,” do you say of the sanctified and sent One that He blasphemes because He said He is the Son of God?

The comparison is reasoning. Chapter 61 will follow that reasoning as its object: the logic of the argument and the weight assigned to the written text. This chapter must see that verse 36 is not a detached identity-claim. It is the use of the written case upon the present charge. Without verse 36, verses 34–35 would be a lemma about a psalm. With verse 36, the lemma is an answer to blasphemy.

What verse 36 does not do, at this stage, is finish the a fortiori as a dedicated demonstration. The reader can see that an application is being made. The dedicated account of how the application follows, and what weight the written wording is given in that following, is Chapter 61.

Thus:

> **Verse 36 applies the written case to the blasphemy-charge against the sanctified and sent Son. Application belongs to the complete passage. The dedicated reasoning-file remains Chapter 61.**

# 59.26 The Argumentative Structure of the Three Verses

The unit has a structure that can be exhibited without becoming Chapter 61.

**Occasion of the answer:** a blasphemy-charge following the claim of oneness with the Father, after works offered as witness, in a public Temple controversy.

**Move 1 — Question of writing (v. 34a):** Is it not written in your law?

**Move 2 — Citation (v. 34b):** I said, Ye are gods.

**Move 3 — Written case (v. 35a):** If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came.

**Move 4 — Standing of the writing (v. 35b):** and the scripture cannot be broken.

**Move 5 — Application (v. 36):** Do you say of the Father’s sanctified and sent One, Thou blasphemest, because I said, I am the Son of God?

The structure is forensic. It is the structure of an argument from a received text to a present charge. It is not the structure of a mystic’s assertion. It is not the structure of a later creed’s first article. It is not the structure of a proof-text card.

Two observations belong to this chapter’s exhibition of structure.

First, **the writing is not an afterthought**. It is Move 1. Before He restates His own identity in verse 36, He has already asked about their law, cited wording, characterized the written case, and stated the writing’s standing.

# 59.27 Why the Appeal Is to Scripture as Decisive in the Dispute

“Decisive in the dispute” must be defined at this level, or it will be heard either as the Graphē-gate or as a weak metaphor.

In a dispute, a court is decisive when the parties are placed under it, such that the argument proceeds by what that court says rather than by mere preference. Christ places this dispute under what is written in their law. He does not say, “Let us set Scripture aside and speak from experience.” He does not say, “Let the stones decide.” He does not say, “My prestige decides.” He says, “Is it not written…?”

The appeal is decisive in the dispute for four inspectable reasons, none of which is yet Chapter 65.

**It is their own writing.** They cannot recuse the court as foreign.

**It is writing, not mood.** γεγραμμένον names a standing text.

**It is used as a case, not as decoration.** Verse 35 treats the wording as a calling of persons to whom the word of God came.

**It governs the application.** Verse 36 does not proceed as if verses 34–35 had not been spoken.

What “decisive” does not mean here is that every later question of authority has been answered. Mishandling remains later. Primary written authority as the book’s finished gate remains later. The dedicated why of unbreakability remains later. Decisive in this dispute is already a full conclusion: the Graphē is the court He opens, and He treats that court as governing the answer to blasphemy.

If the writing were not decisive in this sense, verse 34 would be theatre. If it were already the finished gate, Chapters 60–65 would be repetition. The present sense is the sense the passage warrants at the opening of the movement.

Thus:

> **The appeal is to Scripture as the court of this dispute: their writing, as writing, used as a case, governing the application. That is decisiveness at this level, not the Graphē-gate.**

# 59.28 This Appeal Is Not Mere Opinion

Mere opinion has already been defined by contrast. It remains to show, from the passage, that the contrast is the passage’s own.

Opinion would sound like: I think I am the Son of God; I prefer this reading; accept me because I say so. The record does contain His own sentences of identity. It does not treat those sentences as unsupported preference. In this scene He has already named the works as witness. He now names the writing as court. Identity-claim, works, and writing are the architecture of the answer. Remove the writing, and the remaining architecture is still Chapter 45. Remove the works, and the remaining architecture is still a Graphē-appeal. Remove both, and one is left with a sentence without court and without works — which is not this scene.

The form “Is it not written” is a form that submits the speaker, as arguer, to a wording already standing. He is not less the Son for submitting the argument to the writing. He is the Son who argues from the writing. Chapter 48 already established that the Truth treats received Scripture as binding written word. This scene is that practice under stones.

The appeal is therefore a verdict upon the Graphē and a verdict upon opinion. The Graphē stands as court. Opinion, if it means a preference detachable from writing and from works, is not the court. The later distinction between the authority of the text and the authority of interpreters (Chapter 66) is not this file. The present point is Christ’s own act: He does not place His identity-claim in a court of preference. He places it in the court of what is written.

Thus:

> **The appeal is not mere opinion. He argues from a received writing’s standing, in a dispute already supplied with works as witness. Preference is not the court He opens.**
