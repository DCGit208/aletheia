# 51.3 Define the Question: The Scriptural Corpus

**The scriptural corpus**, in this chapter, is the body of writings the biblical record itself treats as Scripture: earlier written witnesses already standing as Holy Writ, and later written witnesses continuous with that body rather than a rival corpus invented later.

The object is not a later ecclesiastical catalogue. It is not a council's decree. It is not yet the closed canon of Chapter 52. It is not yet the conclusion that the Graphē is primary written authority. It is the body the record names and uses as γραφή / “the scriptures,” across the writings Christ confirmed and the writings His sent witnesses deposited.

Four elements belong to the definition.

First, **body**. A corpus is not a single isolated sentence, and not an abstract idea of “revelation.” It is a collection of writings treated together as Scripture. Chapter 16 already established library. The present chapter asks what makes that library *scriptural* in the record's own treatment: it is read as Scripture, cited as Scripture, fulfilled as Scripture, searched as Scripture, and later writings are located in relation to those Scriptures.

Second, **the record's own treatment**. Recognition by a later church is a real historical question. It is Chapter 52's question. The present question is prior in the book's sequence: how do the writings themselves treat writings as Scripture? A later list may acknowledge a body. It does not create the first fact that Moses is already Scripture in the Lord's mouth, or that Paul's letters are already spoken of in relation to the other Scriptures in 2 Peter's sentence.

Third, **earlier and later written witnesses**. Earlier, in this chapter, names the writings already in force when Christ confirms them: Law, Prophets, Psalms — the Scriptures He received. Later names the apostolic deposition: gospel-narrative, Acts as preached and written witness, letters written among the churches. Earlier and later are chronological and mediatorial distinctions inside one scriptural body. They are not two religions.

Fourth, **relationship**. The later witnesses do not replace the earlier. They do not sit beside them as a second canon awaiting a later merger. They stand in the relationship Chapter 50 already authenticated as fulfillment and handing-on, now examined as membership in one body under the record's names.

**Recognized as Scripture**, in this chapter's title, does not mean “recognized by a later synod.” It means treated, named, and used as Scripture in the biblical record itself. The English of the locked TOC is thereby controlled. Recognition as later historical process remains Chapter 52. Recognition as the record's own treatment is this file.

The question of this chapter joins those definitions:

> **Does the authentic testimony present a body of writings treated as Scripture, such that earlier witnesses already stand as that Scripture, later witnesses stand continuous with it rather than as a rival corpus, and the names γραφή / “the scriptures” are used across both without flattening the witnesses into one undifferentiated pile and without constructing the later list?**

Several elements belong to the question as this chapter defines it.

First, **the earlier body He names**. The law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms; Moses and all the prophets; all the scriptures.

Second, **the earlier body's public life**. The book of the law read; the prophet's book opened; the scriptures searched; the law and the prophets read in the synagogue.

Third, **the later deposition**. Ordered narrative written from what was delivered; signs written that the reader might believe; letters written and circulated; a later letter spoken of with the other scriptures.

Fourth, **the names used across both**. ἡ γραφή, αἱ γραφαί, ἱερὰ γράμματα, πᾶσα γραφή, ταῖς λοιπαῖς γραφαῖς.

Thus:

> **The question of the scriptural corpus is the question of what body the record itself treats as Scripture, earlier and later, under names already established, with confirmation and apostolic deposition as premises, and with list-construction, flattening, replacement, and the Graphē-gate refused as this chapter's objects.**

# 51.6 Corpus Defined

**Corpus**, in this chapter, is a body of writings treated together as Scripture.

The Latin word means body. It is used here as a precise English instrument, not as a rival to the biblical names. The biblical names are γραφή, αἱ γραφαί, ἱερὰ γράμματα, the law, the prophets, the psalms, the book, the books. Corpus names the collected character of what those terms, in their relevant uses, designate.

Three marks belong to the definition as the record exhibits it.

**Written.** The object is not oral tradition as such, not an unwritten memory of Israel's past, and not an inner impression. It is writing. “It is written.” “Have ye not read.” “The book of the prophet.” “This scripture.” Chapter 20 already established that γραφή belongs to writing. The present definition takes that mark as constitutive of the body: the corpus is a body of *writings*.

**Treated as Scripture.** Not every writing in the ancient world is this body. Treaties, invoices, and private letters are writing. The scriptural corpus is writing treated as Holy Writ: read in the assembly, cited as decisive, fulfilled as what must stand, searched as the test of a preached claim. Treatment is the record's own evidence that a writing belongs to the body. Treatment is not yet a later synod's vote.

**Collected without being flattened.** Law is not identical with a later apostolic letter. A psalm is not identical with a prophet's book. A gospel-narrative is not identical with Moses. The body collects them as Scripture. It does not erase the distinctions of form, office, and time. Collection is one body. Flattening would be one pile.

Thus:

> **The scriptural corpus is the collected body of writings the biblical record treats as Scripture. It is written. It is treated as Holy Writ. It is collected without being flattened. It is not yet the closed list, and not yet the Graphē-gate.**

# 51.7 Corpus Is Not a Council Decree

The reduction nearest to hand is to confuse the corpus with a later church-list.

Later churches did produce lists. They argued titles. They distinguished received writings from disputed writings and from writings they refused. Those later facts are real. They have a chapter: Chapter 52. They are not this chapter's origin-story.

The record's own treatment is prior to those later facts and independent of them as origin. Moses is already Scripture in the Lord's mouth before any later Christian catalogue exists. The prophets are already read in the synagogue in Acts 13:15. The psalms are already named with the law and the prophets in Luke 24:44. Paul's letters are already spoken of in relation to the other Scriptures in 2 Peter 3:16. A later list may acknowledge what was already treated as Scripture. It does not create the first treatment.

If a council decree were the corpus, Movement VII would have to wait upon a later corporation to have a body of writings to examine. The book would then be investigating a decree rather than writings. The writings present the opposite order: the body is already named and used as Scripture in Israel's public life, in the Lord's confirmation, and in the apostolic deposition. Later recognition is a question about that body. It is not the body's birth.

The distinction protects both sides. It protects the writings from being dissolved into later institutional history. It protects later recognition from pretending to have invented Moses. Chapter 52 remains necessary precisely because a body treated as Scripture still leaves the question of which writings constitute the authoritative corpus as a dedicated investigation. Necessity of the next chapter is not absence of this one.

Thus:

> **A council decree is not the scriptural corpus. The body is the writings the record itself treats as Scripture. Later lists belong to recognition. Recognition is the next chapter, not this one.**

# 51.8 Corpus Is Not an Undifferentiated Pile

The second near reduction is to treat “one body” as if it meant “one indistinguishable sheet.”

The record does not speak that way. It names the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms. It names the book of Moses and the book of the prophet Isaiah as distinct books. It names “this scripture” as a particular passage and “the scriptures” as the body. It names a later letter in relation to the other scriptures, which is a relation of standing, not a claim that a letter is a psalm. Hebrews 1:1–2 distinguishes God speaking in time past unto the fathers by the prophets from His speaking in these last days by His Son. Distinction of times and of mediators is the record's own grammar. It is not a licence to divide the body into rival religions. It is a licence to refuse flattening.

Flattening would produce several false inferences.

It would infer that because later writings are Scripture with earlier writings, Moses has the same narrative office as John. He does not. Moses writes the law. John writes signs that the reader might believe that Jesus is the Christ.

It would infer that because both are γραφή, a proverb and an apostolic greeting are the same kind of sentence. Chapter 31 already refused to make literary diversity into partial inspiration. The present chapter refuses to make one category into one literary form.

It would infer that “one body” means that earlier and later witnesses have no sequence. They have sequence. Earlier writings stand when Christ confirms them. Later writings are deposited after the hinge, as confirmation unto those who were not in the room. Sequence is not rivalry. Sequence is the order of the body.

Thus:

> **One scriptural body is not an undifferentiated pile. The record names Law, Prophets, Psalms, and later apostolic writings with distinctions of form, office, and time, while treating them as Scripture. Collection without flattening is the grammar of this chapter.**

# 51.9 Earlier Written Witnesses Defined

**Earlier written witnesses**, in this chapter, are the writings already in force as Scripture when Christ confirms them, and already in force as Scripture when the apostles preach and write according to them.

The designation is chronological and historical, not honorary. These writings precede the public ministry of Jesus of Nazareth. They are the Scriptures of Israel. They are read in synagogues. They are the Law, the Prophets, and, in His own naming, the Psalms. Chapter 48 established His confirmation of that body as binding written word. The present chapter takes that confirmation as a premise and identifies those writings as the earlier members of the scriptural corpus.

Three marks belong to the definition.

**Already standing.** They do not wait upon the apostolic deposition to become Scripture. “It is written” is a formula of present force in the wilderness, in controversy, and in the synagogue. A writing that binds the Son in temptation is already Scripture. A writing read as fulfilled this day in the hearers' ears is already Scripture. Standing is not a later courtesy extended backward.

**Named as a body.** Luke 24:44 does not confirm a handful of favourite sentences detached from books. It names the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms as the writings concerning Him. Luke 24:27 begins at Moses and all the prophets and expounds in all the scriptures the things concerning Himself. The body is named as a body. Naming a body is not enumerating every later title at the edges. Chapter 48 already refused both inflation and evacuation. That refusal remains in force.

**Used as Scripture in Israel's public life before the later deposition.** The book of the law is read to the people. The prophet's book is a scroll that can be handed and opened. The scriptures are searched. The law and the prophets are read in the synagogue every sabbath. Public use is evidence of standing. It is not yet Chapter 52's recognition-history of a later church.

Thus:

> **Earlier written witnesses are the Scriptures already standing when Christ confirms them: Law, Prophets, Psalms — a named body, publicly used as Holy Writ, not waiting upon later Christian writing to become Scripture.**

---

# 51.10 Later Written Witnesses Defined

**Later written witnesses**, in this chapter, are the writings deposited by the apostolic witness concerning Christ: the ordered narratives of what was delivered by eyewitnesses and ministers of the word, the written continuation of the public preaching, and the letters written among the churches.

The designation is chronological and mediatorial, not a claim of inferior force. These writings come after the hinge. They confirm unto later hearers what the Lord spoke. They hand on the gospel according to the Scriptures. Chapter 50 established their authenticating force as the New Testament witness. The present chapter takes that force as a premise and asks how those writings stand inside the scriptural body.

Three marks belong to the definition.

**Deposited.** Luke writes in order that Theophilus might know the certainty of things delivered. John writes selected signs that the reader might believe. A named disciple testifies and wrote. Paul writes to churches. Peter writes of Paul's letters. Deposition is writing as the ordered continuation of witness, already exhibited in Chapter 50. The present chapter needs that mark as membership: later Scripture, if it is Scripture, is written witness, not a second oral religion.

**Located according to the earlier Scriptures.** “According to the scriptures” is the gospel's own location of itself. The later witness does not arrive as a replacement library. It arrives as testimony that the earlier writings are fulfilled in Him, and as the handing-on of what was written of Him together with what they saw and heard. Location is a corpus-mark. A later writing that discarded Moses would not be this later witness.

**Spoken of in relation to the scriptures.** 2 Peter 3:16 locates Paul's letters in relation to the other Scriptures. 1 Timothy 5:18 joins a Mosaic sentence and a sentence matching the Lord's word under “the scripture saith.” Those texts will be examined in their contexts. Their force at this stage is naming, not list-construction: later writing is treated inside the scriptural category, not parked outside it as mere commentary.

Thus:

> **Later written witnesses are the apostolic deposition: written continuation of the commissioned witness, located according to the earlier Scriptures, and named in relation to the scriptures — continuous members of the body, not a rival corpus.**

---

# 51.11 One Body Without Rivalry

Earlier and later, so defined, are not two corpora awaiting a later merger.

The rival-corpus claim was already examined and refused as a global account in Chapter 50. The present chapter uses that refusal as a premise and states the positive membership-claim: one scriptural body under investigation, with earlier and later as distinguishable witnesses inside it.

The structure is not:

> **Old Testament library + New Testament library = a later composite called the Bible.**

That English convenience may describe a printed binding. It does not describe the record's own order. The record's order is:

> **Scriptures already standing → the Christ of whom they were written → sent witnesses who preach and write according to those Scriptures → later writings treated in relation to the other Scriptures.**

The later writings do not create the earlier. The earlier writings do not remain a closed room into which later writing may not enter. The body is one because the record treats later writing as belonging with the scriptures, and because the later writing locates itself by the earlier. Belonging and location are the positive relation. Rivalry would require the later witness to present itself as a second god, a second law, or a second people of God with a second holy book. The record does not present that.

Hebrews 1:1–2 is sometimes misused as if “in time past” and “in these last days” named two bodies. The sentence names two times of speaking and two classes of mediators: the prophets, and the Son. It does not name two scriptures. The Son confirms the prophets' writings. The sent witnesses of the Son write according to those writings. Sequence of speech is not schism of corpus.

Thus:

> **Earlier and later written witnesses are one scriptural body under investigation. Sequence is real. Rivalry is not the record's claim. A printed two-testament binding is a later convenience, not the origin of the body.**

---

# 51.12 The Record's Names: γραφή and “the scriptures”

The body is not an unnamed heap upon which the investigator imposes a theory. It is named.

The principal New Testament name is **γραφή (*graphē*, G1124)**. Chapters 20–22 established the lexical field and the biblical usage. The present chapter uses that name as the record's designation of the body and of its members.

The usage is both singular and plural, both particular and collected.

**ἡ γραφή (*hē graphē*)** — the scripture — can designate a particular writing or passage: “this scripture”; “the scripture saith”; “the scripture cannot be broken,” which last remains Movement VIII's dedicated file and is named here only as an instance of the term. Particular designation is not the absence of a body. A member is named as a member.

**αἱ γραφαί (*hai graphai*)** — the scriptures — designates the writings as a body: “have ye not read in the scriptures”; “ye do err, not knowing the scriptures”; “search the scriptures”; “according to the scriptures”; “all the scriptures.” The plural is the body's ordinary name.

**ἱερὰ γράμματα (*hiera grammata*, G2413 + G1121)** — holy writings — is Paul's name in 2 Timothy 3:15 for the sacred writings Timothy has known from childhood. **γράμμα (*gramma*)** is letter, writing. The adjective marks them as holy. Verse 16 then speaks of πᾶσα γραφή. The two expressions stand in one paragraph. They will be distinguished without being split.

**νόμος (*nomos*, G3551)** — law — in relevant uses names the written Torah, often with the prophets as a pair: “the law and the prophets.” **προφῆται (*prophētai*)** names the prophets as a written body as well as a class of speakers. **ψαλμοί (*psalmoi*, G5568)** names the psalms as the third in Luke 24:44.

**βίβλος (*biblos*)** and **βιβλίον (*biblion*)** name a book: the book of Moses, the book of the prophet, the book of the law. Book is not yet the English word *Bible*. Chapter 15 already established that correspondence as history of a name. The present chapter needs the biblical use: a scripture can be a book that is opened, read, and cited.

These names are not a slogan list. They are the grammar by which the record treats writings as Scripture. The chapter must now show that grammar at work, first in the earlier witnesses, then in the later, then across both.

Thus:

> **The scriptural corpus is named in the record as γραφή / the scriptures, with law, prophets, psalms, holy writings, and book as belonging designations. The names are the evidence of treatment. They are not yet a table of titles.**

---

# 51.13 Lexical Control of γραφή

The lexical sequence required by the methodology is not optional.

**Original script:** γραφή.

**Transliteration:** *graphē*.

**Strong's number:** G1124.

**Basic lexical meaning:** writing; a writing; what is written. The noun belongs with **γράφω (*graphō*, G1125)**, to write. Classical usage includes drawing and writing. The New Testament usage that matters here is the specialized designation of Holy Writ / Scripture.

**Grammatical forms:** singular γραφή / ἡ γραφή; plural γραφαί / αἱ γραφαί; dative ταῖς γραφαῖς; genitive γραφῆς. Context decides whether a particular passage, a particular book, or the collected scriptures are in view.

**Biblical usage:** the term is the New Testament's ordinary word for Scripture. It is what is read, what must be fulfilled, what is searched, what says, what cannot be broken — the last of which remains later as doctrine. It is not a generic word for any document in the empire.

**Contextual meaning in this chapter:** γραφή names the writings treated as Holy Writ, earlier and later, without being mechanically translated “the Bible” in every occurrence. Chapter 20 already forbade that mechanical translation. The prohibition remains. A verse that says “this scripture” is not thereby naming a sixty-six-book English volume. A verse that says “the other scriptures” is not thereby printing a table of contents. The term still names Scripture. Scope is contextual. Category is stable.

**Argumentative significance:** if the record uses one category-name across earlier passages, earlier books, the collected earlier body, and later apostolic writings in relation to that body, then the investigator is warranted in treating those writings as one scriptural body under investigation. The name is not the whole warrant. The use is the warrant. The name makes the use inspectable.

Thus:

> **γραφή is writing that, in the relevant biblical usage, designates Scripture. It may name a passage, a book, or the body. It must not be flattened into “the Bible” in every sentence. Its use across earlier and later witnesses is this chapter's lexical hinge.**

---

# 51.14 Singular and Plural: a Scripture and the Scriptures

The distinction between singular and plural is a corpus-distinction, not a curiosity.

When Jesus asks, “Did ye never read in the scriptures” (Matthew 21:42), and then cites Psalm 118:22, the plural names the body in which a particular writing is found. When He says of a passage, “This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears” (Luke 4:21), the singular names the Isaiah-text just read. When Paul writes, “the scripture saith” (Romans 4:3; 1 Timothy 5:18), the singular can introduce a cited writing as Scripture speaking. When Luke writes that beginning at Moses and all the prophets He expounded “in all the scriptures” the things concerning Himself (Luke 24:27), the plural names the body as the field of exposition.

The grammar forbids two errors.

**Error 1 — particular as absence of body.** If “this scripture” names a passage, it does not follow that there is no collected body. A passage is cited *as scripture* because it belongs to the scriptures. Membership is implied by the designation.

**Error 2 — body as already closed list.** If “the scriptures” names a body, it does not follow that the sentence has enumerated the later titles. A named body can be definite for its users without being a synod's catalogue. The Lord's hearers know what He means by the scriptures. That knowledge is historical and public. It is not Chapter 52's later recognition-file.

The same grammar will matter when later writings are spoken of with “the other scriptures.” “Other” is a class-word. It locates Paul's letters beside scriptures already so called. It does not print the remaining titles.

Thus:

> **Singular and plural are the record's way of naming member and body. A passage is scripture in the scriptures. The body is named without being listed. That grammar is already corpus-grammar.**

---

# 51.15 Holy Writings and Scripture Distinguished Without Split

2 Timothy 3:15–16 stands in one paragraph.

> **“And that from a child thou hast known the holy scriptures, which are able to make thee wise unto salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus. All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness.”**

**ἱερὰ γράμματα (*hiera grammata*)** in verse 15 names the sacred writings Timothy has known from childhood. The childhood-mark identifies those writings as the Scriptures of Israel, already standing, already taught in a Jewish household, already able — in Paul's claim — to make wise unto salvation through faith in Christ Jesus. The earlier body is not a preparatory ethic awaiting a later sacredness. It is already holy writing, and already ordered toward the Christ now preached.

**πᾶσα γραφή (*pasa graphē*)** in verse 16 names Scripture comprehensively within the category. Chapter 31 established that “all” cannot become “some,” and that the immediate context of Timothy's known writings is the earlier body, while the category γραφή is not thereby sealed against later writings treated as Scripture elsewhere. That double discipline remains: the childhood writings are the earlier corpus; the category is Scripture as such.

The present chapter needs the paragraph as corpus-evidence, not as the guarantee-file.

It exhibits the earlier body as already holy writing known in childhood.

It exhibits that earlier body as ordered toward salvation through faith in Christ Jesus — continuity, not replacement.

It exhibits γραφή as the category-name in the next sentence, comprehensive within its referent.

It does not, in this file, convert θεόπνευστος (*theopneustos*, G2315) into the finished doctrine of what inspiration guarantees. Chapter 30 examined the mode. Chapters 56–57 remain the guarantee and infallibility files. Sequence is not a weakening of verse 16. It is the book's order.

Thus:

> **Holy writings and Scripture stand in one paragraph without being split into two religions, and without being flattened into one sentence that has already closed the list and finished the guarantees. Timothy's childhood writings are the earlier body. γραφή is the category. Later membership remains to be shown from the record's other namings.**

# 51.16 Law as Standing Scripture

The first named member of the earlier body is the Law.

**νόμος Μωϋσέως (*nomos Mōyseōs*)** — the law of Moses — is not, in the relevant uses, a mood of moral seriousness. It is writing. Jesus cites Deuteronomy in the wilderness as what is written. He cites Genesis as what was said at the beginning, and treats the written beginning as measuring later concession. He cites Exodus at the bush as the book of Moses that must be read. He names Moses as a writer: “he wrote of me” (John 5:46). “If ye believe not his writings, how shall ye believe my words?” (John 5:47). **γράμμασιν (*grammasin*)** in that sentence is writings. Moses is not a rumour. Moses is a corpus-member already in force.

The Law's standing is public in Israel's life before the later deposition.

Joshua is commanded that this book of the law shall not depart out of his mouth; he shall meditate therein day and night (Joshua 1:8). The book is copied on stones and read before the assembly (Joshua 8:32–35). In the days of Josiah the book of the law is found in the house of the LORD and read, and the reading is treated as decisive for the nation's standing before God (2 Kings 22–23). In Nehemiah 8 Ezra brings the book of the law of Moses, reads it before the congregation, and the Levites give the sense. These scenes are not later Christian theory about “Old Testament inspiration.” They are Israel's own public treatment of a writing as the LORD's law in a book.

Deuteronomy itself commands a king to write a copy of this law in a book, to read therein all the days of his life (Deuteronomy 17:18–19). The command assumes a written object capable of copying and reading. Corpus begins, historically, as a book that can be copied.

The present chapter does not re-try Chapter 48's confirmation-file. It states the corpus-consequence: the Law is already Scripture, already a book, already public, already binding in the record's treatment, before any apostolic letter is written.

Thus:

> **The Law is earlier written witness in the strict sense: a book associated with Moses, read, copied, cited as what is written, and confirmed by Christ as standing Scripture. It does not wait upon later Christian writing to enter the body.**

---

# 51.17 The Prophets as Written Witnesses

The second named member is the Prophets.

A prophet is first a speaker sent. Chapter 25 examined prophets as a mode of giving. Chapter 37 examined prophecy and fulfillment as an authentication line. The present chapter needs a narrower mark: the prophets are also writers, and their writings stand as Scripture.

Daniel 9:2 exhibits the mark with unusual explicitness:

> **“I Daniel understood by books the number of the years, whereof the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah the prophet, that he would accomplish seventy years in the desolations of Jerusalem.”**

Daniel understands **by books**. The word of the LORD that came to Jeremiah is available as writing. Prophetic speech has become a book that another prophet reads. That is corpus-formation inside the earlier body: a later writer treats an earlier prophet's book as the place where the LORD's word is to be understood. The scene is not a church-list. It is a prophet reading a prophet.

Luke 4:17–21 exhibits the mark in the Lord's own synagogue:

> **“And there was delivered unto him the book of the prophet Esaias. And when he had opened the book, he found the place where it was written…”**

The prophet Isaiah is a book that can be delivered, opened, and found at a place where it is written. “This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears.” The writing is γραφή. The book is the prophet's book. Fulfillment is the filling of what the writing already contained. Chapter 48 examined the scene as confirmation. The present chapter examines it as corpus: a prophetic writing standing as Scripture in public reading.

Matthew 26:56: “But all this was done, that the scriptures of the prophets might be fulfilled.” **αἱ γραφαὶ τῶν προφητῶν (*hai graphai tōn prophētōn*)** — the scriptures of the prophets — is a named written body. Romans 1:2: the gospel of God “which he had promised afore by his prophets in the holy scriptures.” Romans 16:26: “the scriptures of the prophets.” The prophets are not only a memory of voices. They are holy scriptures in which promise was written.

Acts 13:15 shows the public pair:

> **“And after the reading of the law and the prophets…”**

The synagogue reading is not “the law” alone. The prophets are read with the law as standing public Scripture. Acts 24:14: Paul confesses that he believes “all things which are written in the law and in the prophets.” Acts 26:22: he witnesses “none other things than those which the prophets and Moses did say should come.” Acts 28:23: he persuades concerning Jesus, “both out of the law of Moses, and out of the prophets, from morning till evening.” The later witness locates itself by the earlier written prophets. Location is corpus-relation.

Thus:

> **The Prophets are earlier written witnesses: books that can be opened and read, scriptures in which promise was written, paired with the Law in public reading, and used by the later witness as the writings according to which Christ is preached. They are Scripture before the apostolic deposition, and they remain Scripture in it.**

# 51.18 The Psalms as Named Third

The third named member is the Psalms.

Luke 24:44 records the Lord's own grouping:

> **“These are the words which I spake unto you, while I was yet with you, that all things must be fulfilled, which were written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning me.”**

**ψαλμοί (*psalmoi*)** stand as the named third. Chapter 48 already established that this naming confirms at least the Psalter as belonging to the writings concerning Himself, and that it is historically coherent with a threefold Jewish division of Law, Prophets, and Writings, of which the Psalms are the head and representative. Coherence is not yet Chapter 52's closed list. It is reception of a known body under names His hearers already used. That conclusion is a premise here.

The present chapter needs the psalms as corpus-members.

They are written. Jesus cites them as writing: “in the book of Psalms” (Luke 20:42; Acts 1:20). **βίβλῳ ψαλμῶν (*biblō psalmōn*)** — in the book of Psalms — is a book-name. A psalm is not an uncollected hymn floating in the air. It is in a book.

They are treated as Scripture concerning Him. Acts 2 argues from David. Acts 4:25–26 cites Psalm 2 as what God said by the mouth of David. Acts 13:33–35 cites the psalms as fulfilled in the raising. The later witness does not invent a new sacred poetry. It reads the psalms as already Scripture.

They represent, in His naming, the third division of the received body. The Bible Introduction's observation that the Jewish Scriptures were divided as Law, Prophets, and Writings, and that Christ referred to this division as the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms, is historical correspondence already used in Chapter 48. The correspondence supports identification of the earlier corpus as a known threefold body. It does not license this chapter to settle which later titles belong at the edges of the Writings, nor to treat a traditional attribution of arrangement as if it were this file's demonstrated recognition-history. Edges and recognition remain Chapter 52.

Thus:

> **The Psalms are earlier written witness: a book, cited as writing, named by the raised Lord with the Law and the Prophets as the things written concerning Himself, and used by the later witness as Scripture. The third is a real member of the body. It is not a closed table of every title later counted among the Writings.**

---

# 51.19 Luke 24:27 and 24:44: The Named Earlier Body

The two Luke 24 sentences must be read as corpus-sentences, not as a second confirmation-trial.

Luke 24:27:

> **“And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself.”**

**Μωϋσέως καὶ ἀπὸ πάντων τῶν προφητῶν (*Mōyseōs kai apo pantōn tōn prophētōn*)** — beginning at Moses and from all the prophets. **ἐν πάσαις ταῖς γραφαῖς (*en pasais tais graphais*)** — in all the scriptures. Moses and the prophets are the starting-points. All the scriptures are the field. The object of exposition is the writings. The subject of the writings, in His claim, is Himself.

Luke 24:44 adds the third name and the fulfillment-claim: all things must be fulfilled which were written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning Me.

The two sentences together yield a corpus-identification of full force for the earlier body.

The earlier body is **writings**. What is expounded is what is written.

The earlier body is **collected**. “All the scriptures” is a body-name.

The earlier body is **articulated**. Law of Moses, prophets, psalms are distinguishable members, not one pile.

The earlier body is **definite for its users**. The disciples are charged as fools and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken (Luke 24:25). The charge assumes that the writings are known and that belief was already their due. A body that can be believed or unbelieved as “all that the prophets have spoken” is already Scripture.

The earlier body is **ordered toward Him**. “Concerning me” is the Lord's claim about the writings' subject. That claim is confirmation, already established. The corpus-consequence is that the earlier body is not a different religion's library which the later witness must replace. It is the scripture concerning the Christ now raised.

What the sentences do not yield is a closed list of later titles, a settlement of disputed edges, or Movement VIII's finished Graphē-doctrine. The named body is the earlier corpus. Naming is not enumeration of every scroll later counted. Chapter 48's refusal of inflation and evacuation remains the control.

Thus:

> **Luke 24:27 and 24:44 identify the earlier scriptural corpus as all the scriptures, articulated as Moses, the prophets, and the psalms, definite for their hearers, and concerning Him. That identification is this chapter's chief sentence for the earlier witnesses.**

---

# 51.20 John 5:39–47: Searchable Scriptures That Testify

John 5:39–47 exhibits the earlier body as searchable, testimonial, and already decisive.

> **“Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me.”**

**ἐραυνᾶτε τὰς γραφάς (*eraunate tas graphas*)** — search the scriptures. The verb is investigation of writings. The object is the body. The hearers already think they have eternal life in them. Jesus does not deny that the scriptures are the place of life. He charges that those scriptures testify of Him, and that the hearers will not come to Him that they might have life (John 5:40).

The argument then names Moses as writer:

> **“Do not think that I will accuse you to the Father: there is one that accuseth you, even Moses, in whom ye trust. For had ye believed Moses, ye would have believed me: for he wrote of me. But if ye believe not his writings, how shall ye believe my words?”** (John 5:45–47)

Several corpus-marks are simultaneous.

The scriptures are a body that can be searched. Search assumes a public, available writing, not a private oracle.

The scriptures testify of Him. Testimony is the earlier body's relation to the later events. It is not replacement.

Moses is trusted as an authority. The trust is not dismissed as misplaced in Moses. It is exposed as inconsistent if it refuses the One of whom Moses wrote.

Moses wrote. The writings are the court. Unbelief toward the writings explains unbelief toward His words. Chapter 48 used this as confirmation that the writings bind. The present chapter uses it as corpus: the earlier writings are the scriptural body already in the controversy, already the measure of whether His words will be believed.

The sentence does not list the later apostolic writings. It cannot. They are not yet deposited in the scene. The earlier corpus can be complete as earlier without being the whole body this chapter will identify once later witnesses are in view. Completeness as earlier is not closedness of the whole. That distinction protects both Luke 24 and the later namings.

Thus:

> **John 5 identifies the earlier corpus as searchable scriptures that testify of Him, with Moses' writings as a named member whose unbelief explains unbelief toward Christ's words. The earlier body is already the court. It is not yet the whole body once later witnesses have been deposited.**

---

# 51.21 The Earlier Body in Israel's Public Reading

The earlier corpus is not only named in controversy. It is read.

Acts 13:15: after the reading of the law and the prophets, the rulers of the synagogue invite a word of exhortation. The reading is the synagogue's ordinary act. The law and the prophets are the ordinary public Scripture. Paul's subsequent word is not a replacement of that reading. It is a word that argues from it: “Ye men and brethren, children of the stock of Abraham, and whosoever among you feareth God, to you is the word of this salvation sent” (Acts 13:26). He recites Israel's history as already written, cites the psalms, and proclaims the raising as the fulfillment of what was written.

Luke 4:16: Jesus, as His custom was, went into the synagogue on the sabbath day and stood up to read. Custom is public habit. The prophet's book is the reading-object. The earlier corpus lives as reading before it lives as Christian citation.

2 Corinthians 3:14–15 notes that until this day the same veil remains in the reading of the old testament — **ἐπὶ τῇ ἀναγνώσει τῆς παλαιᾶς διαθήκης (*epi tē anagnōsei tēs palaias diathēkēs*)**. **ἀνάγνωσις (*anagnōsis*, G320)** is reading. The old covenant is read as a writing. The sentence distinguishes old covenant from the present turning to the Lord. It does not discard the reading as a false scripture. The veil is over the heart in the reading, not a verdict that the writing is not Scripture. Distinction of covenant-administration is not schism of corpus. Chapter 54 may treat continuity more fully. The present point is that the earlier body is a reading-body.

1 Timothy 4:13: “Till I come, give attendance to reading, to exhortation, to doctrine.” Public reading continues in the churches. Colossians 4:16 commands that the letter be read among them and in Laodicea, and that they likewise read the letter from Laodicea. Later writings enter the same public act: they are read. Reading is a corpus-habit. It is not yet a list.

Thus:

> **The earlier body is public reading: law and prophets in the synagogue, the prophet's book as custom, the old covenant as a writing read. Later letters are commanded to be read. Reading is how the body lives among the people. It is not how a later synod invents the body.**

---

# 51.22 Romans and the Holy Scriptures

Paul's letter to Rome names the earlier body with unusual density.

Romans 1:1–2:

> **“…separated unto the gospel of God, (Which he had promised afore by his prophets in the holy scriptures,)”**

**ἐν γραφαῖς ἁγίαις (*en graphais hagiais*)** — in holy scriptures. The gospel is not a novelty detached from writing. It is what God promised beforehand by His prophets in holy scriptures. Later proclamation is located inside earlier holy writing.

Romans 3:21:

> **“But now the righteousness of God without the law is manifested, being witnessed by the law and the prophets.”**

The law and the prophets witness. They are not abolished as witnesses when righteousness without the law is manifested. They remain the witnessing body. “Without the law” as the ground of justification is not “without the scriptures.” The distinction is soteriological. It is not corpus-schism.

Romans 15:4:

> **“For whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning, that we through patience and comfort of the scriptures might have hope.”**

**ὅσα γὰρ προεγράφη (*hosa gar proegraphē*)** — whatsoever things were written aforetime. **διὰ τῆς ὑπομονῆς καὶ διὰ τῆς παρακλήσεως τῶν γραφῶν (*dia tēs hypomonēs kai dia tēs paraklēseōs tōn graphōn*)** — through patience and through the comfort of the scriptures. The earlier writings are for the later church's learning. They are not a discarded first volume. They are the scriptures by which the later people are to have hope.

Romans 16:26: the mystery “now is made manifest, and by the scriptures of the prophets, according to the commandment of the everlasting God, made known to all nations for the obedience of faith.” Made known to the nations does not discard the prophets' scriptures. It publishes what those scriptures contained, now manifested.

The four sentences together are corpus-grammar: holy scriptures; law and prophets as witnesses; things written aforetime as the later people's scriptures; scriptures of the prophets as the means by which the manifested mystery is made known. The later witness does not stand beside a closed Jewish library as a second sacredness. It stands in the earlier scriptures and makes them known.

Thus:

> **Romans names the earlier body as holy scriptures, the law and the prophets as witnessing writings, and the things written aforetime as the later people's scriptures for learning and hope. That is membership of the earlier witnesses in the one body, already in the apostolic letter.**

---

# 51.23 Daniel 9:2 and “the Books”

Daniel 9:2 deserves a dedicated sentence because it exhibits corpus inside the earlier body itself.

Daniel understands by **books** the years of which the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah. The Hebrew designation in view is writings already collected enough to be called books, already available to a reader in the exile, already the place where a prior prophet's word is to be understood. The Bible Introduction notes that the Greek of Daniel 9:2 in the Septuagint uses language of **the books**, and that such usage belongs to the history by which sacred writings came to be called the books — later *biblia*, later *Bible*. Chapter 15 already reconstructed that name-history. The present chapter needs the biblical fact: an earlier writer reads an earlier prophet as a book inside a body of books.

The inference that is warranted: the earlier corpus is not a single scroll produced at one sitting. It is a growing body of writings treated as the LORD's word in books. Jeremiah can be read by Daniel. The law can be read by Josiah. Isaiah can be read by Jesus. Growth of the body inside Israel's history is not chaos. It is how a corpus forms: later writing that is Scripture stands in relation to earlier writing that is already Scripture.

The inference that is not warranted: that Daniel 9:2 has closed the later Christian list, or that the English word *Bible* is thereby a biblical lemma in Daniel. Name-history is correspondence. It is not this chapter's licence to treat a printed edition as the autograph, a conclusion Chapter 40 already forbade in another file.

Thus:

> **Daniel 9:2 exhibits the earlier corpus as books: a prophet reading a prophet's writing as the place of the LORD's word. Corpus-formation is already the earlier body's life. It is not a later church's invention of the idea of a scriptural body.**

---

# 51.24 Later Written Witnesses as Deposition, Not a Second Library

The later witnesses must now be identified as members, not re-authenticated as if Chapter 50 had not closed.

Chapter 50 established that apostolic testimony authenticates the New Testament witness as the confirming, positioned, hinge-preaching testimony of those He sent, continuous with earlier Scripture rather than a rival corpus invented later. The present chapter takes that verdict as a premise and asks the corpus-question: what writings are that later witness, and how does the record treat them as belonging with the scriptures?

The later writings in view are not a closed table of twenty-seven titles. They are the deposited forms already exhibited:

- ordered narrative written from what eyewitnesses and ministers of the word delivered (Luke 1:1–4);
- selected signs written that the reader might believe (John 20:30–31), by a named disciple who testifies and wrote (John 21:24);
- public preaching preserved as written Acts-witness, arguing from the earlier scriptures;
- letters written to churches, read among them, and — in 2 Peter's sentence — spoken of in relation to the other scriptures.

Those are later written witnesses. They are later in time. They are apostolic in mediation. They are not later in the sense of a second sacredness invented when the first had failed.

The governing distinction is:

> **Later as sequence inside one body**
>
> versus
>
> **later as rival corpus.**

Sequence is the record's claim. Rivalry is the reduction already refused. Membership is this chapter's positive work.

Thus:

> **The later written witnesses are the apostolic deposition already authenticated as continuous with earlier Scripture. This chapter does not re-try that authentication. It locates those writings as later members of the scriptural body.**

---

# 51.25 Luke 1:1–4 as Later Writing of the Same Body's Continuity

Luke 1:1–4 exhibits later writing as investigative deposition, not as a new religion's charter.

> **“Forasmuch as many have taken in hand to set forth in order a declaration of those things which are most surely believed among us, Even as they delivered them unto us, which from the beginning were eyewitnesses, and ministers of the word; It seemed good to me also, having had perfect understanding of all things from the very first, to write unto thee in order, most excellent Theophilus, That thou mightest know the certainty of those things, wherein thou hast been instructed.”**

Chapter 50 used the paragraph as written deposition toward certainty. The present chapter needs three corpus-marks.

First, **it is writing**. **γράψαι (*grapsai*)** — to write. The later witness is not left as unwritten preaching only. It is deposited.

Second, **it is writing of what was delivered by eyewitnesses and ministers of the word**. The later writing does not invent a career. It orders what was delivered. Continuity with the events, and with the earlier scriptures those events fulfill, is the writing's own claim. Luke's gospel will open Moses, the prophets, and the psalms in chapter 24. The prologue is already aimed at that body of things believed.

Third, **it is writing for a later reader**. Theophilus is not in the room of the first delivery. The corpus, if it is to include later witnesses, must be capable of reaching those who were not there. Writing is that reach. Chapter 50 established the authenticating force. The present chapter states the membership-consequence: such writing is the later witness in the body, not an appendix of private notes.

The paragraph does not call itself γραφή. Absence of the noun in the prologue is not absence of later scriptural standing. Standing will be shown where the record does use the category of later writings. Luke's prologue shows the act of later writing in continuity with delivered witness. The name will be examined in 2 Peter 3:16 and 1 Timothy 5:18. Act and name together are the later membership. Either alone would be thinner than the record.

Thus:

> **Luke 1:1–4 exhibits later writing as ordered deposition of delivered witness toward a later reader's certainty. It is the act of later membership. The category-name is supplied in the record's other sentences. Act without name would be incomplete; name without act would be a label on nothing.**

---

# 51.26 John 20:30–31 and 21:24 as Later Written Witness

John's closing sentences exhibit later writing as selected, purposeful, and identified.

John 20:30–31:

> **“And many other signs truly did Jesus in the presence of his disciples, which are not written in this book: But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name.”**

**γεγραμμένα (*gegrammena*)** — written. **ἐν τῷ βιβλίῳ τούτῳ (*en tō bibliō toutō*)** — in this book. The later witness is a book. It is not the exhaustive archive of all signs. It is a selected writing aimed at belief that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God. The aim joins the later book to the earlier scriptures that testify of Him (John 5:39). The later book is written so that the reader might believe what the earlier scriptures already testified.

John 21:24:

> **“This is the disciple which testifieth of these things, and wrote these things: and we know that his testimony is true.”**

A named disciple testifies and wrote. Recognition that the testimony is true is the company's knowledge. Chapter 50 used the sentence as written deposition. Chapter 52 will ask how such recognition bears upon canonical reception. The present chapter needs the corpus-mark: later witness exists as a book written by a testifying disciple, aimed at the same Christ of whom the earlier scriptures testify. Continuity of subject is continuity of body. Two books about two gods would be two corpora. One book written that the reader might believe that Jesus is the Christ, of whom Moses wrote, is later membership.

Thus:

> **John's book is later written witness: selected signs in a book, written that the reader might believe that Jesus is the Christ of whom the earlier scriptures testify, by a disciple who testifies and wrote. Subject-continuity is body-continuity. Recognition-history remains later.**

---

# 51.27 Pauline Letters as Writings Among the Churches

The later body includes letters.

A letter is a literary form. Chapter 31 already established that letters can be Scripture without being the same form as law or psalm. The present chapter needs the historical mark: apostolic letters are written, sent, read, and circulated among the churches.

Colossians 4:16: “And when this epistle is read among you, cause that it be read also in the church of the Laodiceans; and that ye likewise read the epistle from Laodicea.” Reading and interchange are public corpus-habits. 1 Thessalonians 5:27: “I charge you by the Lord that this epistle be read unto all the holy brethren.” 2 Peter 3:1: “This second epistle, beloved, I now write unto you.” 2 Peter 3:15: “even as our beloved brother Paul also according to the wisdom given unto him hath written unto you.”

The letters are not private spiritual diaries later mistaken for Scripture. They are writings addressed to churches, commanded to be read, known in more than one place, and — in 2 Peter's sentence — already a plurality: “all his epistles.”

Plurality of letters is not yet a table of thirteen or fourteen titles. It is a deposited class: Paul's writings. Class is more than one occasional scrap and less than a closed list. The present chapter needs the class. Chapter 52 may ask the titles.

2 Peter 3:16 will now be examined as the record's own location of that class in relation to the other scriptures. The letters must be in view as writings before they can be in view as scriptures among scriptures.

Thus:

> **Pauline letters are later written witnesses as a class: written, sent, read, circulated, known as his epistles. Class is not a closed list. It is enough to make 2 Peter 3:16 a sentence about writings, not about rumours.**

---

# 51.28 2 Peter 3:15–16: Later Writings and “the Other Scriptures”

2 Peter 3:15–16 is this chapter's principal later-membership text. Chapter 50 used it as relationship of standing for apostolic testimony. The present chapter uses it as corpus-naming. The functions are distinct. Shared wording is not duplication of assignment.

> **“And account that the longsuffering of our Lord is salvation; even as our beloved brother Paul also according to the wisdom given unto him hath written unto you; As also in all his epistles, speaking in them of these things; in which are some things hard to be understood, which they that are unlearned and unstable wrest, as they do also the other scriptures, unto their own destruction.”**

The sentence must be read in its letter-context. Chapter 3 concerns the promise of His coming, scoffers, the patience of the Lord, and the charge to be found in peace. Paul's writings are adduced as already known to the readers, speaking of these things. Some things in them are hard to be understood. The unlearned and unstable wrest them. They do so **as they do also the other scriptures**.

**τὰς λοιπὰς γραφάς (*tas loipas graphas*)** — the other scriptures.

Four marks follow.

First, **Paul's writings are writings**. “Hath written”; “all his epistles”; “speaking in them.” The object is a deposited literary class.

Second, **those writings can be wrested**. Wrestling is a treatment of a text. It assumes a public writing with a sense that can be distorted. The same operation is performed on the other scriptures. The parallel is the argument.

Third, **the other scriptures are the class beside which Paul's letters are placed**. “Other” is unintelligible if Paul's letters stand outside the scriptural category. One does not wrest a secular memo “as” one wrests the scriptures unless the memo is being treated in that class. The ὡς καί (*hōs kai*) — as also — is class-comparison.

Fourth, **the sentence is relationship of standing, not a closed list**. It does not enumerate Paul's titles. It does not enumerate the other scriptures. It does not narrate a synod. It locates a known apostolic literary class in the scriptural category alongside scriptures already so called. That is later membership at full force. It is not Chapter 52's recognition-history.

The inference that is warranted: later apostolic writing is treated in the record as belonging with the scriptures. The scriptural corpus, in the record's own naming, is not restricted to the earlier body once later writings have been deposited.

The inference that is not warranted: that 2 Peter 3:16 has issued the later twenty-seven-book list, or that every later Christian writing is thereby scripture, or that inspiration's guarantees have been finished. Category-membership of Paul's letters with the other scriptures is the sentence's force. The edges of the category remain the next chapter.

Thus:

> **2 Peter 3:15–16 locates Paul's letters in relation to the other scriptures as later members of the scriptural category. The naming is full force for corpus-membership. It is not a table of contents, not a licence for every later writing, and not the closed canon.**

---

# 51.29 1 Timothy 5:18: “The Scripture Saith” Across Earlier and Later

1 Timothy 5:17–18 exhibits the category-name spanning a Mosaic sentence and a sentence of the Lord.

> **“Let the elders that rule well be counted worthy of double honour, especially they who labour in the word and doctrine. For the scripture saith, Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treadeth out the corn. And, The labourer is worthy of his reward.”**

**λέγει γὰρ ἡ γραφή (*legei gar hē graphē*)** — for the scripture saith.

The first cited sentence is Deuteronomy 25:4: “Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn.” That is earlier written witness, Law, already Scripture. Paul uses it elsewhere of gospel-labourers (1 Corinthians 9:9–14). The Mosaic writing is treated as present force for the church's honouring of those who labour in the word.

The second cited sentence — “The labourer is worthy of his reward” — matches the Lord's word as written in Luke 10:7: “for the labourer is worthy of his hire.” Matthew 10:10 has the related sentence, “for the workman is worthy of his meat.” The wording in 1 Timothy stands nearer Luke's form.

What is explicit: ἡ γραφή introduces two sentences, the second joined by “and,” as what the scripture saith. The first is Moses. The second is the Lord's word concerning the labourer.

What is inferred, at proportional force: a sentence of the Lord, known in the churches and written in the gospel-narrative, is treated under the same introduction as Moses. Later witness of the Lord's word is thereby named inside the scriptural category alongside the Law.

What is not established: that the sentence has voted Luke as a titled book in a closed list of twenty-seven, or that every dominical saying is thereby a separate scripture, or that questions of the letter's setting have been tried as a dedicated historical file. The force is naming across earlier and later, not enumeration.

The proportional discipline matters. If the second clause were treated as certainly a formal quotation of a finished gospel-title, the chapter would outrun the wording. If the second clause were treated as certainly not scripture because the noun γραφή had already been used of Moses, the chapter would ignore the grammar that joins the two under one introduction. The warranted middle is the grammar: one “the scripture saith,” two sentences, earlier law and later word of the Lord as written and known.

Thus:

> **1 Timothy 5:18 uses ἡ γραφή of a Mosaic sentence and a sentence matching the Lord's written word. That is the record naming earlier and later under one scriptural introduction. It is membership-grammar. It is not a synod.**

---

# 51.30 2 Timothy 3:15–16 as Category, Not Guarantees

The paragraph already examined for holy writings must now be placed in the later-membership argument without stealing Chapters 56–57.

Timothy's childhood writings are the earlier body. They are able to make him wise unto salvation through faith in Christ Jesus. The earlier corpus is already ordered toward the later gospel. Verse 16 then says πᾶσα γραφή is God-breathed and profitable.

Chapter 31 established that “all” is comprehensive within the referent designated by γραφή, that the immediate childhood-context is the earlier writings, and that the category is not thereby reduced to “some.” The present chapter adds only the corpus-placement:

If γραφή is the category of Scripture, and if 2 Peter 3:16 locates later apostolic writings with the other scriptures, then the category in 2 Timothy 3:16 is not a wall against later membership. The childhood writings identify the earlier body Timothy already knew. The category-name is Scripture as such. Later writings treated as γραφή elsewhere belong to the same category. They do not belong because this chapter has closed a list. They belong because the record uses the name of them.

What this use does not do: it does not treat θεόπνευστος as the finished account of what inspiration guarantees; it does not treat profitability for doctrine as the infallibility verdict; it does not treat “all scripture” as a silent list of sixty-six English titles. Category is in force. Guarantees remain later. The list remains later.

Thus:

> **2 Timothy 3:15–16 supplies the earlier body as holy writings known from childhood, and γραφή as the comprehensive category. Later membership is not proved from this paragraph alone. It is permitted by the category and shown from the record's other namings. Guarantees remain the later file.**

# 51.31 How the Record Names and Uses “the Scriptures” Across Both

The names must now be seen together, earlier and later, without flattening.

**Of the earlier body the record says:**

- search the scriptures; they testify of Me;
- have ye not read in the scriptures;
- ye do err, not knowing the scriptures;
- beginning at Moses and all the prophets, He expounded in all the scriptures;
- law of Moses, prophets, psalms;
- holy scriptures in which the gospel was promised;
- the law and the prophets as witnessing writings;
- whatsoever things were written aforetime, the comfort of the scriptures;
- the book of the law; the book of the prophet; the book of Psalms;
- the scripture saith (of Moses, of Abraham, of Pharaoh, of Elijah).

**Of the later writings the record says:**

- it seemed good to write in order, that you might know the certainty;
- these are written, that you might believe;
- this is the disciple who testifies and wrote;
- this epistle is to be read; interchange the letters;
- Paul has written; all his epistles;
- they wrest them as they do also the other scriptures;
- the scripture saith — Moses, and the labourer worthy of his reward.

**Across both the record does not say:**

- that later writing replaces earlier writing;
- that earlier writing is a closed room;
- that law, prophet, psalm, gospel, and letter are one literary form;
- that a later synod created the first fact of Scripture;
- that the English word *Bible* is the biblical lemma.

The pattern is inspectable. The earlier body is named as scriptures, books, law, prophets, psalms, holy writings. The later writings are deposited as books and letters, located according to the earlier scriptures, and named in relation to the other scriptures. The same category-word spans a passage, a book, the collected earlier body, and later apostolic writings as “other” members of that category. Span is not flattening. Span is one body with distinguishable witnesses.

Thus:

> **The record names and uses γραφή / the scriptures of the earlier body as standing Holy Writ, and of later apostolic writings as belonging with the other scriptures. That double use, without replacement and without identity of form, is the linguistic warrant for one scriptural corpus under investigation.**

# 51.32 Sequence Without Flattening

Sequence must be stated as a positive architecture, not as a hedge.

God spoke in time past unto the fathers by the prophets. He has spoken in these last days by His Son (Hebrews 1:1–2). The Son confirms the prophets' writings. The Son sends witnesses. The witnesses preach the Son according to the scriptures. The witnesses write. Their writings are spoken of with the other scriptures.

That is sequence:

> **prophetic writing → the Son who fulfills and confirms → apostolic witness → apostolic writing → later writing treated with the other scriptures.**

Flattening would erase the arrows. It would treat Moses as if he wrote a gospel, or John as if he wrote Sinai. The record refuses that erasure. Moses is read as Moses. Isaiah is opened as Isaiah. A later letter is a letter. The body collects them as Scripture. It does not assign them one office.

The distinction of offices is already in the names: law, prophets, psalms, gospel-narrative, epistle. The distinction of times is already in “aforetime” and “now.” The distinction of mediators is already in prophets and Son and apostles. None of those distinctions is a second corpus. All of them are internal to the one body.

Chapter 54 will examine continuity among the witnesses as a dedicated file. The present chapter needs only this much: sequence is how the body is in fact given; flattening is not how the body is named.

Thus:

> **One body is compatible with sequence. Sequence is the order of giving and of witness. Flattening would be the denial of that order. The record preserves the order inside the category.**

---

# 51.33 Continuity Without Replacement

Replacement remains a live reduction and must be refused again as a corpus-claim, not as a second trial of Chapter 50.

If later writings replaced earlier writings, the scriptural corpus would be a succession of discarded libraries: Moses discarded by the prophets, the prophets discarded by the gospel, the gospel discarded by the letters. The record's sentences would then be unintelligible. “According to the scriptures” would mean “according to the writings we have just abolished.” “Search the scriptures” would be a trap. “Whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning” would be false.

The record's positive claim is continuity.

The gospel is promised beforehand in the holy scriptures (Romans 1:2).

Christ died and rose according to the scriptures (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).

The Bereans search the scriptures daily, whether those things were so (Acts 17:11). The later preaching is tested by the earlier writings. Testing is continuity. It is not replacement.

Paul persuades concerning Jesus out of the law of Moses and out of the prophets (Acts 28:23). The later witness argues from the earlier body. Argument from a discarded book is theatre.

The raised Lord opens Moses, the prophets, and the psalms concerning Himself. Opening is continuity of subject.

Later writings hand on that opening. They do not close the earlier books.

Continuity is not Chapter 54's dedicated unity-file. It is the membership-relation: later witnesses belong with earlier witnesses because they testify to the same Christ of the same writings, and because they are named with those writings as scriptures.

Thus:

> **Later written witnesses do not replace earlier written witnesses. They belong with them. Replacement would make the record's own citations unintelligible. Continuity of subject and of name is the corpus-relation.**

---

# 51.34 Without Constructing the List

The chapter must now state, as a positive limit of assignment, what it has not done with titles.

It has identified the earlier body as Law, Prophets, Psalms — a named, public, already-standing scriptural corpus, coherent with a known threefold Jewish division, without enumerating every later title at the edges of the Writings.

It has identified the later body as apostolic deposition: gospel-narrative, written preaching, letters as a class, located with the other scriptures, without enumerating twenty-seven titles.

It has not listed Genesis through Malachi as a later Protestant table and called the table this chapter's achievement.

It has not listed Matthew through Revelation as a later Protestant table and called the table this chapter's achievement.

It has not adjudicated disputed edges.

It has not narrated later synods.

It has not treated a particular printed edition as the corpus.

Those refusals are sequence, not poverty. A body can be identified as the writings the record treats as Scripture before every title is settled. Chapter 15 could identify the Bible as a body of writings before canon was examined. Chapter 31 could establish “all Scripture” before the list was closed. The present chapter can identify earlier and later witnesses as one scriptural body before Chapter 52 asks which writings constitute the authoritative corpus.

Inflation would say: the named body is already the closed list; Movement VII is finished. Evacuation would say: because the list is not closed, there is no body. Both fail. The record names a body. The next chapter asks the constitution of the authoritative corpus as recognition.

Thus:

> **Corpus-identification is not list-construction. The body is named and used. The titles at the edges, and the history of recognizing them, remain Chapter 52. Absence of a synod in this file is not absence of a scriptural body.**

# 51.35 Positive Warrant for One Scriptural Body Under Investigation

The warranted conclusion of membership can now be stated before the reductions are catalogued.

The investigator is warranted, with full force, in treating earlier and later written witnesses as one scriptural body under investigation, at the level earned so far, for the following reasons.

The writings are authentic testimony (Movement V).

The Person who is the Truth confirms Law, Prophets, and Psalms as binding written word (Chapter 48). Those writings already stand as Scripture in Israel's public life and in His mouth.

That Person sends apostles whose testimony authenticates the New Testament witness as continuous with those Scriptures rather than as a rival corpus (Chapters 49–50).

The record names the earlier writings as the scriptures, holy scriptures, law and prophets, psalms, books.

The record deposits later writings as books and letters of the same Christ, located according to the scriptures.

The record names later apostolic writings in relation to the other scriptures, and introduces a Mosaic sentence and a sentence of the Lord's word under “the scripture saith.”

Therefore the object now before the investigation is one scriptural corpus: the body of writings the biblical record itself treats as Scripture, earlier and later, collected without being flattened, continuous without replacement, named without being listed.

That warrant is not the closed canon. It is not infallibility. It is not the Graphē-gate. It is the opening verdict of Movement VII: there is a scriptural body to recognize, and it is this body, earlier and later, not two rival libraries and not a later decree's invention.

Thus:

> **Earlier and later written witnesses are one scriptural body under investigation. The warrant is the record's own treatment, naming, and use, on premises already in force. The next question is recognition of which writings constitute the authoritative corpus. That question is later because it is different, not because this body is in doubt.**

# 51.36 Competing Claim: The Corpus Is a Later Church-List

The first live reduction is to identify the scriptural corpus with a later ecclesiastical catalogue.

The claim has a real historical object. Later churches did list books. They distinguished received, disputed, and refused writings. A printed table of sixty-six titles is a familiar later convenience in some communions; other communions have printed other tables. Those facts belong to recognition-history.

The claim fails as an account of this chapter's object.

It reverses the order of the record. Moses is Scripture in the Lord's mouth before any later Christian list exists. The prophets are read in the synagogue as Scripture. The psalms are named with the law and the prophets as the things written concerning Him. Paul's letters are spoken of with the other scriptures in a first-generation letter. A later list may acknowledge that body. It does not create the treatment.

It confuses two questions. “What body does the record treat as Scripture?” is not “Which titles did later assemblies print?” The second question is real. It is Chapter 52. Using it as the definition of corpus makes Movement VII begin at the end.

It cannot explain the record's own names. γραφή, the scriptures, the law and the prophets, the holy scriptures, the other scriptures — these are not later Latin decrees. They are the writings' own grammar.

If the reduction were true as a global account, this chapter would have nothing to examine until a later corporation spoke. The writings would be an undefined heap. They are not. They are already a named body.

Thus:

> **A later church-list is not the scriptural corpus. It may recognize a corpus. Recognition is the next chapter. The body the record treats as Scripture is already before the investigation.**

---

# 51.37 Competing Claim: Two Rival Corpora

The second live reduction is to treat earlier and later writings as two sacred libraries, two religions, or two gods' books, later bound together by accident or by ecclesiastical policy.

The claim can take a hostile form: the New Testament is a later invention that hijacked Israel's scriptures. It can take a pious form: the Old Testament is a discarded first volume which the gospel has replaced. Both forms are rivalry.

Rivalry fails as a global account of the conjunction already in force.

The Lord confirms the earlier writings as binding word. A hijack does not confirm. A replacement does not open Moses, the prophets, and the psalms concerning Himself after being raised.

The apostolic witness locates the gospel according to the scriptures, argues from the law and the prophets, and names later letters with the other scriptures. A second library does not cite the first as the court of its own gospel.

The earlier writings are said to be for the later people's learning and hope. A discarded volume is not comfort of the scriptures.

Chapter 50 already refused later invention of a rival corpus as a global account of the New Testament witness. The present chapter states the corpus-consequence: if the later witness is not a rival corpus, the later writings are not a rival corpus. They are later members.

Local difficulties of interpretation, apparent tensions, and later misuse of either body are not this reduction. Difficulty is not schism of corpus. Chapter 38 already distinguished coherence from the erasure of difficulty. Chapter 54 remains the later unity-file. Rivalry as a global account of two libraries fails now.

Thus:

> **Earlier and later writings are not two rival corpora. Confirmation, according-to-the-scriptures location, and naming with the other scriptures are unintelligible as rivalry. Sequence inside one body is the record's claim.**

---

# 51.38 Competing Claim: Flattening into One Undifferentiated Pile

The third live reduction is to treat one body as one indistinguishable sheet.

The claim can take a pious form: all scripture is the same sentence in different fonts. It can take a critical form: if they are one body, they must be one author, one genre, one date, or the claim of unity is false. Both forms erase the record's names.

Flattening fails because the record articulates the body.

It names law, prophets, and psalms.

It names this scripture and the scriptures.

It names a prophet's book as a book among books.

It names later letters as epistles in relation to other scriptures, which is relation, not identity of form.

It names times: in time past; in these last days; written aforetime; now made manifest.

It names mediators: Moses; the prophets; the Son; the apostles.

One category with internal distinctions is ordinary in the record. πᾶσα γραφή is comprehensive within the category without making a genealogy into a proverb. Chapter 31 already established literary diversity inside the category. The present chapter uses that establishment: diversity of witness is not evidence against one body. It is the body's actual shape.

Thus:

> **One scriptural body is not one undifferentiated pile. The record's own names preserve Law, Prophets, Psalms, and later apostolic writings as distinguishable witnesses in one category. Flattening is not loyalty to “all Scripture.” It is refusal to read the names.**

---

# 51.39 Competing Claim: Only the Earlier Writings Are Scripture

The fourth live reduction is to restrict γραφή to the earlier body and to treat later apostolic writings as commentary, however valuable, outside the scriptural category.

The claim has a real observation inside it. The immediate childhood-writings of 2 Timothy 3:15 are the earlier body. Most New Testament uses of “the scriptures” in the Gospels and Acts designate that earlier body, because that is the body then being read and fulfilled. Observation of those uses is not the error. The error is the inference that later writings cannot belong to the category.

The inference fails.

2 Peter 3:16 locates Paul's letters with the other scriptures. The sentence is unintelligible as a comparison of commentary with scripture. Wrestling them as they wrest the other scriptures is class-comparison.

1 Timothy 5:18 introduces a Mosaic sentence and a sentence matching the Lord's word under “the scripture saith.” Restriction of the noun to Moses alone must break the grammar that joins the second clause.

John writes a book that the reader might believe that Jesus is the Christ, of whom the earlier scriptures testify. Luke writes that a later reader might know the certainty of what was delivered. Those acts are the later body's life. Combined with the namings, they are membership, not commentary-status.

The reduction also contradicts Chapter 50. If later writings are merely commentary, then apostolic testimony does not authenticate a New Testament witness as witness in relation to the scriptures. It produces only notes on a closed first volume. The writings do not present the sent as annotators of a discarded case. They present them as witnesses whose word is the means of later belief, and whose letters stand with the other scriptures.

Thus:

> **The earlier body is Scripture. It is not the whole scriptural category once later writings have been deposited and named with the other scriptures. Restriction of γραφή to Moses, the prophets, and the psalms after 2 Peter 3:16 is not fidelity to the earlier writings. It is refusal of the record's later naming.**

---

# 51.40 Competing Claim: This Chapter Already Is Canon-Recognition

The fifth live reduction is to treat corpus-identification as already Chapter 52.

The claim is tempting because the words are near. Corpus, canon, recognition, list, authority — later English often uses them as one pile. The book's architecture refuses the pile.
