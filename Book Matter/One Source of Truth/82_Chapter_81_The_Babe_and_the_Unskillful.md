# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 81 — The Babe and the Unskillful

> **Movement X — The Scholarly Labour of the Graphē**

> **Central objective:** Examine the biblical diagnosis of immaturity and unskillfulness without reducing it to age or institutional status. Develop Hebrews 5:11–14 as the principal passage; distinguish the babe from the unskilful, distinguish lack of development from lack of intelligence, examine the consequences of prolonged immaturity, and establish why the learner must develop capacity to handle the Word before advancing to the discipline of rightly dividing it.

---

## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 74 established the scholarly impetus of Scripture.

Chapter 75 established perception, learning, and understanding.

Chapter 76 established reasoning through the Word.

Chapter 77 established quotation, meaning, and knowledge.

Chapter 78 established **σπουδάζω — diligence, endeavour, labour, study**.

Chapter 79 established exercise through responsible use.

Chapter 80 established maturity, skill, strong meat, and trained discernment.

The sequence has therefore advanced:

> **Perceive → Learn → Understand → Reason → Quote → Know → Diligence → Use → Exercise → Skill → Maturity → Discernment.**

But the positive description of maturity creates a necessary negative question.

If maturity is developed capacity, what does the text say about the person who has not developed?

If the mature are skilled, what is the condition of the unskilful?

If the mature are able to receive strong meat, what characterizes the babe who still requires milk?

If time creates an expectation of development, what does it mean when development has not occurred?

These questions belong to Chapter 81.

The governing passage remains Hebrews 5:11–14.

The writer says:

> “Of whom we have many things to say, and hard to be uttered, seeing ye are dull of hearing.”

Then:

> “For when for the time ye ought to be teachers, ye have need that one teach you again which be the first principles of the oracles of God…”

Then:

> “And are become such as have need of milk, and not of strong meat.”

Then:

> “For every one that useth milk is unskilful in the word of righteousness: for he is a babe.”

The passage does not merely describe an innocent stage of biological development.

It diagnoses a condition of learners in relation to what they ought by now to be capable of receiving and handling.

That distinction is essential.

A babe is not condemned for being a babe at the beginning of life.

The problem in Hebrews is that those addressed have had sufficient time that they ought to have developed beyond the condition being described.

The issue is therefore not infancy by itself.

The issue is **remaining in infancy when development ought to have occurred**.

This chapter must therefore distinguish:

> **Babe ≠ stupid.**

> **Babe ≠ worthless.**

> **Babe ≠ incapable of all learning.**

> **Babe ≠ permanently immature.**

And likewise:

> **Unskilful ≠ ignorant of everything.**

> **Unskilful ≠ unintelligent.**

> **Unskilful ≠ uneducated in every field.**

> **Unskilful ≠ incapable of becoming skilled.**

The biblical diagnosis concerns developmental capacity in relation to the Word.

That is the question.

---

# 81.1 The Text Gives the Diagnosis

Hebrews 5:11–14 must be allowed to speak in its own order.

The writer begins with a subject he wants to explain.

The subject concerns Christ's priesthood after the order of Melchisedec.

But there is a problem.

The difficulty is not presented first as a defect in the subject.

The writer says the subject is difficult to explain **because of the hearers' condition**.

This is significant.

The text therefore connects the complexity of teaching with the capacity of the learner.

The writer does not simply say:

> “This doctrine is difficult.”

He says, in effect:

> “There is much to say, but your condition makes it difficult to communicate it.”

Then he explains why.

They are dull of hearing.

They have had sufficient time that they ought to be teachers.

They need elementary instruction again.

They require milk rather than strong meat.

They are unskilful in the word of righteousness.

They are babes.

The argument moves from:

> **hearing → time → expected development → regression or deficiency → milk → unskilfulness → babe.**

The order matters.

The word “babe” should not be detached from the diagnosis that surrounds it.

---

# 81.2 “Babe” Is a Developmental Description

The Greek noun is:

> **νήπιος — nēpios**

The term is used for a child or infant and can also function figuratively for immaturity.

The immediate context supplies the figurative force.

The writer contrasts:

> **νήπιος**

with:

> **τελείων**

The contrast is developmental.

The babe has not reached the condition described as mature.

This does not require the conclusion that every use of νήπιος throughout the New Testament carries exactly the same nuance.

Words do not carry a single mechanical meaning independent of context.

The present passage supplies its own contrast.

The babe is the person who remains dependent upon milk and is described as unskilful in the word of righteousness.

The mature are those whose faculties have been trained through use toward discernment.

Therefore:

> **In Hebrews 5, “babe” identifies a developmental condition rather than a permanent identity.**

This matters because development is implied by the metaphor itself.

A babe is expected to grow.

---

# 81.3 A Babe Is Not an Insult

The metaphor can be abused.

Calling someone a “babe” can become a rhetorical weapon.

That is not the purpose of the text.

The writer is not merely insulting his audience.

He is diagnosing a condition that has consequences for their ability to receive instruction.

The metaphor is therefore functional.

It answers:

> What can this learner presently receive?

> What can this learner presently handle?

> What level of instruction is appropriate?

> What development should have occurred?

The point is not humiliation.

The point is diagnosis.

This distinction matters for teaching.

A teacher who calls a learner immature merely to assert superiority has not necessarily demonstrated maturity.

The biblical use of the metaphor requires the teacher to identify the actual developmental condition and its relation to the learner's capacity.

Thus:

> **A biblical diagnosis of immaturity should produce correction and development, not contempt.**

---

# 81.4 The Babe Is Still a Learner

The imagery of milk establishes something important.

The babe is not outside the process of learning.

The babe receives nourishment.

The babe participates.

The babe is being taught.

The problem is not that the babe can learn nothing.

The problem is that the babe is not yet capable of receiving what belongs to the mature.

This gives us a useful distinction:

> **Immaturity is not the absence of learning; it is insufficient development for the demands presently before the learner.**

That is why the Hebrews can simultaneously possess knowledge and remain unskilful.

They have heard.

They have learned.

They have been exposed.

They have received instruction.

Yet they have not developed proportionately to their opportunity.

The condition is therefore not simple emptiness.

It is arrested or deficient development.

---

# 81.5 The Babe and Dependence

The metaphor of milk also indicates dependence.

A babe depends upon nourishment supplied by another.

There is nothing inherently wrong with dependence at the proper developmental stage.

The problem occurs when a person remains dependent upon elementary instruction when he ought to have developed the capacity to teach or receive stronger instruction.

Hebrews 5:12 makes this explicit.

The writer says:

> “When for the time ye ought to be teachers…”

The expectation is development.

The criticism is therefore not:

> “You needed teaching.”

Everyone needs teaching.

The criticism is:

> **You still require elementary teaching when your development should have placed you beyond that condition.**

This is a much more precise diagnosis.

---

# 81.6 Everyone Remains Teachable

The distinction must not be pushed too far.

Maturity does not mean independence from all teachers.

Nor does being mature mean that one never receives instruction from another person.

The mature still learn.

The mature still listen.

The mature still compare.

The mature still correct themselves.

The mature still benefit from teachers.

The difference concerns capacity.

A mature learner can receive instruction without remaining permanently dependent upon another person's elementary explanation of every matter.

The mature learner can investigate.

He can reason.

He can compare.

He can test.

He can recognize the difference between what the teacher has actually established and what the teacher has merely asserted.

Therefore:

> **Maturity does not eliminate teachability; it makes teachability more responsible.**

---

# 81.7 The Unskilful: Ἄπειρος

Hebrews 5:13 uses:

> **ἄπειρος — apeiros**

The term can carry the sense of being inexperienced, unacquainted, or unskilled with respect to something.

The construction is:

> **ἄπειρος λόγου δικαιοσύνης**

The immediate point is not that the person has no words.

Nor is the point that the person has never heard Scripture.

The passage has already established that these people have received instruction.

The point concerns their lack of developed skill in relation to the Word of righteousness.

This is consistent with the movement established in Chapters 79 and 80.

Exercise develops capacity.

Capacity produces skill.

Skill contributes to maturity.

The opposite condition is therefore not necessarily absolute ignorance.

It is lack of developed competence.

Thus:

> **Unskilfulness is a deficiency of developed capacity in the use and handling of what has been received.**

---

# 81.8 Unskilful Does Not Mean Unintelligent

This distinction is essential.

Intelligence and skill are not identical.

A person may be naturally quick in reasoning and still be unskilful in a particular field.

A person may possess excellent memory and still be unskilful.

A person may be academically accomplished and still be unskilful in handling Scripture.

A person may speak confidently and still be unskilful.

A person may know many theological terms and still be unskilful.

Skill is developed through appropriate use.

Hebrews 5:14 connects mature capacity with trained faculties.

Therefore the question is not:

> “How intelligent is this person?”

The question is:

> **“What has this person become capable of doing with the Word through use?”**

That is a different question.

---

# 81.9 Unskilfulness Is Field-Specific

Skill is ordinarily related to a domain.

A person can be skilled in mathematics and unskilled in music.

A person can be skilled in engineering and unskilled in law.

A person can be skilled in business and unskilled in biblical interpretation.

Therefore “unskilful in the word of righteousness” should not be transformed into a judgment about the total intellectual worth of a person.

The passage concerns a particular field of capacity.

This protects the argument from unnecessary insult.

It also makes the diagnosis more useful.

The question becomes:

> Where is the skill lacking?

> What capacity has not developed?

> What practice has been missing?

> What distinctions are not yet being made?

> What kind of instruction is required?

That is how a developmental diagnosis becomes constructive.

---

# 81.10 The Word Can Be Possessed Without Being Skillfully Handled

This conclusion follows directly from the structure of the passage.

The Hebrews have the oracles of God.

They have received instruction.

They have had time.

Yet they are still called unskilful.

Therefore possession is not the same as skill.

This confirms what Chapter 77 established concerning quotation and knowledge.

A person may possess the words.

A person may quote the words.

A person may recognize the words.

A person may even affirm the words.

Yet the person may still lack developed capacity to handle their meaning, relationships, implications, and application.

The manuscript must therefore preserve the distinction:

> **Possession → Knowledge → Understanding → Skill**

These are related, but they are not interchangeable.

---

# 81.11 The Babe May Know Genuine Truth

Another error would be to assume that immaturity makes every statement of the immature false.

That does not follow.

A babe can know genuine truth.

A child can know that a proposition is true without possessing the maturity to reason through its wider implications.

A beginner can accurately repeat an established doctrine without being able to defend it.

A learner can know a verse without understanding all the arguments connected to it.

Therefore immaturity does not automatically invalidate everything a person says.

The question is proportionality.

What does the learner actually know?

What has the learner actually understood?

What can the learner responsibly infer?

What can the learner responsibly teach?

The mature discipline requires those questions to remain distinct.

---

# 81.12 Immaturity Can Produce Overreach

One danger of insufficient development is overreach.

A learner may possess one true proposition and then draw conclusions far beyond what the proposition establishes.

This was already examined in Chapter 76.

The mature learner asks:

> What does this establish?

And:

> What does this not establish?

The immature learner may not yet possess that restraint.

He may move rapidly from quotation to conclusion.

He may confuse association with entailment.

He may confuse possibility with necessity.

He may treat an illustration as proof.

He may treat a tradition as if it were Scripture.

He may treat a commentary as if it were the text itself.

Thus unskillfulness can become an epistemic danger.

---

# 81.13 Immaturity Can Produce Underreach

Overreach is not the only problem.

The immature learner may also underreach.

He may refuse to follow an argument because it requires effort.

He may stop at the first quotation.

He may say:

> “The Bible says it, therefore I need not understand the reasoning.”

But the earlier chapters have already established that the Graphē itself contains arguments and requires understanding.

Faithful submission to Scripture does not mean refusing to reason through what Scripture says.

Thus the immature learner can fail in two directions:

> **Overreach — claiming more than the text establishes.**

> **Underreach — refusing to follow what the text actually establishes.**

Skill requires both restraint and courage.

---

# 81.14 Immaturity and Repetition

Repetition can create the appearance of maturity.

A person may have heard the same doctrine for twenty years.

He may know the expected answer.

He may know the vocabulary.

He may know which verses are normally quoted.

But familiarity is not necessarily skill.

The Hebrews appear to have had substantial exposure.

Yet the writer says they need elementary things taught again.

Therefore:

> **Repeated exposure without corresponding development does not establish maturity.**

This is one of the most important lessons of the passage.

The purpose of repeated engagement with the Word is not merely to increase familiarity.

It is to develop capacity.

---

# 81.15 Familiarity Can Conceal Deficiency

A familiar statement can feel understood simply because it has been heard many times.

This creates an intellectual danger.

The learner hears:

> “Faith.”

> “Grace.”

> “Salvation.”

> “Kingdom.”

> “Righteousness.”

> “Spirit.”

> “Law.”

> “Truth.”

The words are familiar.

But familiarity with a word does not establish that its meaning has been correctly understood in every context.

Chapter 75 distinguished perception, learning, and understanding.

Chapter 77 distinguished quotation from complete understanding.

Chapter 80 distinguished information from maturity.

Chapter 81 must now add:

> **Familiarity is not skill.**

The learner must be able to use the concept accurately.

---

# 81.16 The Babe May Be Dependent Upon Formulas

One sign of immaturity can be dependence upon formulas.

The learner may know:

> “If this verse is mentioned, quote that verse.”

> “If this question is asked, give this answer.”

> “If this doctrine is challenged, repeat this phrase.”

Such memorized responses can be useful.

They are not useless.

But they do not by themselves demonstrate developed discernment.

A mature learner must know when the formula applies, why it applies, what it establishes, and where it stops.

Thus:

> **Memorized answers can support skill; they cannot substitute for skill.**

---

# 81.17 The Babe and the First Principles

Hebrews 5:12 says the hearers need to be taught again:

> “the first principles of the oracles of God.”

The phrase must not be confused with the Six First Principles that this book will address later.

The manuscript must preserve the distinction.

Hebrews is discussing foundational instruction in its immediate context.

The present book has a later architectural destination.

The Six First Principles belong to the structure that follows completion of this foundational work.

Therefore Chapter 81 should not prematurely identify Hebrews 5:12 with the later Six First Principles.

The text establishes a general developmental point:

> Learners who remain immature may need foundational instruction repeated.

That is enough for the present argument.

---

# 81.18 “Again” Matters

The writer says they need someone to teach them again.

The word “again” is important.

They are not encountering elementary instruction for the first time.

They have already encountered it.

The problem is therefore not merely absence of exposure.

Something has happened between instruction and development.

This raises a deeper question.

Why can a person hear repeatedly and remain unskilful?

The answer cannot simply be:

> “Because they did not hear.”

They did hear.

The passage itself says they became dull of hearing.

Therefore the issue includes the quality of their engagement.

They have not developed in proportion to the time and instruction available to them.

This is why the earlier sequence matters.

> **Exposure must become learning.**

> **Learning must become understanding.**

> **Understanding must become responsible use.**

> **Use must become trained capacity.**

Without this movement, repetition can remain repetition.

---

# 81.19 Dull Hearing

Hebrews 5:11 describes the hearers as:

> **νωθροί**

in relation to hearing.

The exact lexical range should not be expanded beyond the contextual point.

The writer identifies a sluggish or dull condition in their reception of the teaching.

This is important because the problem appears at the beginning of the intellectual chain.

Before the learner can reason through a proposition, he must attend to it.

Before he can understand, he must properly receive it.

Before he can exercise it, he must learn it.

Thus:

> **A failure at the stage of hearing can propagate through the entire chain of development.**

Poor attention can become poor understanding.

Poor understanding can become poor reasoning.

Poor reasoning can become poor practice.

Poor practice can become unskillfulness.

Therefore the condition of the babe is not disconnected from perception and learning.

---

# 81.20 Hearing Is Not Merely Auditory

In the biblical argument, “hearing” frequently carries more than the physical reception of sound.

A person can hear words without properly attending to what is being communicated.

The writer's concern is therefore intellectual and responsive.

This is consistent with the earlier chapters.

Perception is not understanding.

Hearing is not automatically learning.

Learning is not automatically skill.

The learner must move through the stages.

Thus a person can sit under teaching for years and still fail to develop if the teaching never becomes disciplined understanding and use.

---

# 81.21 The Problem of Delayed Development

Hebrews 5 contains an expectation.

> “For when for the time ye ought to be teachers…”

The phrase establishes a relationship between time and responsibility.

The writer is not saying:

> “Everyone should become a teacher immediately.”

He is addressing people whose time and prior exposure make teaching capacity an appropriate expectation.

This distinction prevents abuse.

Developmental expectations should be proportionate.

A new learner should not be condemned for being new.

A beginner should be allowed to begin.

The problem is prolonged immaturity when sufficient opportunity and instruction have already existed.

Therefore:

> **Biblical maturity is developmental, not instantaneous.**

---

# 81.22 Time Creates Responsibility

Time alone does not create maturity.

But time can create responsibility.

This is a subtle but important distinction.

If a learner has received substantial instruction over a long period, there may come a point at which continued inability to handle elementary material is no longer merely a neutral description of beginner status.

It becomes a developmental problem.

The writer of Hebrews uses exactly this logic.

The passage therefore warns against treating perpetual beginner status as harmless when development is expected.

The question is not:

> “Have I been around long enough to call myself mature?”

The question is:

> **“Given the opportunity and instruction I have received, what capacity should now reasonably have developed?”**

---

# 81.23 The Babe and Responsibility

Greater maturity brings greater responsibility.

But the immature also have responsibility appropriate to their stage.

The babe should learn.

The babe should receive nourishment.

The babe should develop.

The babe should not pretend to possess mature capacity that has not been developed.

This is especially important in interpretation.

A learner should be able to say:

> “I do not yet know.”

That statement can be intellectually stronger than a premature conclusion.

The immature learner becomes dangerous when he mistakes his present capacity for the final measure of truth.

---

# 81.24 Unskilfulness and the Word of Righteousness

The phrase “word of righteousness” deserves restraint.

The immediate passage does not invite an elaborate speculative definition.

The writer's point is that the learner is unskilful in relation to this Word.

The term therefore belongs within the developmental argument.

The person needs milk because he is a babe.

The babe is unskilful in the word of righteousness.

The mature receive strong meat because their faculties are trained.

The two conditions are contrasted.

Therefore:

> **The passage presents the Word as something in relation to which human capacity can be more or less developed.**

The Word itself does not change.

The learner changes.

This distinction is fundamental.

---

# 81.25 The Word Does Not Become Stronger Because the Learner Matures

“Strong meat” can create the false impression that the content itself changes from weak to strong.

The metaphor is relational.

The food is solid.

The learner's capacity determines whether solid food is appropriate.

Likewise, Scripture does not become more authoritative as the learner matures.

Truth does not become truer.

The Graphē does not become more authoritative.

The learner becomes more capable of receiving and handling what was already authoritative.

Therefore:

> **Maturity changes the learner's capacity, not the ontological status of the Word.**

This preserves the authority established earlier in the book.

---

# 81.26 The Babe Cannot Make His Condition the Standard

An immature learner may make an elementary understanding the measure of all truth.

If he cannot understand a proposition, he may conclude:

> “It cannot be true.”

But that inference is invalid.

Difficulty of comprehension is not evidence of falsehood.

Likewise, if a mature learner understands something that a beginner does not, the mature learner has not thereby created a new truth.

Both errors arise when the learner's capacity becomes the measure of reality.

The proper order is:

> **Truth → Word → understanding → developed capacity.**

Not:

> **my present capacity → therefore truth.**

---

# 81.27 The Babe and the Authority of the Teacher

The immature learner may become excessively dependent upon a teacher.

Because he cannot yet reason through the Word independently, he may treat the teacher's explanation as if it were the Word itself.

This creates a serious danger.

The teacher may be useful.

The teacher may be correct.

The teacher may possess developed skill.

But the teacher is not thereby transformed into the Graphē.

The distinction established in Chapter 66 remains:

> **Authority of Scripture ≠ authority of interpretation.**

The mature learner must eventually be capable of distinguishing the teacher's quotation, interpretation, inference, and application.

That distinction is one of the marks of development.

---

# 81.28 The Opposite Danger: Rejecting Every Teacher

The answer to dependence is not rebellion against teaching.

An immature learner can also misuse the language of independence:

> “I only need the Bible.”

But if this statement means:

> “I refuse to learn from anyone,”

it contradicts the very reality of learning.

The New Testament contains teachers.

Hebrews itself teaches through an author.

Paul teaches.

Jesus teaches.

The learner is expected to hear.

The problem is not receiving instruction from teachers.

The problem is making the teacher's interpretation the primary authority.

Thus:

> **Maturity is neither blind dependence nor arrogant isolation. It is responsible learning under the authority of the Word.**

---

# 81.29 The Babe and Correctability

A healthy learner can be corrected.

The babe should expect correction.

The mature learner should also remain correctable.

The distinction is not between:

> “those who need correction” and “those who do not.”

Everyone remains fallible as an interpreter.

The distinction concerns the developed capacity to recognize and respond to correction.

An unskilful learner may defend an interpretation simply because it is familiar.

A mature learner can examine the objection.

He can return to the text.

He can expose his premise.

He can revise his conclusion.

Therefore:

> **Correctability is compatible with maturity; refusing correction is not evidence of maturity.**

---

# 81.30 The Babe and Intellectual Pride

Immaturity can produce pride because the learner mistakes familiarity for mastery.

A person may know enough to speak confidently but not enough to recognize the limits of his own understanding.

This is dangerous.

The mature learner does not merely know what he believes.

He knows why he believes it.

He knows what the text establishes.

He knows what is inference.

He knows what remains uncertain.

He knows where his argument depends upon an additional premise.

This is why intellectual humility is not intellectual weakness.

It is disciplined proportion.

---

# 81.31 The Babe and Intellectual Laziness

The opposite of pride is not automatically diligence.

A learner can remain immature through laziness.

If he repeatedly refuses to investigate context, language, reasoning, and evidence, his lack of development should not surprise him.

The earlier chapters have established the labour required by Scripture.

Chapter 78 established **σπουδάζω**.

Chapter 79 established exercise.

Chapter 80 established trained faculties.

Chapter 81 therefore identifies the condition that results when the expected development has not occurred.

The problem is not merely lack of opportunity.

Where opportunity exists, failure to apply oneself can preserve immaturity.

---

# 81.32 The Babe and Information Hoarding

The learner can also mistake accumulation for development.

He may collect:

- books;
- sermons;
- notes;
- quotations;
- theological terms;
- Greek words;
- Hebrew words;
- doctrinal statements;
- historical facts;
- online lectures.

The collection may become enormous.

Yet the person may remain unable to reason through a passage.

This is precisely why the manuscript has distinguished information from maturity.

The test is functional.

Can the learner use what he possesses?

Can he distinguish?

Can he reason?

Can he explain?

Can he recognize a false inference?

Can he correct himself?

If not, accumulation has not yet become developed skill.

---

# 81.33 The Babe and Memorization

Memorization is valuable.

Scripture should be remembered.

Quotation can be necessary.

Memory can make comparison faster.

But memorization alone does not create mature discernment.

A learner may memorize dozens of passages and still misuse them.

Indeed, a memorized text can become a weapon for confirmation bias if the learner quotes only what supports a predetermined conclusion.

The mature learner asks:

> What does the whole passage say?

> What is the speaker doing?

> What is the scope?

> What is the context?

> What does the quotation actually establish?

Therefore:

> **Memory supplies material for thought; it does not replace thought.**

---

# 81.34 The Babe and Proof-Texting

Proof-texting is one expression of unskilfulness.

A learner finds a sentence that resembles his desired conclusion and treats it as conclusive proof without examining context or argumentative scope.

This can occur even when the quotation itself is perfectly accurate.

The error lies in handling.

Thus:

> **A correct verse can be used incorrectly.**

This was established in Chapter 77 and Chapter 76.

The babe is particularly vulnerable to this because he has not yet developed the trained capacity to distinguish quotation from argument.

---

# 81.35 The Babe and Context

Context is one of the basic disciplines required for development.

The learner must ask:

- Who is speaking?
- To whom?
- About what?
- Under what circumstances?
- What comes before?
- What comes after?
- What question is being answered?
- What proposition is actually being asserted?
- What is the scope of the statement?

Without these questions, the learner may treat isolated sentences as independent propositions.

This is not merely an academic problem.

It is a problem of skill.

The mature learner has developed habits that protect him from such errors.

---

# 81.36 The Babe and False Equivalence

Another form of unskillfulness is treating related things as identical.

For example:

> quotation = interpretation;

> interpretation = inference;

> inference = revelation;

> teacher = Scripture;

> familiarity = understanding;

> knowledge = maturity;

> confidence = certainty.

These equivalences are false.

The mature learner distinguishes categories.

The babe often collapses them.

Therefore the development from babe to mature includes the development of conceptual distinctions.

---

# 81.37 The Babe and Necessary Distinctions

A learner cannot reason accurately without distinctions.

The book has repeatedly demonstrated this.

Truth must be distinguished from belief.

Evidence from authority.

Quotation from interpretation.

Understanding from inference.

Inference from revelation.

Skill from status.

Maturity from age.

These distinctions are not unnecessary complexity.

They are instruments of accuracy.

Thus:

> **One mark of development is the ability to preserve distinctions that matter.**

---

# 81.38 The Babe and Premature Synthesis

The immature learner may combine passages too quickly.

He sees similar vocabulary and assumes identical meaning.

He sees a common theme and assumes identical argument.

He sees two doctrines that appear related and immediately constructs a synthesis.

But synthesis requires discipline.

The learner must establish:

> similarity;

> relationship;

> relevance;

> compatibility;

> scope;

> inference.

Without this work, synthesis becomes association.

The mature learner can say:

> “These passages may be related, but I must first establish how.”

That restraint is skill.

---

# 81.39 The Babe and Tradition

Tradition can make immaturity difficult to detect.

A learner may possess a traditional answer that has been repeated for generations.

Because many people say it, the answer feels established.

But institutional repetition is not proof.

The mature learner returns to the text.

He asks:

> Where is this stated?

> What does the passage mean?

> What inference is being made?

> What historical claim supports it?

> What additional premise has entered?

The babe may simply repeat:

> “This is what we have always believed.”

The mature learner investigates.

---

# 81.40 The Babe and Institutional Confidence

Institutional confidence can produce the same problem.

If a doctrine is attached to an institution, denomination, academic school, or respected teacher, the learner may feel no need to examine it.

But the authority of the institution does not automatically transfer to every interpretation.

This returns us to the foundational distinction:

> **The authority of the Graphē is not identical to the authority of an institution's interpretation of the Graphē.**

The mature learner can respect a teacher or institution without surrendering the duty to examine.

---

# 81.41 The Babe and Majority Agreement

Majority agreement can also conceal unskillfulness.

If ten teachers say the same thing, the learner may assume that the conclusion is established.

But ten repetitions do not automatically become one proof.

The mature learner asks what evidence supports the conclusion.

He distinguishes independent evidence from repeated testimony.

He distinguishes consensus from demonstration.

This is not a rejection of consensus.

Consensus can be informative.

But consensus is not automatically authority.

---

# 81.42 The Babe and Novelty

The opposite error is equally possible.

The immature learner may assume that anything new is deeper.

A new interpretation can appear sophisticated simply because it is unfamiliar.

Novelty is not proof.

A mature learner therefore evaluates both tradition and innovation by the same discipline:

> **What does the text establish?**

> **What evidence supports the interpretation?**

> **What reasoning connects the evidence to the conclusion?**

The mature learner is neither captive to tradition nor addicted to novelty.

---

# 81.43 The Babe and Emotional Force

Emotional intensity can also be mistaken for truth.

A sermon may be moving.

A teacher may be passionate.

A testimony may be powerful.

A doctrine may produce strong feelings.

None of these by itself establishes the proposition being taught.

The mature learner does not despise emotion.

He simply refuses to confuse emotional force with evidential force.

This is another form of discernment.

---

# 81.44 The Babe and Difficult Passages

The mature learner does not panic when encountering a difficult passage.

He recognizes that difficulty is a reason for labour, not an automatic reason for rejection.

The Hebrews passage itself demonstrates this.

The writer has much to say about Melchisedec, but the audience's condition makes the explanation difficult.

The answer is not to abandon the subject.

The answer is development.

This is one reason Movement X matters.

The book is training the reader for difficult intellectual work.

The reader must become capable of remaining with a difficult question long enough to investigate it.

---

# 81.45 The Babe and the Demand for Simplicity

There is a legitimate place for simple explanation.

But simplicity must not become an excuse for avoiding distinctions.

Some truths can be stated simply.

Some arguments require development.

Some passages contain conditions, qualifications, contrasts, and relationships that cannot responsibly be reduced to a slogan.

The mature learner knows when simplification preserves meaning and when simplification destroys it.

Therefore:

> **Simplicity is a virtue when it clarifies; it is a defect when it removes necessary structure.**

---

# 81.46 The Babe and the Demand for Complexity

The opposite error is to make everything complicated.

The immature learner may believe that complexity itself proves sophistication.

He may introduce terminology that the text does not require.

He may build systems where a simpler explanation is sufficient.

This is not maturity.

The mature learner can distinguish what requires complexity from what does not.

He does not add premises merely to appear profound.

He follows the text.

Therefore:

> **Maturity is not maximum complexity; it is proportionate complexity.**

---

# 81.47 The Babe and the Limits of Present Capacity

A learner must know the limits of his present capacity.

This is not defeat.

It is an accurate starting point.

If the learner cannot yet distinguish an inference from an explicit statement, he needs training in that distinction.

If he cannot follow a long argument, he needs practice reconstructing arguments.

If he cannot evaluate competing interpretations, he needs practice identifying premises.

If he cannot recognize contextual scope, he needs practice reading complete passages.

The solution to unskillfulness is development.

---

# 81.48 Development Requires Practice

The mature person in Hebrews is not mature because he possesses a mystical gift.

His senses have been exercised.

This returns the argument to Chapter 79.

The faculties are trained through use.

Therefore development requires practice.

Practice means repeated engagement under appropriate discipline.

It is not random repetition.

A person can practice an error.

Therefore practice must be corrected by the authoritative Word.

The sequence is:

> **Word → Use → Correction → Repeated use → Trained capacity → Discernment.**

This preserves the authority of the Word while explaining the development of the learner.

---

# 81.49 The Babe Can Practice the Wrong Thing

Practice alone is not sufficient.

A person who repeatedly misreads Scripture can become highly skilled at reproducing his error.

This is why exercise must remain under authority.

The mature learner does not merely become more efficient.

He becomes more accurate.

Thus:

> **Training without truth can produce trained error.**

The object of biblical exercise is not simply competence.

It is faithful competence.

---

# 81.50 The Babe and Correction of Practice

Because practice can reinforce error, correction matters.

The learner must compare his conclusions with the text.

He must test his reasoning.

He must examine counterexamples.

He must revisit assumptions.

He must allow the evidence to correct him.

This is why self-correction was included in Chapter 76.

A learner who cannot revise is not demonstrating maturity.

The goal is not to become impossible to correct.

The goal is to become increasingly capable of detecting and correcting one's own errors.

---

# 81.51 The Babe and the Illusion of Mastery

One of the most dangerous states is false mastery.

The learner thinks he has mastered a subject because he can discuss it fluently.

Fluency is useful.

It is not conclusive.

The real test is whether the learner can handle unfamiliar questions.

Can he explain why his conclusion follows?

Can he identify the premise he is using?

Can he recognize when a passage does not support his claim?

Can he distinguish what is certain from what is probable?

Can he revise his position when evidence changes?

These are stronger tests of developed capacity.

---

# 81.52 The Babe and Unfamiliar Questions

A learner who has memorized answers may perform well when asked familiar questions.

An unfamiliar question reveals whether the learner has developed underlying skill.

This is analogous to exercise.

If the learner only knows the exact path already practiced, he may not yet possess general discernment.

The mature learner has developed transferable capacity.

He can approach a new passage with the same disciplines:

> perceive;

> understand;

> identify propositions;

> examine context;

> identify premises;

> reason;

> test;

> conclude.

This is why Movement X is not merely about accumulating biblical information.

It is about intellectual formation under the Word.

---

# 81.53 The Babe and Transfer of Skill

Skill becomes mature when it can be transferred appropriately.

A learner who can analyze one verse but cannot apply the same discipline to another has limited development.

A learner who can identify an argument in one passage but not another has partial skill.

The mature learner develops a method that remains faithful across contexts.

But transfer must not become mechanical.

The learner must still respect the uniqueness of each text.

Thus:

> **Mature skill is transferable discipline, not mechanical formula.**

---

# 81.54 The Babe and Discernment Between Good and Evil

Hebrews 5:14 identifies the immediate result:

> discernment of good and evil.

The language is broad.

The chapter must not reduce it to one narrow application.

The important point is discrimination.

The mature learner can distinguish.

He is not simply presented with material and left unable to evaluate it.

He has trained faculties.

Therefore the developmental objective is not merely more information.

It is increased discrimination.

This is why unskillfulness matters.

Without skill, information can remain undifferentiated.

---

# 81.55 Discernment Is Not Suspicion

Discernment should not be confused with permanent distrust.

A suspicious person may reject everything.

That is not necessarily discernment.

Discernment distinguishes accurately.

It can say:

> true;

> false;

> established;

> inferred;

> possible;

> uncertain;

> relevant;

> irrelevant;

> stronger;

> weaker;

> explicit;

> implicit.

This is why discernment requires understanding and reasoning.

Suspicion reacts.

Discernment examines.

---

# 81.56 Discernment Is Not Cynicism

Cynicism assumes corruption.

Discernment tests claims.

The mature learner does not need to distrust a teacher merely because the teacher is a teacher.

Nor does he need to trust a teacher merely because the teacher is respected.

He examines.

This is the intellectual posture required by the architecture of the book.

The Graphē remains primary.

Human interpretation remains fallible.

Therefore the learner must develop enough skill to examine claims without becoming either gullible or cynical.

---

# 81.57 The Babe and Overconfidence

Overconfidence is particularly dangerous in interpretation because the learner can become certain before the argument is complete.

The mature learner asks:

> What evidence do I have?

> What premise am I assuming?

> Is the inference necessary?

> Could another interpretation explain the evidence?

> What would falsify my conclusion?

These questions do not weaken faith.

They discipline thought.

They keep the learner from making his confidence the measure of truth.

---

# 81.58 The Babe and Underconfidence

The opposite problem also exists.

A learner may become so intimidated by scholars, teachers, languages, or institutions that he assumes he cannot understand anything for himself.

That can create permanent dependence.

The biblical model does not require this.

The learner is expected to hear, learn, understand, reason, exercise, and mature.

The goal is not to make the learner permanently dependent upon specialists.

The goal is to develop responsible capacity.

Therefore:

> **Respect expertise without surrendering responsibility.**

---

# 81.59 The Babe and Language

Language can become a source of unnecessary dependence.

Greek and Hebrew are valuable tools.

Lexical analysis can clarify meaning.

But the existence of original-language scholarship does not mean that every ordinary reader is incapable of understanding Scripture.

Nor does knowledge of Greek automatically produce correct interpretation.

A scholar can commit an interpretive error.

A non-specialist can understand a proposition accurately.

The mature learner uses language tools proportionately.

He neither despises them nor worships them.

---

# 81.60 The Babe and Credentials

Credentials can indicate training.

They can be useful evidence of study.

But credentials are not a substitute for demonstration.

A person with credentials can still misread a passage.

A person without formal credentials can sometimes correctly understand a passage.

The proper question remains:

> What does the text establish?

Credentials may help establish expertise.

They do not transfer the authority of the Graphē to the credentialed person.

Thus the manuscript must preserve:

> **Expertise ≠ infallibility.**

---

# 81.61 The Babe and the Fear of Error

A learner can become so afraid of making an error that he refuses to reason.

This is another form of immaturity.

The solution is not recklessness.

It is disciplined provisional reasoning.

The learner can say:

> “This is what the evidence currently supports.”

He can continue investigating.

He can revise.

This is compatible with serious commitment to truth.

Indeed, the ability to revise in response to evidence is part of intellectual integrity.

---

# 81.62 The Babe and the Desire to Win

The immature learner may treat interpretation as competition.

The objective becomes:

> defeat the other person;

> prove superiority;

> preserve reputation;

> win the debate.

But the objective of the book is truth.

A person can win an argument while misunderstanding the text.

A person can lose a debate while possessing the stronger conclusion.

Therefore:

> **Argumentative victory is not the measure of biblical maturity.**

The mature learner seeks to know what the Word establishes.

---

# 81.63 The Babe and the Desire to Be Deep

Another immature motive is the desire to appear deep.

The learner wants to discover something nobody else has seen.

This can generate speculative interpretation.

But the Graphē does not become more authoritative because an interpretation is novel.

Depth must be earned.

The mature learner is willing to state an obvious conclusion when the evidence is obvious.

He is also willing to acknowledge a difficult conclusion when the evidence requires it.

Thus:

> **Depth is not obscurity.**

---

# 81.64 The Babe and the Desire to Be Approved by People

Social approval can distort interpretation.

A learner may say what his group expects.

He may avoid conclusions that threaten his standing.

He may accept a doctrine because respected people endorse it.

This can preserve immaturity even while increasing institutional conformity.

The mature learner must remain accountable to the truth.

That does not require disrespect.

It requires intellectual independence under the authority of the Word.

---

# 81.65 The Babe and Intellectual Independence

Intellectual independence does not mean inventing one's own truth.

It means taking responsibility for one's reasoning.

The mature learner can receive a conclusion from a teacher and then examine it.

He can say:

> “This is established by the text.”

Or:

> “This is the teacher's interpretation.”

Or:

> “This is a necessary inference.”

Or:

> “This is a possible application.”

Those distinctions are evidence of development.

---

# 81.66 The Babe and Application

Application can reveal unskillfulness.

The learner may take a legitimate principle and turn it into an absolute rule without establishing the additional premise.

Or he may take a historical command and apply it without examining its audience and circumstances.

Or he may turn an example into a universal command.

The mature learner distinguishes:

> text;

> principle;

> inference;

> application.

This distinction prepares the way for the later discipline of interpretation.

---

# 81.67 The Babe and Universal Claims

A learner can become unskilful by converting a local statement into a universal rule.

For example:

> “This happened here; therefore it must always happen.”

Or:

> “This command was given to them; therefore it applies identically to everyone.”

Such reasoning may sometimes be valid.

But it requires an argument.

The mature learner does not assume universality merely because a statement is found in Scripture.

He examines scope.

---

# 81.68 The Babe and Silence

The immature learner may reason from silence:

> “The text does not mention X, therefore X is forbidden.”

Or:

> “The text does not mention X, therefore X must be permitted.”

Neither conclusion automatically follows.

Silence can have evidential significance in some arguments, but its force must be demonstrated.

The mature learner asks:

> What conclusion does this silence actually warrant?

This is another application of Chapter 76's discipline of stopping where the evidence requires.

---

# 81.69 The Babe and Apparent Contradiction

An immature learner may encounter two passages that appear difficult to reconcile and immediately conclude that Scripture contradicts itself.

The mature learner pauses.

He examines:

- wording;
- context;
- speaker;
- audience;
- time;
- genre;
- scope;
- assumptions;
- translation;
- inference.

An apparent contradiction is a question to investigate.

It is not automatically a demonstrated contradiction.

The mature learner neither hides the difficulty nor exaggerates it.

---

# 81.70 The Babe and Translation

The immature learner may treat one English wording as though it were the only possible form of the text.

The mature learner recognizes the distinction between:

> underlying-language wording;

> translation;

> interpretation.

This does not mean that ordinary translations are useless.

It means that translation questions should be handled with precision.

A lexical argument should not be constructed merely from an English word's range when the underlying language is relevant.

Again:

> **Skill preserves distinctions.**

---

# 81.71 The Babe and Lexical Novelty

Knowing an original-language word can also become a form of immaturity when the learner assumes that displaying Greek or Hebrew settles the argument.

It does not.

A lexical meaning is not automatically the contextual meaning.

A dictionary definition is not automatically the author's intended sense in every passage.

The mature learner asks:

> What does the word mean?

Then:

> What sense does it carry here?

Then:

> How does the grammar and context constrain it?

The language tool serves interpretation.

It does not replace interpretation.

---

# 81.72 The Babe and Commentary

Commentaries can be useful.

They can preserve historical arguments, lexical observations, manuscript discussions, and interpretive proposals.

But a commentary remains a secondary source.

The immature learner may quote a commentary as though the commentary itself settles the biblical question.

The mature learner asks:

> What does the commentator claim?

> What evidence is offered?

> What passage supports it?

> What inference is being made?

> Is the conclusion stronger than the evidence?

Thus the commentary becomes an object of examination rather than a substitute authority.

---

# 81.73 The Babe and Scholarly Authority

Scholarship should be respected without being absolutized.

A scholar can know more about a particular field than the ordinary reader.

That expertise matters.

But expertise does not transform interpretation into revelation.

The mature learner can benefit from scholarship while preserving the distinction between:

> evidence;

> argument;

> interpretation;

> conclusion.

This is precisely the kind of capacity Movement X is designed to develop.

---

# 81.74 The Babe and Institutional Language

Institutions often develop technical vocabularies.

These vocabularies can make communication efficient.

But they can also conceal assumptions.

The mature learner must be able to translate technical language back into propositions.

What exactly is being claimed?

What does the term mean?

What follows?

What does not follow?

Where did the definition come from?

This prevents terminology from becoming a substitute for reasoning.

---

# 81.75 The Babe and the Need for Elementary Instruction

There is nothing shameful about elementary instruction.

The shame lies not in learning foundations.

The problem arises when a person refuses to progress beyond foundations when progression is required.

A mature person can return to fundamentals.

Indeed, mature reasoning often depends upon clear fundamentals.

The difference is that the mature person can use foundational truths as premises in further reasoning.

The babe remains dependent upon having the foundation explained every time.

Thus:

> **Maturity does not abandon fundamentals; it builds upon them.**

---

# 81.76 The Babe and First Principles

Foundations matter because complex reasoning depends upon them.

A learner who cannot distinguish basic categories will struggle with advanced synthesis.

This is why Hebrews treats the need to be taught elementary matters again as a sign of developmental deficiency.

The learner must possess enough foundation to move forward.

The architecture of this book follows the same principle.

The Doctrine of the Graphē establishes foundational distinctions before the reader enters the Six First Principles.

Therefore:

> **Advanced instruction without adequate foundations produces confusion rather than maturity.**

---

# 81.77 The Babe and Patience

Development takes time.

The book must not create a false expectation of instant mastery.

A learner may need repeated exposure.

He may need correction.

He may need to revisit difficult passages.

He may need to practice reasoning.

He may need to fail and revise.

The presence of difficulty does not prove failure.

The critical question is whether development is occurring.

Therefore:

> **Maturity is progressive, but progress must be real.**

---

# 81.78 The Babe and Regression

Hebrews uses language that suggests deterioration into a condition requiring elementary instruction again.

This warns that development is not automatic.

A person can regress.

A person can become dull.

A person can cease exercising his faculties.

A person can allow habits of careless interpretation to replace disciplined engagement.

Therefore maturity requires maintenance.

The trained faculty must continue to be exercised.

This is consistent with the broader biblical emphasis on endurance, continuance, and steadfastness.

---

# 81.79 The Babe and Habit

Chapter 79 examined **ἕξις** in connection with developed practice.

A settled habit can be formed in either direction.

Repeated careful use can develop skill.

Repeated careless use can reinforce carelessness.

Therefore habit itself is not sufficient.

The question is:

> **What kind of habit has been developed?**

The mature learner develops habits of:

- reading in context;
- checking claims;
- distinguishing quotation from interpretation;
- exposing assumptions;
- following arguments;
- testing conclusions;
- correcting errors.

These habits become intellectual disciplines.

---

# 81.80 The Babe and the Responsibility of Development

If the learner has received truth, opportunity, instruction, and time, development becomes a responsibility.

This does not mean that every learner must reach identical levels.

Human capacities and circumstances differ.

But the principle remains:

> **What has been entrusted creates corresponding responsibility.**

A learner who has been given much should not remain content with perpetual elementary repetition if development is possible.

The writer of Hebrews appeals to precisely this expectation.

---

# 81.81 The Babe and Teaching Others

The writer says they ought to be teachers.

Teaching is not the only possible evidence of maturity.

But in this passage it is an important indicator because teaching requires developed understanding.

To teach responsibly, a person must be able to:

> understand;

> explain;

> distinguish;

> answer;

> correct;

> recognize error;

> and communicate proportionately.

This does not mean every mature person occupies a public teaching office.

It means the capacity to communicate what has been learned is evidence that development has occurred.

---

# 81.82 Teaching Is Not Mere Repetition

A person can teach by repeating another person's words.

That can be useful.

But mature teaching requires understanding.

The teacher must know what the words mean.

He must know why the conclusion follows.

He must know where the argument depends upon inference.

He must know what objections can be raised.

He must know the limits of the claim.

Therefore:

> **Teaching reveals whether knowledge has become usable understanding.**

---

# 81.83 The Babe and Responsibility for Others

A person who teaches while remaining unskilful can transmit his own deficiencies.

This is why maturity matters beyond the individual.

Interpretive error can become communal error.

A weak inference can become doctrine.

A misunderstood verse can become tradition.

A speculative claim can become institutional teaching.

Therefore the development of skill is not merely self-improvement.

It is protection for those who receive instruction.

This makes the movement from babe to mature ethically significant.

---

# 81.84 The Babe and the Danger of Premature Authority

The learner should not claim authority merely because he has begun learning.

Likewise, the mature learner should not claim infallibility merely because he has developed skill.

The proper order is:

> **Authority belongs to the authoritative source.**

> **Responsibility belongs to the interpreter.**

> **Skill belongs to the developed learner.**

> **Correction remains possible.**

This preserves the central architecture of the manuscript.

---

# 81.85 The Babe and the Difference Between Capacity and Authority

This distinction deserves explicit emphasis.

A mature learner may have greater capacity.

That does not give him authority to change the text.

A skilled interpreter may offer a better-supported interpretation.

That does not make his interpretation Scripture.

A teacher may explain the Word accurately.

That does not make the teacher the source of the Word.

Therefore:

> **Maturity increases the capacity to serve truth; it does not create authority over truth.**

This is one of the most important safeguards against intellectual pride.

---

# 81.86 The Babe and the Goal of Growth

The goal is not to remain a babe.

The goal is development.

The goal is not to collect a permanent identity as “advanced.”

The goal is increasing capacity to receive, understand, use, discern, and communicate the Word faithfully.

The learner should therefore ask:

> Am I developing?

Not merely:

> How long have I been learning?

Not merely:

> How much do I know?

Not merely:

> How many books have I read?

Not merely:

> How many people agree with me?

The biblical question is functional.

> **What can I now faithfully do with what I have learned?**

---

# 81.87 From Babe and Unskilful to Rightly Dividing

The argument has now reached the next necessary question.

If the babe is unskilful, and if maturity involves trained faculties, then what does the mature learner actually do with the Word?

How does skill become responsible handling?

How does the learner move from general discernment to accurate division, distinction, and handling of the Word of Truth?

This question leads directly to the next chapter.

Chapter 81 has established:

1. The babe is a developmental category in Hebrews 5.
2. The babe is not synonymous with stupidity.
3. The babe is not synonymous with worthlessness.
4. The babe is not a permanent identity.
5. The babe is characterized by dependence upon milk.
6. The Hebrews' problem is prolonged immaturity relative to the time and opportunity they have received.
7. **ἄπειρος** identifies unskilfulness or lack of developed experience in relation to the Word of righteousness.
8. Unskilfulness is not identical with total ignorance.
9. Unskilfulness is not identical with low intelligence.
10. Skill is field-specific developed capacity.
11. Possessing Scripture is not the same as skillfully handling Scripture.
12. Repetition is not development.
13. Familiarity is not understanding.
14. Memorization is not mature discernment.
15. Credentials are not infallibility.
16. Institutional authority is not the authority of the Graphē.
17. Scholarship can assist the learner without becoming primary authority.
18. The mature learner preserves distinctions.
19. The mature learner can receive correction.
20. The mature learner can distinguish evidence, quotation, interpretation, inference, and application.
21. Development requires practice.
22. Practice must remain under the authority of the Word.
23. Discernment is active discrimination, not mere suspicion.
24. Maturity does not create private revelation.
25. Maturity increases capacity and responsibility rather than authority over the text.
26. The purpose of development is faithful handling of what has been given.

The sequence now stands:

> **Perceive → Learn → Understand → Reason → Quote → Know → Diligence → Use → Exercise → Skill → Maturity → Discernment → Handling.**

But “handling” has not yet been fully examined.

The next question is therefore unavoidable:

> **What does it mean to rightly divide the Word of Truth?**

That is the question of Chapter 82.

---

## Conclusion: The Babe Must Grow

The biblical picture of the babe is not a picture of human worthlessness.

It is a picture of developmental limitation.

The babe can learn.

The babe can receive.

The babe can possess genuine truth.

The babe can begin the journey.

But the babe has not yet developed the trained capacity of the mature.

The unskilful learner is likewise not necessarily empty of knowledge.

He may possess the Word.

He may know many propositions.

He may quote accurately.

He may have heard teaching for years.

Yet if he cannot responsibly distinguish, reason, use, and handle what he has received, his possession has not become developed skill.

This is the warning contained in Hebrews 5.

The writer does not condemn learning.

He condemns arrested development.

He does not condemn dependence at the beginning.

He exposes dependence that has remained when development should have occurred.

He does not despise elementary instruction.

He exposes the condition in which elementary instruction must continually be repeated because the learner has not progressed.

Therefore the question for the learner is not merely:

> **“How much Scripture do I have?”**

Nor:

> **“How many years have I studied?”**

Nor:

> **“How many doctrines do I know?”**

Nor:

> **“How many books have I read?”**

Nor:

> **“How respected is my teacher?”**

Nor:

> **“How confident am I?”**

The deeper question is:

> **“What have I become capable of doing with the Word?”**

Can I perceive it accurately?

Can I learn it carefully?

Can I understand its meaning?

Can I follow its reasoning?

Can I distinguish quotation from interpretation?

Can I distinguish interpretation from inference?

Can I identify the premises on which my conclusion rests?

Can I recognize when a conclusion goes beyond its evidence?

Can I compare passages without confusing similarity with proof?

Can I recognize context?

Can I receive correction?

Can I revise an interpretation when the text requires it?

Can I discern?

Can I teach without confusing my explanation with the authority of the source?

Can I handle the Word faithfully?

These are developmental questions.

And they lead naturally to the next stage.

The babe must grow.

The unskilful must develop skill.

The exercised faculty must become capable of responsible handling.

The learner who has been trained toward discernment must now learn what faithful division and handling of the Word actually require.

Therefore the movement continues:

> **Diligence → Use → Exercise → Skill → Maturity → Discernment → Right Handling.**

And the next question is:

> **What does it mean to rightly divide the Word of Truth?**
