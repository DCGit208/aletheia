# Author's Preface

There is a question beneath many questions.

Before asking what a person, institution, tradition, commentary, philosophy, school, government, or religious system teaches, one must ask a more fundamental question:

> **What is true?**

And once truth is sought, another question immediately follows:

> **How can truth be known?**

These questions cannot be settled merely by the strength of an assertion. A proposition does not become true because it is ancient, popular, sincerely held, institutionally authorized, academically defended, repeatedly quoted, or emotionally compelling. The proposition must correspond to what is actually the case.

Yet the investigation does not end there. If truth is objective, human beings still require a reliable way of knowing it. If an ultimate written authority is claimed, that authority itself requires examination. One cannot simply place a book at the beginning of an argument and then use that same book as the conclusion of the argument without explaining why the book deserves that place.

This work therefore begins before the usual discussion of Scripture. It begins with truth, reality, knowledge, evidence, reasoning, and warrant. It asks what can legitimately move the mind from a claim to a conclusion.

The investigation then turns to God. This is not an invitation to replace the biblical record with abstract speculation about God. It is the opposite. The question is how truth, divine reality, divine revelation, and the written record are related, and why the biblical account can be received as an authentic revelation rather than as an unsupported religious assertion.

The investigation then asks what the Bible actually is.

That question must be answered before broad statements are made about "the Bible." The Bible is not merely a label attached to a modern volume. It is a body of writings with a history, languages, literary forms, witnesses, claims of divine communication, prophetic testimony, apostolic testimony, revelation, inspiration, transmission, and canonical recognition.

The Greek term **γραφή (*graphē*, G1124)** becomes central at this point. The lexical starting point is simple: *graphē* means **writing**. But biblical usage gives the term a particular force when it refers to the sacred written record—Holy Writ, Scripture. The question is therefore not merely what the word means in a lexicon, but what Scripture itself does with the written thing to which the term refers.

That question eventually reaches the words of Jesus:

> **"the scripture cannot be broken."**  
> — John 10:35

Those words cannot responsibly be treated as a slogan. They occur inside an argument. Jesus cites what is written, identifies the writing as Scripture, reasons from its wording, and assigns to it a status that makes His argument decisive.

Why?

That "why" matters.

The answer cannot simply be, "because Jesus quoted it." That would leave the authority unexplained. Nor can the answer be, "because the Bible says it is authoritative," without first examining the nature and authentication of the record that makes the claim.

The investigation therefore proceeds cumulatively.

It considers divine communication, eyewitness testimony, prophetic testimony, apostolic testimony, miracles, fulfilled prophecy, Christ's historical existence, His works, His death and resurrection, the unity and continuity of the written record, historical correspondence, manuscript transmission, preservation, canonical recognition, and Christ's own treatment of Scripture.

None of these should be isolated into a magic proof. Each must be asked a precise question:

> **What does this evidence establish, and what does it not establish?**

That discipline is essential.

This book is not intended to become a philosophical apologetics manual that substitutes human speculation for Scripture. Neither is it intended to become a theology textbook in which later systems become the controlling authority. It is not a Bible commentary whose principal purpose is to explain every passage. Nor is it a catalogue of proof texts.

It is an investigation.

Its object is to establish a foundation strong enough that the reader can understand why the Graphē is treated as the primary written authority for truth, while also understanding why the interpreter must never confuse his own interpretation with the authority of the text.

The work consequently distinguishes four questions throughout:

1. **Ontological:** What is true or real?
2. **Epistemological:** How can truth be known?
3. **Evidential:** What warrants a truth claim?
4. **Hermeneutical:** Once an authoritative truthful text has been established, how is it correctly understood?

Keeping these questions distinct prevents circularity and confusion.

The final movement of the book is therefore not merely toward possession of a doctrine. It is toward **competence**.

The written Word must be perceived, learned, understood, reasoned through, exercised, and rightly handled. This is why Isaiah 1:18, Hebrews 5:11–14, and 2 Timothy 2:15 become so important. The biblical demand is not passive familiarity. The language of **σπουδάζω (*spoudazō*, G4704)** encompasses diligence, endeavour, exertion, earnest effort, labour, and study. Hebrews uses **ἄπειρος (*apeiros*, G552)** for the one inexperienced or unskillful in the Word. The mature learner has senses exercised by use and possesses discriminating judgment.

The purpose is not intellectualism for its own sake.

The purpose is that truth may be known and that the Word may be handled faithfully.

This is why *One Source of Truth* stands before the Six First Principles. It is not a seventh principle. It is the foundational work through which the learner is prepared to approach those principles with the requisite understanding, discipline, reasonability, and skill.

The reader is therefore invited not merely to agree with conclusions, but to follow the argument.

# How to Read This Book

This book should be read as a **cumulative investigation**, not as a collection of independent essays.

Each major movement establishes material required by the movement that follows.

When a chapter asks a question, the reader should expect one of three things:

- the question will be answered in that chapter;
- the question will be deliberately developed and answered in a later chapter identified by the argument;
- or the question will be shown to be outside the scope of the particular conclusion under consideration.

The reader should pay particular attention to the difference between:

> **What is asserted?**

and

> **Why is it warranted?**

A conclusion should be understood in relation to the premises that support it.

The book also uses Scripture in more than one way. Sometimes a passage is evidence for a proposition. Sometimes it supplies a definition or distinction. Sometimes it contains an argument that must itself be followed. Sometimes it records testimony or an historical event. The reader should therefore resist the habit of treating every quotation as if it were functioning identically.

Likewise, a quotation from an authoritative text is not itself a defect in reasoning. Quotation can communicate knowledge. The intellectual question is whether the quoted material is understood, correctly contextualized, and properly applied.

The book should be read with a willingness to distinguish:

- the written Word from commentary about the written Word;
- revelation from speculation;
- evidence from conclusion;
- testimony from conjecture;
- Scripture from interpretation;
- authority from institutional status;
- knowledge from mere familiarity;
- understanding from repetition;
- reasonability from rationalistic hostility to revelation.

# The Purpose and Scope of This Work

The purpose of *One Source of Truth* is to establish, with disciplined reasoning and biblical evidence, the foundations necessary for treating the **Graphē — Holy Writ** as the primary written authority under which truth is known, tested, reasoned through, taught, discerned, and lived.

The scope therefore includes five connected investigations.

### 1. Truth

What is truth? What is its relationship to reality? How is truth distinguished from error? What does it mean for a proposition to correspond to actuality?

### 2. Knowledge and warrant

How does a person move from perception and testimony to knowledge? What counts as evidence? What makes an inference rationally warranted? What is the difference between an assertion and a demonstrated conclusion?

### 3. God and revelation

What grounds truth? What is the relationship between God and truth? What does Scripture establish concerning God's truthfulness, faithfulness, immutability, and inability to lie? How does divine revelation differ from human speculation?

### 4. The Bible and the Graphē

What is the Bible? What kind of writing is it? How was its content given? What are revelation, witness, testimony, writing, inspiration, canon, transmission, and authentication? What is **γραφή (*graphē*)**, and why is it called Holy Writ?

### 5. Authority, interpretation, and labour

Why is Scripture authoritative? Why can it be called an infallible record? Why does Jesus say that "the scripture cannot be broken"? How should an authoritative text be handled? What produces unskillfulness, doctrinal turning aside, aridity, and accepted error? What does diligent scholarly labour in the Word require?

The scope is deliberately broad because the conclusion requires a broad foundation.

# Methodological / Conceptual Orientation

The central methodological principle is:

> **Do not ask the reader to accept a conclusion merely because the conclusion is stated. Establish the premises, examine the evidence, reason through the relationship between them, and show why the conclusion follows.**

The book therefore follows a disciplined progression:

> **Claim → Definition → Evidence → Analysis → Inference → Conclusion → Consequence**

This does not mean that every paragraph must mechanically display these seven labels. It means that significant arguments must actually contain these intellectual movements.

## The Bible is not to be treated as an undefined premise

A circular argument would say:

> The Bible is God's Word because the Bible says it is God's Word.

That is insufficient as a complete authentication argument.

The proper investigation asks:

> What is this writing?  
> What does it claim to be?  
> How was it given?  
> What evidence surrounds those claims?  
> What witnesses stand behind them?  
> What is the historical relationship between the record and the events it reports?  
> What does Christ do with the Scriptures?  
> What does inspiration mean?  
> What is the relationship between revelation and writing?  
> Why should the resulting record be regarded as truthful and authoritative?

Only after those questions have been properly developed can the argument concerning primary written authority be fully drawn.

## Reason is not the enemy of revelation

Reasonability is not a demand that human philosophy sit in judgment over God.

It is the capacity to perceive relationships, distinguish categories, examine evidence, follow implications, detect contradictions, compare statements, and draw warranted conclusions.

Isaiah 1:18 supplies an important biblical anchor:

> **"Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD."**

The command does not make human reason the source of truth. It demonstrates that engagement with what God says is not intended to be an abandonment of thought.

The Bible itself reasons.

Its writers argue from premises. They compare testimony. They appeal to events. They identify witnesses. They distinguish causes and consequences. They reason from what is written. Christ Himself reasons from Scripture.

The task, therefore, is not to choose between Scripture and reasoning.

> **The task is to reason through Scripture.**

# The Four Questions Governing the Investigation

## 1. Ontological — What Is True or Real?

The ontological question concerns what actually exists or what is actually the case.

Truth is not created by human preference. If a thing is true, its truth is related to reality.

The investigation therefore begins with actuality rather than institutional assertion.

## 2. Epistemological — How Can Truth Be Known?

The epistemological question concerns knowledge.

How does a person know that something is true?

The investigation considers perception, memory, testimony, evidence, revelation, reasoning, inference, and warranted conclusion.

## 3. Evidential — What Warrants a Truth Claim?

Evidence is not identical to authority, and evidence is not identical to conclusion.

The question is:

> What does the evidence establish?

and:

> Does the reasoning from that evidence to the conclusion hold?

This is why eyewitness testimony, prophecy, miracles, historical correspondence, manuscript evidence, unity, preservation, and Christ's testimony must be examined carefully rather than merely listed.

## 4. Hermeneutical — How Is an Authoritative Text Correctly Understood?

Once an authoritative truthful text has been established, another problem remains:

> **What does it mean?**

The authority of the text does not automatically make the interpreter infallible.

Understanding requires attention to language, grammar, context, literary form, historical setting, comparison of passages, distinctions, inference, and the principles by which the text itself is handled.

# Important Terminology

## Truth

Truth concerns what conforms to reality or actuality. The investigation must distinguish truth from assertion, opinion, convention, popularity, institutional acceptance, and repetition.

## Knowledge

Knowledge concerns what is genuinely known rather than merely imagined, asserted, or repeated. In this work, knowledge may involve testimony, evidence, perception, learning, reasoning, and warranted conclusion.

## Evidence

Evidence is that which bears upon the truth or falsity of a claim and can rationally contribute to a conclusion.

## Warrant

Warrant concerns the grounds that make a conclusion rationally supportable.

## Revelation

Revelation concerns what God makes known that human beings could not otherwise possess merely through ordinary human discovery.

## Witness

A witness is one who sees, hears, encounters, receives, or otherwise stands in a position to testify concerning an event or communication.

## Testimony

Testimony is the communicated report of what a witness knows or claims to have witnessed.

## Inspiration

Inspiration concerns the divine operation by which Scripture is given in written form. The doctrine must be developed from the biblical record and must not be reduced to a theory about mechanical dictation.

## Canon

Canon concerns the recognized authoritative corpus of Scripture.

## Transmission

Transmission concerns the historical preservation and copying of the written record.

## Authentication

Authentication concerns the grounds by which a claimed record is recognized as genuine, trustworthy, and authoritative.

## Infallibility

Infallibility is the conclusion concerning the inability of the inspired record to fail as truth. It must therefore be established from the preceding account of God, revelation, inspiration, authentication, and Scripture rather than simply asserted at the beginning.

## Graphē — γραφή

**Greek:** γραφή  
**Transliteration:** *graphē*  
**Strong's:** G1124  
**Basic lexical sense:** writing.

In biblical usage, *graphē* is used for the sacred written record—**Scripture / Holy Writ**.

The book will therefore distinguish the lexical meaning of the word from the theological conclusions that are established from its biblical usage.

## Holy Writ

"Holy Writ" identifies the sacred written record. The designation is significant because the investigation concerns writings that are presented as carrying divine authority, not ordinary writings merely discussing God.

## Scripture

"Scripture" is the English designation commonly used for the sacred writings. The book will examine how Scripture itself uses and reasons from the category.

# Introduction

## The Question Beneath the Questions

Every serious investigation begins by deciding what kind of question is being asked.

If a person asks, "What does this institution teach?", the question is about an institution's teaching.

If the question is, "What does this theologian believe?", the question concerns the theologian.

If the question is, "What does this tradition say?", the question concerns the tradition.

But none of these questions is identical to:

> **What is true?**

That distinction is foundational.

A statement can be sincerely held and still be false. A doctrine can be ancient and still be false. A teaching can be accepted by millions and still be false. A proposition can be repeated in classrooms, pulpits, books, commentaries, councils, institutions, and governments and still fail to correspond to reality.

Truth therefore cannot be reduced to the social history of a proposition.

Nor can truth be reduced to belief.

Belief is a person's acceptance of a proposition. Truth concerns whether the proposition actually corresponds to reality.

That distinction matters enormously when the subject is Scripture.

The question before this work is not simply:

> What do people who identify themselves as followers of Christ believe about the Bible?

Nor is it:

> What have religious institutions historically said about Scripture?

Nor is it:

> What do theologians and philosophers think the Bible means?

The prior question is:

> **What is the written record itself, what does it claim to be, what evidence authenticates it, and why should its truth and authority govern our judgment?**

That question requires intellectual discipline.

---

## Truth Must Be Distinguished From Assertion

Consider a simple proposition:

> "Something happened."

The fact that someone says it happened does not itself establish that it happened.

The statement may be true. It may be false. It may be partially true. It may be misunderstood. It may be deliberately fabricated. It may be an honest but mistaken report.

The statement therefore requires examination.

The same principle applies to a much larger proposition:

> "This writing is the Word of God."

The seriousness of that claim does not exempt it from examination. Indeed, its seriousness makes examination more important.

But examination must also be fair.

One must not reject a claim merely because it is extraordinary. Nor should one accept a claim merely because it is comforting, familiar, or institutionally protected.

The rational question is:

> **What is the claim, what evidence bears upon it, what premises support it, and does the conclusion follow?**

This is the intellectual posture required throughout this book.

---

## Truth Is Not Created by the Investigator

There is another danger.

Once people become aware that claims require examination, they can make the opposite mistake and behave as though truth itself is produced by the investigator.

It is not.

A person does not make a historical event true by believing it happened.

A person does not make a proposition false by disliking it.

A government does not make a fact true by legislating it.

An institution does not make a doctrine true by adopting it.

A school does not make an interpretation authoritative merely by teaching it.

The investigator's task is not to manufacture truth but to discover, recognize, understand, and faithfully represent what is true.

This is why the question of reality must precede the question of institutional acceptance.

---

## The Ground of Truth

Once truth is recognized as something that cannot simply be manufactured by human preference, the deeper question appears:

> **What ultimately grounds truth?**

If reality is objective, what accounts for its intelligibility?

If truth is more than convention, what is its ultimate foundation?

If God is the source and ground of all that exists, then the relation between God and truth cannot be treated as incidental.

The biblical record presents God not as a deceiver who occasionally happens to communicate accurate propositions, but as truthful, faithful, immutable, and incapable of falsehood.

That distinction will become important.

The question is not merely:

> "Does God sometimes tell the truth?"

The deeper question is:

> **Is falsehood compatible with the nature of God?**

If it is not, then divine revelation has a fundamentally different epistemic status from human speculation.

But that conclusion still requires argument.

The existence of a claim that God has spoken does not automatically establish that God has spoken.

The investigation must therefore move from:

**truth → ground → God → revelation → authentication.**

---

## Why Revelation Matters

Human beings can know many things by observation, experience, memory, inference, and testimony.

But there are things they could not know merely by looking at the ordinary world unless someone with knowledge of those things made them known.

The Bible contains claims concerning creation, divine purposes, events beyond ordinary human observation, prophetic events, God's dealings with humanity, the spiritual realm, and the future.

If those claims are true, their truth cannot be explained simply by saying that the human writers guessed correctly.

This is where revelation becomes significant.

Revelation concerns what is made known.

But revelation is not identical to writing.

A revelation may be received by a person.

A person may witness an event.

A prophet may hear a divine communication.

An apostle may see and hear Christ.

A vision may be received.

A dream may convey a message.

A divine manifestation may occur.

The communication may then be testified to and committed to writing.

This distinction is essential:

> **What God reveals, how that revelation is communicated, who witnesses it, what those witnesses testify, how the testimony is written, and how the writing is recognized as Scripture are related questions—but they are not identical questions.**

The book therefore treats them separately before bringing them together.

---

## What Is the Bible?

Before anyone says, "The Bible says...", a basic question should be answered:

> **What is the Bible?**

The answer cannot be merely a definition copied from a dictionary.

The Bible is a body of writings. It contains history, biography, poetry, proverbial sayings, hymns, letters, laws, prophecy, parables, and other forms of human expression. Its writings arise across different periods and circumstances and involve multiple writers and languages.

Yet the existence of literary diversity does not by itself explain the Bible's unity.

Nor does calling it a "book" explain its claims of divine origin.

The Bible Introduction supplied for this work describes it as a **divine library** and as God's inspired revelation concerning the origin and destiny of all things. It emphasizes its unity, superiority of content, preservation, fulfilled prophecy, miracles, historical and scientific claims, moral and spiritual power, internal claims, external historical confirmation, and cumulative evidential character.

Those claims are not to be repeated as slogans.

They are to be examined.

For example, if unity is offered as evidence, the question is:

> What kind of unity is present, across what diversity, and how does that unity bear upon the claim of divine origin?

If fulfilled prophecy is offered as evidence, the question is:

> What was predicted, when was it predicted, what exactly occurred, and how close is the correspondence?

If eyewitness testimony is offered, the question is:

> Who witnessed what, what did they claim to have seen or heard, and what makes their testimony relevant to the conclusion?

That is how evidence becomes reasoning rather than a list.

---

## The Bible Is a Written Record of More Than Human Speculation

The Bible Introduction explicitly distinguishes revelation from inspiration and explains that revelation concerns the discovery of new truth while inspiration concerns the divine superintendence of its communication and recording. It also makes an important distinction: inspiration can accurately record statements made by God, angels, Satan, demons, and human beings without thereby making every statement uttered by those characters true.

That distinction will require careful treatment later in the book.

For now, the central point is that the Bible presents itself as a record.

It records what happened.

It records what people said.

It records what God said.

It records what prophets received.

It records what witnesses saw.

It records what apostles testified.

It records historical events and divine communications.

The task is therefore not to flatten all these categories into one undifferentiated idea. The task is to understand how they belong to the one written record and what inspiration means for that record.

---

## How Was the Written Record Given?

The Bible Introduction identifies multiple modes through which biblical material was given: the audible voice of God, angels, prophets, Jesus Christ, apostles, visions, dreams, revelation, and inspiration. It specifically directs attention to passages such as Exodus 19:19, Deuteronomy 5, Matthew 3:16–17, John 12:28, Acts 7:38, Hebrews 2:2, Acts 3:21, Hebrews 1:1, Revelation 1, Acts 1:2, Ephesians 4, Isaiah 6, Daniel 7–8, Ezekiel 1, Daniel 2, Matthew 1:20, Matthew 2:12, and 2 Timothy 3:15–17.

This is more than a catalogue.

It establishes that the Bible's origin is not represented as a single mechanical process.

There is divine speech.

There is angelic mediation.

There is prophetic mediation.

There is Christ's revelation.

There is apostolic testimony.

There are visions.

There are dreams.

There is revelation.

There is inspiration.

And there is the final written record.

The investigation must therefore avoid simplistic theories that confuse revelation with dictation or inspiration with the elimination of human authorship.

The Bible Introduction itself says that inspiration guarantees unity of truth rather than sameness of words and expressions.

That observation is crucial for understanding the Bible as a genuine written record rather than as an artificial document stripped of human literary expression.

---

## Witness and Testimony

One of the strongest evidential features of the biblical record is its insistence upon testimony.

Peter writes:

> **"For we have not followed cunningly devised fables, when we made known unto you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but were eyewitnesses of his majesty."**  
> — 2 Peter 1:16

That sentence deserves more than quotation.

It makes a historical claim about the nature of apostolic knowledge.

The apostles are not presenting their account as an imaginative construction.

They claim to have been witnesses.

This does not mean that every claim becomes true simply because a person calls himself a witness. Testimony still has to be assessed.

But it does mean that the epistemic category is historical testimony rather than abstract speculation.

The same principle extends to the Gospel record and apostolic writings.

The reader therefore has to ask:

> Who saw what?

> Who heard what?

> What was reported?

> When was it reported?

> How does the testimony relate to the events?

> How does independent testimony converge?

These are proper evidential questions.

---

## What Is γραφή?

At the heart of the investigation stands one Greek word:

> **γραφή — *graphē* — G1124**

Its basic lexical meaning is **writing**.

This starting point must not be overcomplicated.

*Graphē* is related to the act of writing and to a written thing. In biblical usage, however, it becomes the designation for the sacred written record: **Scripture, Holy Writ**.

The important question is therefore not merely:

> "What does G1124 mean?"

The important question is:

> **What happens when the biblical writers use γραφή to refer to the sacred writing?**

That is where lexical definition becomes biblical doctrine.

The writing is not treated as an ordinary document.

Jesus does not treat Scripture as merely a religious opinion recorded on parchment.

He reasons from what is written.

He appeals to its wording.

He identifies the writing as Scripture.

And He says:

> **"the scripture cannot be broken."**

The force of that statement will only be fully understood after the nature and authentication of the written record have been established.

---

## Why Is It Called Holy Writ?

The designation "Holy Writ" is not a decorative religious title.

It identifies the writing as **holy**—set apart in relation to God—and as **writ**—written.

The question is therefore twofold:

1. What makes the writing a writing?
2. What makes that writing Holy Writ?

The first question is lexical.

The second is evidential and theological.

A document does not become Holy Writ merely because an institution places a sacred label upon it. The designation must have grounds.

Those grounds involve the record's origin, divine communication, prophetic and apostolic testimony, inspiration, canonical recognition, authentication, and the testimony of Christ concerning Scripture.

This is why the book does not begin by simply declaring the Bible authoritative and then using that declaration to prove its authority.

The argument must be built.

---

## Why Does Scripture Matter for Truth?

The ultimate concern of this investigation is truth.

John 17:17 gives the governing statement:

> **"Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth."**

The proposition is extraordinarily direct.

Truth is identified with God's Word.

This does not mean that human experience creates truth.

It does not mean that institutional interpretation creates truth.

It means that the Word is presented as truth.

The consequence is significant.

If God's Word is truth, then human experience must not become the measure by which God's Word is rewritten.

Rather, experience is itself examined in relation to the Word.

A person's experience can be real as an experience and still be misunderstood.

An institution can genuinely exist and still teach error.

A doctrine can genuinely be believed and still be false.

The written Word therefore functions as the standard under which claims are examined.

That is the beginning of the doctrine of the Graphē as primary written authority.

---

## Why Cannot Scripture Be Broken?

John 10:34–36 supplies one of the most decisive passages for the argument.

Jesus is confronted with an accusation.

He answers by appealing to what is written.

He says:

> **"Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?"**

He then identifies those recipients of the written word as those:

> **"unto whom the word of God came"**

and proceeds to the statement:

> **"and the scripture cannot be broken."**

The question is not merely what Jesus quoted.

The question is:

> **Why does He say that Scripture cannot be broken?**

The answer must be grounded in what Scripture is.

If Scripture is the authoritative written Word of God, and if the Word of God is truth, then Scripture cannot be broken in the sense of being annulled, invalidated, overturned, or exposed as false.

This is not because paper, ink, manuscripts, or physical objects possess supernatural invulnerability.

It is because the authority and truthfulness of the written Word derive from what it is and from the God whose Word it is.

The logic therefore reaches backward.

To understand "the scripture cannot be broken," one must understand:

**truth → God → revelation → written Word → Scripture → authority.**

That is why this book spends so much time establishing foundations before reaching John 10.

---

## The Logic of John 10

Jesus' argument is especially important because He does not treat the written wording as disposable.

He cites:

> **"I said, Ye are gods."**

That wording comes from Psalm 82:6:

> **"I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High."**

The argument depends upon the written text.

Jesus does not dismiss the wording as an imperfect historical relic.

He does not say that the passage is too old to matter.

He does not say that the wording was merely the product of a religious community.

He reasons from it.

Then He says:

> **"the scripture cannot be broken."**

The passage therefore becomes a major test of what the Bible means when it treats Scripture as authoritative.

The authority is not generated by the interpreter.

The interpreter stands under the writing.

---

## Truth and the Word

John 17:17 provides the complementary statement:

> **"thy word is truth."**

John 10 establishes that Scripture cannot be broken.

John 17 identifies the Word with truth.

Taken together, these passages provide a powerful biblical relationship:

> **The Word is truth.**

> **Scripture cannot be broken.**

The later chapters will examine the premises required to understand why those statements belong together.

The conclusion is not that human beings cease to reason.

The conclusion is that their reasoning has an authoritative truthful object through which claims can be tested.

---

## The Bible Must Be Studied, Not Merely Possessed

An authoritative book can be possessed without being understood.

A person may own a Bible and misunderstand it.

A person may quote Scripture and misuse it.

A person may memorize verses and fail to understand their relationships.

A person may know terminology and remain unskilled in the Word.

This is why the Bible Introduction's discussion of understanding is so important.

It emphasizes that Scripture is to be studied and that the difficulty often lies not in some inherent impossibility of truth but in the learner's dullness, ignorance, lack of study, hardness, poor perception, and failure to engage the text properly. It also identifies Hebrews 5:11–14 as a diagnosis of dull hearing and unskillfulness, with the mature possessing exercised powers of discrimination.

This is not a minor pedagogical point.

It is part of the doctrine.

If the Word is authoritative truth, then the learner bears a responsibility to learn it.

---

## Reasonability and the Word

Isaiah 1:18 says:

> **"Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD."**

The verse provides a necessary corrective to two opposite errors.

The first error is anti-intellectualism: the idea that faith requires abandoning thought.

The second is rationalistic arrogance: the idea that human reason is itself the ultimate authority over all revelation.

Neither position is adequate.

Biblical reasonability is the disciplined use of the mind in relation to truth.

The Word must be perceived.

Its statements must be understood.

Its relationships must be examined.

Its implications must be followed.

Its claims must be distinguished from human claims about it.

Its evidence must be considered.

Its conclusions must be applied.

That is why the intellectual labour of Scripture becomes a major part of the later movement of this book.

---

## The Scholarly Impetus of Scripture

The Bible Introduction presents the Bible as remarkable not merely because it is ancient or historically influential but because of its extraordinary breadth, literary diversity, doctrinal content, prophetic claims, moral teaching, historical claims, unity, depth, and intellectual comprehensiveness. It describes Scripture as reasonable in its teachings, comprehensive in knowledge, exact in its claims, and demanding sustained study.

This is the **scholarly impetus** of Scripture.

The point is not to turn the Word into an academic specimen.

The point is to recognize that a book claiming to reveal the mind and will of God deserves the highest intellectual seriousness.

If Scripture addresses creation, humanity, history, law, morality, prophecy, salvation, divine purpose, the future, and the nature of God, then superficial reading is inadequate to the scope of its claims.

The reader should therefore become curious.

There is much more to learn about the intellectual architecture of the Bible than can be exhausted in an introductory treatment.

That deeper investigation is precisely why *One Source of Truth* exists.

---

## From the Graphē to Competent Handling

The final question is practical:

> **What must the learner become if the Graphē is the primary written authority?**

Not merely a person who possesses the text.

Not merely a person who quotes the text.

Not merely a person who belongs to an institution that uses the text.

But a person who can **hear, perceive, learn, reason, understand, exercise, distinguish, and rightly handle the Word**.

Hebrews 5:13 uses **ἄπειρος (*apeiros*, G552)** for one who is inexperienced or unskillful in the Word.

Hebrews 5:14 describes mature persons whose faculties are exercised through use for discernment.

And 2 Timothy 2:15 uses **σπουδάζω (*spoudazō*, G4704)**—a term whose range includes diligence, earnest effort, endeavour, exertion, labour, and study.

The intellectual life demanded by Scripture is therefore active.

It requires effort.

It requires discipline.

It requires exercise.

It requires judgment.

It requires maturity.

The objective is not to replace faith with scholarship.

The objective is to prevent ignorance from being mistaken for faith and repetition from being mistaken for understanding.

---

## The Foundation Before the First Principles

Hebrews 5:12 speaks of people who ought to have been teachers but still require instruction in the first principles of the oracles of God.

That passage establishes an important educational sequence.

There are foundations.

There is maturity.

There is skill.

There is progression.

The Doctrine of the Graphē therefore belongs before the Six First Principles.

It is not a seventh principle.

It is the foundation that establishes what the learner is dealing with, why the written Word carries authority, how it was given and authenticated, and what intellectual discipline is required to handle it.

The architecture is therefore:

> **One Source of Truth — The Doctrine of the Graphē**
>
> ↓
>
> **Study and mastery**
>
> ↓
>
> **Demonstrated readiness**
>
> ↓
>
> **The Six First Principles**

The gate is not another principle.

The foundation is not another floor.

The purpose of this work is to prepare the learner to enter the principles with a properly established relationship to truth, Scripture, authority, reasonability, and scholarly labour.

---

## The Investigation Ahead

The pages that follow will therefore not ask the reader simply to admire Scripture.

They will ask the reader to examine it.

Not to replace it with philosophy.

Not to subordinate it to institutional tradition.

Not to confuse it with every interpretation ever made of it.

Not to reduce it to quotations.

Not to reduce truth to belief.

Not to reduce knowledge to memorization.

And not to treat reason as the enemy of revelation.

Instead, the investigation will proceed from the most basic question to the most consequential conclusion:

> **What is true?**

Then:

> **What is reality?**

> **How can truth be known?**

> **What constitutes evidence?**

> **What makes an inference rationally warranted?**

> **What can serve as ultimate authority?**

> **What grounds truth?**

> **What is the relationship between God and truth?**

> **How does God reveal what human beings could not otherwise know?**

> **What is the Bible?**

> **How was its written record given?**

> **What is γραφή — *graphē*?**

> **Why is it Holy Writ?**

> **What authenticates it?**

> **What does inspiration mean?**

> **What does "all Scripture" mean?**

> **Why is the record infallible?**

> **How does Christ treat Scripture?**

> **Why does He say that the Scripture cannot be broken?**

> **Why does John say, "thy word is truth"?**

> **Why does this make the Graphē the primary written authority?**

And then:

> **How should a human being live and reason under that authority?**

That is where the investigation ultimately arrives.

The purpose is not merely to establish that the Graphē is authoritative.

The purpose is to produce a learner who understands **why**, knows **how**, and is prepared to **handle the Word faithfully**.
