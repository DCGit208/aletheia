# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 36 --- Divine Manifestation and Miracles

> **Movement V --- Authentication**

> **Central objective:** Examine manifestations and miracles as claimed
> evidences of divine activity; establish what the biblical record
> presents such events as accomplishing, how they bear upon divine
> commissioning and revelation, and what additional reasoning is
> required before an extraordinary event becomes evidence for a specific
> claim.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Chapter Assignment

The preceding chapters have moved from divine communication to written
testimony and from written testimony to credible human witnesses. The
present chapter examines another element repeatedly joined to those
witnesses in the biblical record: **divine manifestation and miracles**.

The question is not merely whether the Bible contains extraordinary
events. It does. The necessary question is more precise:

> **When the Bible presents a manifestation or miracle as an act of God,
> what does that event establish within the biblical evidential
> argument?**

This chapter therefore examines the relationship among **divine action,
manifestation, sign, miracle, witness, message, and authentication**.

The governing sequence is:

> **divine act → manifestation → sign → witness → interpretation →
> authentication → testimony → written record.**

The point is not to make miracles carry the entire case for Scripture.
The point is to establish exactly what this evidential category
contributes to the cumulative investigation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.1 The Evidential Question

The ordinary word *miracle* can mean almost any event considered
extraordinary or inexplicable. That is too broad for the present
investigation.

The biblical record uses several related categories: **signs, wonders,
mighty works, works, powers, manifestations, judgments, and
deliverances**. These terms overlap, but they are not interchangeable in
every context.

The controlling question is:

> **What function does an extraordinary divine act perform in the
> biblical narrative?**

The answer varies by event. A manifestation may disclose divine
presence. A miracle may demonstrate divine power. A sign may point to a
person or message. A judgment may reveal God's authority. A deliverance
may display God's covenant faithfulness.

Accordingly:

> **Extraordinary does not by itself mean divine.**

But neither does extraordinariness make a claimed divine act irrelevant
to evidence. Where the biblical record connects an event to God,
identifies its purpose, records witnesses, and relates it to a messenger
or message, the event becomes a historical and evidential claim that can
be examined.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.2 Divine Manifestation Is Broader Than Miracle

The chapter title deliberately begins with **Divine Manifestation**.

The Bible presents God's presence or activity becoming perceptible in
numerous ways. God speaks. God appears. God's glory is displayed. Fire,
cloud, storm, angelic appearances, prophetic signs, judgments,
deliverances, and extraordinary works occur within the biblical
narrative.

Not every manifestation is a miracle in the narrow sense.

The burning bush of Exodus 3 is a manifestation in which Moses sees a
bush burning without being consumed. But the significance of the event
is not exhausted by the phenomenon. God identifies Himself, speaks to
Moses, and commissions him.

The Sinai manifestation in Exodus 19--20 likewise includes thunder,
lightning, cloud, fire, trumpet sound, and divine speech. The phenomena
accompany covenantal revelation.

The principle is therefore:

> **The biblical meaning of a manifestation is determined by the divine
> communication and purpose connected with it.**

The phenomenon and the word belong together.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.3 Signs, Wonders, and Mighty Works

## Sign

A **sign** points beyond itself. Its evidential value lies in what it
signifies.

This is particularly clear in John's Gospel. Jesus performs signs, and
John explains that the signs selected for the written record serve the
purpose that readers may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of
God (John 20:30--31).

A sign therefore has a direction:

> **event → significance → conclusion.**

## Wonder

A **wonder** emphasizes the extraordinary character of an event and the
astonishment it produces. Astonishment is a response, not the final
conclusion.

## Mighty work

A **mighty work** emphasizes power. The question remains whose power is
being displayed and what the act signifies.

## Work

Jesus' works are observable actions that the Gospel writers connect to
His identity, authority, mission, and relationship with the Father.

These categories show why reducing miracles to "things that cannot be
explained" misses the biblical argument. The central issue is not merely
unusualness but **divine significance**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.4 Moses: Manifestation, Commission, and Signs

Moses provides one of the clearest biblical examples of a miracle
functioning in relation to divine commissioning.

Exodus 3 records the burning bush. Moses encounters a phenomenon that
arrests his attention. God then identifies Himself as the God of
Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and gives Moses a commission concerning
Israel.

The issue becomes explicit in Exodus 4. Moses asks what will happen if
the Israelites do not believe that the LORD appeared to him.

God gives signs.

Moses' staff becomes a serpent and is restored. His hand becomes
diseased and is restored. A further sign involving water becoming blood
is given.

The purpose is explicitly connected with belief that God has appeared to
Moses (Exodus 4:5).

The evidential architecture is therefore clear:

1.  Moses claims divine commissioning.
2.  God provides signs associated with that commissioning.
3.  The signs are presented as evidence that God has appeared to Moses.
4.  The people are expected to recognize the divine source of his
    mission.

The miracle does not stand alone. It is attached to a messenger and a
message.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.5 The Exodus: Public Divine Action

The Exodus expands the pattern from signs associated with one messenger
to public divine action involving Israel and Egypt.

The plagues are repeatedly associated with God's purpose of making His
identity and authority known. Exodus 7:5 declares that the Egyptians
will know that He is the LORD.

The Red Sea deliverance likewise becomes a defining historical act in
Israel's memory.

The biblical argument is not simply that Moses experienced a private
supernatural event. It presents God acting publicly, a community
experiencing deliverance, Moses and others witnessing and interpreting
the events, and the events becoming part of Israel's transmitted
historical testimony.

This establishes an important category:

> **A divine act may function simultaneously as manifestation, judgment
> or deliverance, public evidence, and historical testimony.**

The event is subsequently remembered, taught, celebrated, and written
about.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.6 Manifestation and Covenant

The Sinai events show why manifestation cannot be separated from
revelation.

God's presence is manifested. The people encounter the event. God
speaks. The covenant is established. The commandments are given. The
written record preserves the communication.

The resulting structure is:

> **manifestation → divine identification → communication → covenant →
> written testimony.**

A manifestation may answer:

> **Who is acting?**

The accompanying word may answer:

> **What is God saying?**

The covenant may answer:

> **What follows from what God has said?**

The written record preserves the testimony for later generations.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.7 Elijah on Carmel

1 Kings 18 provides another explicit public authentication event.

The dispute on Mount Carmel concerns the identity of the true God.
Elijah proposes a public test involving sacrifice and fire.

The prophets of Baal call upon Baal. No answering fire appears. Elijah
calls upon the LORD. Fire falls and consumes the offering and
surrounding materials.

The event is interpreted in relation to the question:

> **Who is God?**

The evidential structure contains:

-   competing claims;
-   a defined public test;
-   invocation of the alleged deity;
-   observable outcome;
-   witnesses;
-   interpretation;
-   and a response by the people.

The event therefore functions as evidence within a defined dispute.

It is not merely astonishing. It bears upon a proposition.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.8 Prophetic Signs

The prophets also use signs and enacted actions to communicate divine
messages.

Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel provide examples in which prophetic
actions become visible representations of what God is declaring.

Some prophetic signs are extraordinary events. Others are enacted
symbols. Some involve ordinary actions given extraordinary revelatory
significance.

This broadens the category of divine manifestation.

A sign may communicate through:

-   an extraordinary event;
-   an enacted action;
-   a symbolic object;
-   a judgment;
-   a deliverance;
-   or another observable act.

The governing principle remains:

> **The sign serves the message; it does not replace the message.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.9 The Miracles of Jesus

The Gospels place divine manifestations and miraculous works in a
concentrated form around Jesus.

Jesus heals the sick, cleanses lepers, casts out demons, feeds
multitudes, calms storms, raises the dead, and performs other
extraordinary works.

The Gospel writers do not present these works as disconnected marvels.

They connect them with Jesus' identity, authority, kingdom proclamation,
compassion, fulfillment, and relationship with the Father.

Matthew 11:2--6 is particularly significant. John the Baptist asks
whether Jesus is the expected one. Jesus points to the works taking
place: the blind receive sight, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, the
deaf hear, the dead are raised, and good news is proclaimed.

The works therefore bear upon the messianic question.

John provides an explicit statement of purpose: the signs recorded are
written so that the reader may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son
of God (John 20:31).

Thus the Gospel signs are part of the historical argument concerning
Jesus.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.10 Jesus' Works as Witness

Jesus repeatedly directs attention to His works.

John 5:36 describes the works given by the Father as a witness
concerning Him. John 10:25 connects His works with His Father's name.
John 10:37--38 explicitly reasons from the works.

The logic is not that every person who performs an extraordinary act
must be divine. The claim is specific to Jesus' works and the claims to
which they are connected.

The structure is:

1.  Jesus makes claims concerning His identity and relationship with the
    Father.
2.  Jesus performs observable works.
3.  The works are presented as works of the Father.
4.  The works therefore function as witness concerning Jesus' claims.

The event and the proposition are connected.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.11 Lazarus: Event, Witness, and Consequence

John 11 presents the raising of Lazarus as a public event in Bethany.

Jesus connects the event with God's glory and belief. The event then
produces different responses. Some believe. Others report what happened
to the authorities.

This matters historically because the event becomes part of subsequent
action and controversy.

It is not an isolated devotional anecdote within the narrative. It
affects what people do next.

The evidential chain is:

> **event → witnesses → reports → consequences → written testimony.**

This is a useful model for evaluating miracle claims historically.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.12 Apostolic Signs and Wonders

Acts continues the pattern after Jesus' resurrection and ascension.

Acts 2:43 describes wonders and signs occurring through the apostles.
Acts 3 records the healing of a lame man at the temple.

Peter does not merely point to the event as spectacle. He explains it
and connects it with Jesus Christ.

The sequence is again:

> **event → public witness → interpretation → proclamation.**

Hebrews 2:3--4 gives a particularly explicit formulation. The salvation
message is first announced by the Lord and confirmed by those who heard
Him, while God bears witness with signs, wonders, miracles, and gifts of
the Holy Spirit.

The phrase **bearing witness** is central to the evidential question.

The divine acts function as testimony or confirmation associated with
the proclaimed message.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.13 Paul's Apostolic Signs

Paul refers to signs associated with his apostolic ministry.

2 Corinthians 12:12 speaks of the signs of an apostle being worked among
the Corinthians in signs, wonders, and mighty deeds. Romans 15:18--19
describes Paul's ministry in terms of what Christ accomplished through
him in word and deed, including mighty signs and wonders.

Paul does not present himself as the ultimate source of supernatural
power. The work is attributed to Christ.

This preserves the distinction between human instrument and divine
agency:

> **The messenger may be the historical agent through whom an act occurs
> without being the ultimate source to whom the act is attributed.**

That distinction is consistent with the divine-human structure
established in the preceding chapters.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.14 Divine Agency and Human Agency

The Bible's miracle accounts are historical narratives involving human
beings.

Moses witnesses.

Israel experiences.

Elijah confronts.

The prophets act.

Disciples observe.

Crowds respond.

Apostles testify.

Writers record.

The existence of human agency does not erase divine agency.

Nor does divine agency erase the historical character of human
testimony.

This is important because a miracle claim reaches the reader through
human testimony. The biblical account does not ordinarily give the
modern reader an unmediated experience of the original event. It gives
testimony about what happened.

Thus the miracle category belongs naturally with the eyewitness and
testimony chapters.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.15 Miracle and Testimony Belong Together

The previous chapter examined credible witnesses. The present chapter
asks what those witnesses claim to have seen.

Two questions must therefore be separated:

> **Did the event occur?**

and:

> **If it occurred, what does it establish?**

The first is historical.

The second is inferential.

This distinction avoids two errors.

The first is to reject an event simply because it is extraordinary.

The second is to accept every proposition associated with an
extraordinary event without examining the relationship between event and
proposition.

The appropriate process is:

> **Investigate the testimony, establish what the evidence supports
> concerning the event, then examine the divine and evidential
> significance attributed to it.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.16 What a Miracle Would Establish

This is the chapter's central logical question.

Suppose an extraordinary event is established.

At the first level, it establishes that an extraordinary event occurred.

If the evidence further establishes that the event was an intentional
act of God, then the conclusion is stronger:

> **God acted in the event.**

If the event is deliberately connected to a messenger's commission, it
can provide evidence for that commission.

If it is deliberately connected to a message, it can provide evidence
bearing upon that message.

The reasoning is:

> **genuine divine act + established connection to messenger → evidence
> for divine commissioning**

and:

> **genuine divine act + established connection to message → evidence
> bearing upon divine authorization of that message.**

The strength of the conclusion depends upon the strength of the
connection.

This is why the biblical accounts repeatedly identify what the sign is
intended to establish.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.17 The Messenger--Message--Sign Relationship

A recurring biblical structure can be expressed as:

> **Messenger --- Message --- Sign**

The messenger claims:

> **God sent me.**

The message states:

> **This is what God says.**

The sign functions as:

> **God bears witness to this commission or message.**

Moses provides a clear example. Elijah provides another. Jesus' works
form an especially concentrated New Testament example. Apostolic signs
continue the pattern.

The relationship is not mechanically identical in every passage. A sign
may identify God, confirm a prophet, demonstrate authority, establish a
messianic claim, or display judgment or mercy.

Therefore the precise rule is:

> **Where the biblical record presents an extraordinary act as a sign
> associated with divine commissioning or revelation, the event bears
> evidentially upon that specific claim.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.18 False Signs and Deceptive Wonders

The Bible itself prevents an overly simplistic doctrine of miracles by
explicitly addressing false signs and deceptive wonders.

Deuteronomy 13:1--3 describes a prophet or dreamer who gives a sign or
wonder that comes to pass but then seeks to lead Israel after other
gods.

The principle is decisive:

> **A sign must not be evaluated independently of its message and its
> relation to the true God.**

Jesus likewise warns of false prophets who may show great signs and
wonders (Matthew 24:24). Paul describes deceptive signs and wonders in 2
Thessalonians 2:9.

Therefore:

> **Supernatural appearance alone is not the biblical authentication
> test.**

Source, message, context, and continuity with God's revealed truth
remain relevant.

This is not a weakness in the miracle argument. It is evidence that the
Bible itself treats authentication as a disciplined process.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.19 Why False Signs Do Not Remove Genuine Signs

The existence of false signs does not make genuine signs impossible or
evidentially irrelevant.

It establishes the necessity of discrimination.

The Bible does not teach:

> **miracle = automatic truth.**

It teaches a more precise structure:

> **sign + source + message + truth + context.**

A genuine sign must be identified as such. A deceptive sign must be
distinguished from it.

The existence of counterfeits therefore confirms that the biblical
category of authentication is more sophisticated than astonishment.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.20 The Sign Must Point Somewhere

A sign has evidential meaning because it points beyond itself.

The fire on Carmel points to the LORD's identity.

Moses' signs point to God's appearance and commission.

The Exodus signs point to God's judgment and deliverance.

Jesus' signs point to His identity and mission.

Apostolic signs point toward divine confirmation of the proclaimed
message.

Thus:

> **The sign is not the final object; it is a pointer to what the sign
> is intended to establish.**

John's use of the term *sign* makes this especially clear. His selected
signs are written so that readers may believe concerning Jesus.

The written sign therefore has an evidential purpose beyond the event
itself.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.21 Manifestation, Revelation, and Authentication

These three concepts must remain distinct.

### Manifestation

God's presence or activity becomes perceptible.

### Revelation

God communicates truth.

### Authentication

Evidence bears upon the claimed divine source of a messenger or message.

One event may participate in all three without making the concepts
identical.

For example:

-   God manifests His activity;
-   God communicates through a messenger;
-   a sign accompanies the commission;
-   the sign bears witness to the claimed divine source.

The distinction keeps the argument precise.

> **Manifestation concerns divine activity becoming perceptible.
> Revelation concerns divine truth being communicated. Authentication
> concerns the evidential confirmation of the claimed divine source.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.22 The Written Record Preserves the Miracle Claim

The modern reader does not ordinarily stand before the Red Sea, Mount
Carmel, Bethany, or the places where Jesus performed His works.

The modern reader receives testimony concerning these events.

The historical chain is therefore:

> **event → witness → testimony → written record → reader.**

Without the event, the testimony would lack the occurrence it claims to
report.

Without testimony, the modern reader would have no historical access to
the event.

Without interpretation, the reader would not know what the event is
claimed to signify.

Without reasoning, the reader would not know what conclusion follows.

This is why miracles are part of the larger doctrine of the Graphē
rather than a detached apologetic subject.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.23 Public Witness and Multiple Observers

Public events create a different evidential situation from purely
private experiences.

The Exodus is presented as involving a nation.

Carmel is public.

Jesus' works are repeatedly described in settings involving disciples,
crowds, beneficiaries, opponents, and religious authorities.

Acts likewise describes public signs in Jerusalem and elsewhere.

This does not mean that the mere presence of a crowd proves that an
event occurred. It means that the biblical claim is structured as public
testimony and therefore has a form suitable for historical examination.

The evidential possibilities increase when an event has multiple
observers, public consequences, repeated references, and transmission
across different witnesses.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.24 Unbelieving and Hostile Observers

Several miracle narratives include people who do not initially believe
the messenger.

The Gospels frequently portray opponents of Jesus as responding to His
works. Some question them. Some report them. Some oppose their
significance.

Such disagreement does not itself prove the miracle.

But it shows that the biblical narratives present many miraculous claims
within public controversy rather than only within private devotion.

The event enters social and historical space.

People respond to it.

Reports circulate.

Decisions follow.

The event therefore becomes part of the historical environment in which
the written testimony is situated.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.25 Event and Interpretation

A miracle claim has two dimensions.

### Dimension 1 --- Occurrence

> **What happened?**

### Dimension 2 --- Significance

> **What does it mean?**

The biblical writers frequently supply interpretation alongside the
event.

Peter explains the healing in Acts 3.

Jesus explains the significance of His works.

John structures signs around conclusions concerning Jesus.

This is important because the same observable occurrence could be
assigned different meanings by different observers.

The biblical testimony therefore does not merely preserve phenomena. It
preserves the claimed meaning of those phenomena.

The evidential task is consequently to distinguish:

-   what was observed;
-   what was reported;
-   what was interpreted;
-   and what conclusion is warranted.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.26 Natural Explanation and Divine Agency

A disciplined miracle investigation must distinguish three questions:

1.  **Can the event be explained by ordinary natural processes?**
2.  **If not, what alternative explanations remain?**
3.  **Does the evidence justify attributing the event to God?**

These questions are not identical.

Failure to explain an event is not by itself proof of divine agency.

The positive divine conclusion requires evidence connecting the event to
God.

The biblical narratives often provide such connections through timing,
divine declaration, prophetic context, public manifestation, the
accompanying message, and the event's relation to a known divine
purpose.

Thus:

> **"We cannot explain it" is weaker than "the evidence identifies it as
> a divine act."**

The latter is the relevant evidential claim.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.27 Possibility and Actuality

The possibility of divine action is not the same as proof that a
particular miracle occurred.

If God exists and can act within creation, extraordinary divine action
is conceptually possible.

But possibility does not establish actuality.

Likewise, the fact that an event could be explained by divine action
does not prove that it was.

The investigation therefore asks:

> **What happened?**

then:

> **What best explains the evidence?**

and finally:

> **What does the event establish concerning the associated divine
> claim?**

This keeps the miracle argument within the book's established
distinction between possibility, evidence, inference, and warranted
conclusion.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.28 Providence and Miracle

The Bible presents God as active in ordinary history as well as
extraordinary events.

This requires a distinction between providence and miracle.

Providence concerns God's governing activity through ordinary and
extraordinary circumstances.

A miracle, in the evidential sense relevant here, is an extraordinary
act presented as divine action and often functioning as a sign or
confirmation.

The existence of providence does not make miracles unnecessary.

Nor does the existence of miracles imply that God is inactive in
ordinary events.

The biblical worldview encompasses both.

Authentication may depend particularly upon extraordinary signs because
their unusual character makes them identifiable as signs when connected
with a divine purpose.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.29 The Pattern Across the Canon

The major examples reveal continuity.

### Moses

Manifestation → commission → signs → public deliverance → covenant →
written testimony.

### Elijah

Prophetic claim → public contest → sign → recognition of the LORD.

### The prophets

Divine message → prophetic action or sign → interpretation → witness.

### Jesus

Messianic claims → works/signs → witnesses → interpretation → Gospel
testimony.

### The apostles

Proclamation → signs and wonders → public witness → interpretation →
apostolic testimony.

The circumstances differ, but the evidential architecture remains
recognizable.

This continuity matters because the Bible is not presenting an isolated
theory of miracles. It repeatedly uses manifestations and extraordinary
acts within its larger communication and authentication system.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.30 The Incarnation and Observable Divine Action

The New Testament's claim that the Word became flesh is significant for
the epistemological structure of revelation.

John 1:14 states:

> **"The Word was made flesh."**

The claim is that divine self-disclosure entered human history in an
embodied, observable form.

People could hear Jesus.

They could see His works.

They could follow Him.

They could question Him.

They could oppose Him.

They could witness His death.

They could testify concerning what they encountered.

Thus divine revelation is not presented as necessarily detached from
historical human experience.

The divine-human character of revelation is itself part of the form in
which the claim becomes historically accessible.

This continues the principle already established concerning Scripture's
human witnesses:

> **God communicates through genuinely human historical agents and acts
> within genuinely human historical experience.**

The miraculous therefore does not remove historical testimony. It
creates extraordinary events to which historical testimony can bear
witness.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.31 What a Miracle Cannot Establish by Itself

The evidential force of a miracle must not be exaggerated.

A miracle, considered by itself, does not automatically establish:

-   every statement made by the person associated with it;
-   every theological conclusion later attached to it;
-   the complete authority of every religious system;
-   or every proposition attributed to the messenger.

The conclusion must remain connected to the proposition actually
authenticated.

A sign confirming Moses' commission bears upon Moses' commission.

Jesus' works bearing witness to His identity must be interpreted
alongside His teaching, the Scriptures, His actions, His death, and the
resurrection testimony.

The miracle therefore contributes to a cumulative argument rather than
replacing it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.32 What a Genuine Divine Miracle Can Establish

The limits just stated do not make the evidential conclusion weak. They
make it precise.

If an event is established as genuinely occurring, extraordinary in
character, connected to a divine claim, witnessed credibly, and
presented in context as God's confirming act, then it can provide
evidence for the divine commissioning of a messenger or the divine
authorization of the associated message.

The structure can be stated as:

> **historical occurrence + credible testimony + extraordinary
> character + divine identification + contextual connection to
> messenger/message = evidence for divine authentication.**

The exact strength of the conclusion depends on the evidence for each
component.

This is rational warrant rather than an unsupported leap.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.33 The Bible's Own Authentication Logic

The biblical record repeatedly contains an authentication pattern:

> **God acts → a messenger speaks → a sign accompanies the claim →
> witnesses encounter the event → the event is interpreted → testimony
> is transmitted.**

This is an evidential system.

It does not reduce belief to emotion.

It does not ask the reader to believe simply because something is
mysterious.

It places extraordinary claims within historical testimony and connects
them with identifiable propositions.

This is why miracles can contribute to the investigation of
authenticity.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.34 Miracle Is Not Magic

The supplied *Bible Introduction* makes an important distinction
concerning the Bible itself: it is not an amulet, charm, fetish, or
object whose mere presence is supposed to work wonders; its claim
concerns what occurs when its teaching is studied and practiced. The
same distinction helps clarify the biblical category of miracle.

Biblical miracles are not presented as techniques by which human beings
control supernatural power.

Moses does not control God.

Elijah does not manipulate God.

Jesus does not teach supernatural power as an occult technique.

The apostles do not present divine power as a mechanism independently
controlled by them.

God is the sovereign agent.

Therefore the relevant question is not:

> **Can a human being produce supernatural effects?**

but:

> **Has God acted, and what has God thereby testified?**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.35 Miracles and the Unity of Biblical Communication

The supplied *Bible Introduction* identifies multiple modes associated
with biblical communication, including apostles, visions, dreams,
revelation, and inspiration, and describes the Bible as a body of
writings organized across its Old and New Testament divisions.

Miracles belong within this broader communication architecture.

They occur alongside:

-   divine speech;
-   prophecy;
-   visions;
-   dreams;
-   written revelation;
-   human testimony;
-   covenant;
-   historical events;
-   and later interpretation.

The unity is therefore not merely thematic.

It is structural:

> **God communicates. God acts. Human beings witness. Human beings
> testify. The written record preserves. Later witnesses appeal to
> earlier acts.**

This is the larger framework in which the miracle evidence belongs.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.36 A Working Evidential Test

For a particular miracle claim, the following questions provide a
disciplined test:

### 1. What exactly is claimed to have happened?

Define the event before evaluating it.

### 2. Who claims it happened?

Identify the witnesses.

### 3. Were the witnesses positioned to know?

Consider proximity, access, and opportunity for observation.

### 4. Was the event public or private?

Public events and private experiences have different evidential
structures.

### 5. What was actually observed?

Distinguish observation from later interpretation.

### 6. What alternative explanations exist?

Consider natural processes, coincidence, deception, misunderstanding,
legend, or other relevant explanations.

### 7. What does the event claim to signify?

Identify the proposition beyond the phenomenon.

### 8. What message accompanies it?

The sign must be connected to the message being authenticated.

### 9. Is the event attributed to God?

Identify the claimed source.

### 10. Is the message consistent with the established revelation?

Biblical continuity matters.

### 11. What historical consequences followed?

Consequences can provide additional evidence.

### 12. How was the event transmitted?

Trace the movement from event to testimony to written record.

### 13. What exactly does the evidence establish?

State the conclusion proportionally.

This is the appropriate standard for serious miracle claims.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.37 Why the Miracle Argument Is Cumulative

The chapter does not argue that one miracle, considered in isolation,
establishes the entire doctrine of Scripture.

The book's method is cumulative.

Miracles constitute one evidential category alongside:

-   credible witnesses;
-   prophetic correspondence;
-   unity and continuity;
-   historical corroboration;
-   manuscript transmission;
-   and the cumulative convergence of independent lines of evidence.

The correct conclusion is therefore not:

> **Miracles exist, therefore every biblical claim is established.**

The conclusion is:

> **Where genuine divine manifestations and miracles are historically
> established and their authenticating relationship to a messenger or
> message is demonstrated, they constitute a distinct evidential line
> supporting the claimed divine source.**

That line then converges with other evidence.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.38 Miracles and Rationality

A miracle is sometimes described as a violation of reason.

That formulation confuses ordinary expectation with logical
contradiction.

The proposition that God caused an extraordinary event is not logically
contradictory merely because such an event is outside ordinary
experience.

The evidential question is instead:

> **Did the event occur, and does the evidence justify attributing it to
> God?**

This is why the book's earlier distinctions matter.

A miracle can be possible without having occurred.

An event can be extraordinary without being divine.

An event can be established without every interpretation of it being
established.

The proper method is evidence followed by inference.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.39 The Miracle Does Not Replace the Word

Biblical miracles are repeatedly joined to speech.

Moses speaks.

Elijah speaks.

Jesus teaches.

The apostles preach.

The sign does not eliminate the message.

The sign serves the message.

The word interprets the sign.

This reciprocal relationship is essential:

> **The miracle authenticates within its context; the word explains what
> the miracle means.**

This is why the written record is indispensable. Scripture preserves
both the event and the testimony concerning its significance.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.40 Historical Accessibility

A miracle claim is extraordinary in content but historical in form.

The claim may be:

> **This happened at this time, in this place, before these people, and
> it was understood as an act of God.**

Such a claim can be investigated using historical questions.

The evidence may include:

-   witness testimony;
-   number and character of witnesses;
-   proximity in time;
-   public setting;
-   hostile or non-believing observers;
-   consequences;
-   repeated references;
-   literary transmission;
-   and consistency with the surrounding historical record.

The supernatural content does not make the historical form disappear.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.41 The Reader's Position

The original witness and the modern reader occupy different epistemic
positions.

The witness encounters the event.

The reader encounters testimony concerning the event.

But testimony is itself a legitimate source of historical knowledge when
the witness and transmission are sufficiently credible.

That is why Chapter 35 matters.

The present chapter adds the content of the testimony:

> **What extraordinary acts did the witnesses claim to have
> encountered?**

The evidential architecture therefore combines witness credibility with
event claims.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.42 Divine Manifestation and the Written Graphē

The Graphē preserves claims about divine action in history.

This creates a direct relationship between the miraculous event and the
written record.

The written record does not make the miracle occur.

It preserves the testimony concerning what is claimed to have occurred.

The miracle does not by itself create the written record.

It becomes part of the record through witnesses and authors.

Thus the same divine-human pattern established earlier continues:

> **divine action → human encounter → human testimony → written
> record.**

The human form of the testimony does not remove the divine claim. It is
the historical means by which the claim reaches later readers.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.43 The Special Concentration in Christ

The miracle argument reaches its greatest concentration in Jesus because
the Gospel narratives place His works directly alongside His identity
claims.

His works cannot be separated from His teaching.

His teaching cannot be separated from the Scriptures He interprets.

His identity cannot be separated from His death and resurrection.

His resurrection cannot be separated from apostolic testimony.

Therefore the Gospel miracle evidence forms part of a larger chain:

> **Scripture → expectation → Jesus' claims → works/signs → witnesses →
> death → resurrection → apostolic testimony → written Gospel.**

Chapter 36 establishes why miracles belong within this chain. Later
chapters will examine Christ's historical claims and works, followed by
His death, resurrection, and eyewitness testimony.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.44 The Limits of the Chapter

The chapter has intentionally not attempted to prove every individual
miracle historically.

That would confuse the category with the complete case.

The chapter has established the evidential function that miracles occupy
in the biblical record.

The historical investigation of particular events belongs wherever those
events become central to later arguments.

This is especially important for the resurrection. The resurrection will
receive dedicated treatment because of its central place in apostolic
testimony and the historical claims concerning Jesus Christ.

The present chapter therefore establishes the general evidential
category without prematurely consuming later chapters.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.45 The Cumulative Architecture

The investigation can now be stated as an accumulating chain.

### God

God is the ultimate source of truth and revelation established in the
earlier movement.

### Revelation

God communicates.

### Human agents

Prophets, apostles, and other witnesses genuinely receive, experience,
speak, and write.

### Manifestation

God's activity becomes perceptible in history.

### Miracle/sign

An extraordinary act functions as a sign, wonder, mighty work, judgment,
deliverance, or confirmation.

### Witness

People encounter the event.

### Interpretation

The event is explained in relation to God's purpose and message.

### Authentication

The event bears witness to the claimed divine source.

### Written testimony

The testimony is preserved.

The resulting chain is:

> **God → revelation → messenger → manifestation → miracle/sign →
> witness → interpretation → authentication → written testimony.**

This is the proper place of miracles in the doctrine of the Graphē.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.46 The Necessary Conclusion

The biblical evidence supports a precise conclusion.

The Bible presents divine manifestations and miracles as acts of God
within its historical and revelatory narrative.

It presents signs, wonders, and mighty works as capable of bearing
witness to divine activity.

It presents signs associated with Moses as confirmation of his divine
commission.

It presents Elijah's sign on Carmel as evidence concerning the identity
of the LORD.

It presents Jesus' works as signs bearing upon His identity and mission.

It presents apostolic signs and wonders as divine confirmation
accompanying proclamation.

It also explicitly warns that signs must be evaluated in relation to the
message and the true God.

Therefore the biblical position is neither:

> **Extraordinary event = automatic truth**

nor:

> **Extraordinary event = irrelevant to truth.**

The precise conclusion is:

> **When an extraordinary event is genuinely established and is
> presented within a coherent revelatory context as God's act, the event
> functions as evidence bearing upon the divine source of the associated
> messenger, message, or claim.**

That is the warranted contribution of miracles to the cumulative
authentication case.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 36.47 What Has Been Established

This chapter has established:

1.  Divine manifestation is broader than miracle.
2.  Biblical signs point beyond themselves to a person, message, divine
    purpose, or identity.
3.  Moses' signs are explicitly connected to the recognition of his
    divine commissioning.
4.  The Exodus presents public divine action that becomes historical and
    communal testimony.
5.  Elijah's Carmel sign functions within a defined public question
    concerning the LORD's identity.
6.  Jesus' works are presented as signs and witnesses bearing upon His
    identity and mission.
7.  Apostolic signs and wonders are presented as accompanying divine
    confirmation of proclamation.
8.  The Bible itself distinguishes genuine divine authentication from
    false or deceptive signs.
9.  The occurrence of a miracle and the interpretation of what it
    establishes are distinct questions.
10. A genuine divine miracle can provide evidence for divine
    commissioning or authorization when the event is properly connected
    to the messenger or message.
11. Miracles therefore constitute a distinct evidential line within the
    book's cumulative case rather than a substitute for the entire case.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Chapter Summary

> **The chapter's central conclusion:** The Bible presents divine
> manifestation and miracles as historical acts of God that can function
> as signs and divine witness---revealing God's activity and, where
> expressly connected to a messenger or message, providing evidential
> confirmation of divine commissioning and revelation.

The essential evidential architecture is:

> **Divine act → manifestation → sign → witness → interpretation →
> authentication → testimony → written record.**

The next evidential question follows naturally:

> **If God can authenticate a messenger or message through an act
> occurring in history, can God also authenticate revelation through
> knowledge of events before they occur?**

That question leads to the next chapter.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Transition to Chapter 37

## Chapter 37 --- Prophecy and Fulfillment

> **Examine predictive prophecy and fulfillment as evidence. Distinguish
> genuine predictive correspondence from vague retrospective
> interpretation.**

The investigation now moves from extraordinary acts occurring in history
to predictive claims concerning what will occur in history.

The question will not be whether the Bible contains statements later
interpreted as predictions.

The question will be whether specific predictive claims can be shown to
precede their alleged fulfillment and correspond with later events in a
manner sufficiently definite to function as evidence.

The same governing standard remains:

> **Define the claim. Establish the chronology. Examine the text.
> Establish the correspondence. Test alternative explanations. State
> precisely what follows.**

The cumulative investigation continues.
