# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT I — TRUTH AND THE POSSIBILITY OF KNOWLEDGE

# Chapter 3 — Truth and Error

> **Central question:** How is truth distinguished from error?

Chapter 1 established truth in relation to reality. Chapter 2 examined the relation between truth and what actually is. This chapter now takes the necessary next step: **if a proposition can correspond to reality or fail to correspond to reality, how do we distinguish the true proposition from the erroneous one?**

The investigation must therefore move from:

> **What is true?**

to:

> **How do we recognize when a claim is true rather than erroneous?**

That question cannot be answered merely by naming sources of error. It requires a disciplined account of **comparison, evidence, perception, reasoning, testimony, interpretation, and judgment**.

---

## 1. Truth and Error Are Opposed by Correspondence

If truth concerns conformity to reality, error is the contrary condition: a claim fails to conform adequately to what is actually the case.

The basic distinction is therefore:

> **Truth corresponds to reality. Error does not.**

Consider two propositions concerning the same event:

> **Proposition A:** The event occurred.

> **Proposition B:** The event did not occur.

If the event actually occurred, A corresponds to reality and B does not.

The truth of A does not depend upon how many people believe A. Nor does the falsity of B depend upon whether the person asserting B is sincere.

The decisive question is the event itself.

This gives the investigation an objective point of reference:

> **Reality adjudicates between competing propositions.**

The investigator must therefore resist the temptation to treat disagreement itself as proof that truth cannot be known.

Two people can disagree because:

- one has better evidence;
- one has misunderstood the evidence;
- one has incomplete information;
- one has reasoned incorrectly;
- one has interpreted the evidence differently;
- or both may be mistaken.

Disagreement establishes that there is a disagreement.

It does not establish that there is no truth.

---

# 2. Error Does Not Become Truth Through Sincerity

A person may sincerely believe a proposition and still be mistaken.

The important distinction is between the **mental condition of the person** and the **truth condition of the proposition**.

Suppose a witness sincerely reports that he saw an event. His sincerity is relevant to evaluating his testimony. It may increase the plausibility that he is reporting what he remembers rather than deliberately fabricating it.

But sincerity cannot transform an inaccurate perception into an accurate one.

A sincere witness can:

- misperceive;
- misremember;
- misunderstand;
- confuse persons or events;
- infer more than he actually observed.

Therefore:

> **Sincerity may bear upon testimony; it does not constitute truth.**

This distinction is important without turning sincerity into a negative concept. Sincerity is a valuable quality of a witness or investigator. It simply answers a different question.

**Sincerity asks:** Is the person genuinely reporting what he believes or knows?

**Truth asks:** Does the proposition correspond to reality?

The two questions must not be collapsed.

---

# 3. Error Can Occur at Different Stages of Knowing

Error does not enter human knowledge in only one way.

A useful analytical sequence is:

> **Reality → perception → information → interpretation → inference → judgment → proposition**

An error can arise at any stage.

### Perceptual error

A person may perceive an object incorrectly.

### Informational error

A person may receive an inaccurate or incomplete report.

### Interpretive error

A person may misunderstand what a statement means.

### Inferential error

A person may possess true premises but draw an invalid conclusion.

### Judgment error

A person may possess relevant evidence but weigh it improperly.

### Reporting error

A person may accurately remember an event but communicate it inaccurately.

This is why serious investigation cannot stop at:

> “I saw it.”

Nor can it stop at:

> “Someone said it.”

The investigator must ask what was actually observed, what was communicated, what was inferred, and whether the final proposition follows.

This distinction will later become essential when the biblical record is examined. The existence of testimony does not eliminate the need for reasoning; rather, testimony becomes one kind of evidence whose relation to reality must be examined.

---

# 4. Not Every Difference Is a Contradiction

A second major distinction concerns disagreement.

Two statements are not contradictory merely because they are different.

For example:

> “Peter entered the house.”

and:

> “John entered the house.”

may both be true.

Likewise:

> “Jesus was in Galilee.”

and:

> “Jesus was in Jerusalem.”

may both be true if they refer to different times.

A contradiction requires incompatible claims concerning the **same subject, in the same respect, at the same time, under the same relevant conditions**.

This principle is indispensable when examining a collection of historical writings.

Different witnesses may describe different aspects of the same event.

Different writers may emphasize different details.

One account may provide information another does not.

Variation is therefore not automatically contradiction.

At the same time, genuine contradiction cannot simply be dismissed by declaring that all differences are complementary.

The proper method is examination:

1. Identify the propositions.
2. Determine precisely what each statement asserts.
3. Determine whether they concern the same matter in the same respect.
4. Examine context and temporal sequence.
5. Determine whether the propositions are actually incompatible.
6. If they are incompatible, investigate which proposition corresponds to reality.

This is an example of **dianoetic thinking**: analysis before judgment.

---

# 5. Truth Must Be Distinguished From Opinion

An opinion is a person's judgment, view, or assessment.

It may be correct.

It may be incorrect.

Its classification as an opinion does not settle its truth.

A person can say:

> “In my opinion, this happened.”

The phrase “in my opinion” does not make the proposition false. It simply identifies the statement as the speaker's judgment.

The important question remains:

> **What grounds the judgment?**

This is why the mature investigator does not merely collect opinions.

He examines:

- the proposition;
- the evidence;
- the premises;
- the inference;
- the conclusion.

A person who says, “That is just your opinion,” has not necessarily answered the proposition. He may merely have identified that another person holds a view.

The proper response is:

> **What makes the proposition true or false?**

That question moves the discussion back to reality.

---

# 6. Truth Is Not Determined by Popularity

Popularity can tell us how widely a proposition is accepted.

It cannot, by itself, determine whether the proposition is true.

If one person believes that the earth is spherical and one billion people believe otherwise, the number of believers does not determine the shape of the earth.

The same principle applies to questions of doctrine, history, Scripture, and God.

A majority can be mistaken.

A minority can be mistaken.

A majority can also be correct.

The size of the group does not settle the correspondence between proposition and reality.

This does not make collective testimony irrelevant. Independent agreement among many witnesses can be powerful evidence when the witnesses are genuinely independent and competent to testify.

The distinction is therefore:

> **Agreement may be evidence; agreement is not the definition of truth.**

---

# 7. Truth Is Not Determined by Tradition

Tradition concerns what has been transmitted from earlier generations.

Transmission can preserve truth.

Transmission can also preserve error.

Therefore tradition must itself be examined.

An ancient proposition is not true merely because it is ancient.

Likewise, a recent proposition is not false merely because it is recent.

When a traditional teaching is encountered, the disciplined investigator asks:

> What is the claim?

> What is its evidence?

> What is its origin?

> How was it transmitted?

> Does it correspond to reality?

> What reasoning supports it?

This becomes especially important when examining claims about Scripture.

A doctrine cannot acquire divine authority merely because generations of interpreters have repeated it.

If the doctrine is true, it must be possible to establish why it is true.

If it is derived from Scripture, the derivation must be demonstrable.

Thus tradition may become part of the historical evidence surrounding a claim without becoming the final standard of truth.

---

# 8. Truth Is Not Determined by Institutional Status

The same reasoning applies to institutions.

A school may teach a proposition.

A church or religious organization may adopt a doctrine.

A university may publish an interpretation.

A government may establish a policy.

A respected scholar may defend a thesis.

None of those facts, by themselves, determines whether the proposition corresponds to reality.

Institutional authority and truth are therefore distinct concepts.

This does not mean institutions have no legitimate authority within their proper domains.

It means that institutional recognition cannot substitute for truth.

If an institution makes a claim about Scripture, God, history, or reality, the claim remains subject to examination.

This distinction will become decisive later when the investigation asks what makes the Holy Writ authoritative. The authority of the written record cannot be explained by saying merely that an institution recognized it. The direction of explanation must ultimately run toward the grounds of the record's authority.

---

# 9. Repetition Can Preserve Truth Without Creating It

A true proposition may be quoted repeatedly.

A false proposition may also be quoted repeatedly.

Repetition therefore has no magical power to change truth into error or error into truth.

But repetition does have an important function.

It can preserve information.

A written record may transmit a proposition across generations. A quotation may accurately reproduce a statement. A chain of testimony may preserve knowledge of an event.

The intellectual question is therefore not:

> “Was this quoted?”

but:

> **“What was quoted, is the quotation accurate, what does the statement mean, and is the proposition true?”**

This distinction becomes important for Scripture because quotation can be genuine evidence that a text existed and was being used. Yet quotation must still be understood.

A person can quote an authoritative text and misunderstand it.

A person can quote a true proposition and use it to support a conclusion that does not follow.

A person can also quote a text accurately and draw a conclusion that the text genuinely establishes.

Therefore quotation and reasoning belong together.

> **Accurate quotation preserves a statement; sound reasoning determines what follows from it.**

---

# 10. Error Can Occur Even When the Premises Are True

One of the most important forms of error is invalid inference.

Suppose these propositions are true:

1. All human beings die.
2. Socrates is a human being.

The conclusion:

> Socrates dies.

follows.

But suppose someone reasons:

1. All human beings die.
2. Socrates dies.

Therefore:

> Socrates is a human being.

That conclusion does not necessarily follow from those two premises. Other beings may die.

The example illustrates a critical distinction:

> **True premises do not guarantee a true conclusion when the inference is invalid.**

Therefore the investigation of truth cannot consist only of collecting true statements.

It must also examine the reasoning connecting statements.

This is why the architecture places:

**Chapter 4 — Knowledge, Evidence, and Rational Warrant**

before:

**Chapter 5 — Reason, Inference, and Ratiocination.**

The investigation first establishes what constitutes support for knowledge and then develops the reasoning by which conclusions are drawn from that support.

---

# 11. Scripture Must Be Handled Without Inventing Contradictions

When the investigation eventually turns directly to Scripture, the same standard will apply.

The reader should neither:

- manufacture contradictions where none exist,

nor:

- conceal genuine difficulties by refusing to examine them.

Both are failures of disciplined inquiry.

The correct procedure is to examine the actual propositions.

For each alleged contradiction, ask:

### What exactly does passage A assert?

### What exactly does passage B assert?

### Are they speaking about the same thing?

### Are they speaking in the same sense?

### Are they referring to the same time?

### Is one statement broader or narrower than the other?

### Does context qualify either statement?

### Is one statement reporting what someone said rather than affirming the statement as true?

### Does the conclusion of contradiction actually follow?

These questions are not evasions.

They are the minimum intellectual discipline required before declaring two propositions contradictory.

The same principle applies outside Scripture. It is simply the general logic of responsible investigation.

---

# 12. Error and the Difference Between Report and Assertion

A written record can contain a report of what someone said without thereby affirming that everything said by that person is true.

This distinction must be handled carefully when interpreting any historical document.

For example, a historical record may accurately report:

> “Person X said Y.”

The truth of the historical proposition

> “Person X said Y”

is different from the truth of the proposition:

> “Y is true.”

The first concerns what Person X said.

The second concerns the content of Person X's statement.

Therefore the investigator must distinguish:

> **recorded speech**

from

> **the truth-value of the speech recorded.**

This distinction does not diminish the truthfulness of the record. In fact, it can demonstrate the precision of the record: a truthful record can accurately report both true and false statements made by different persons.

The crucial question is always:

> **What proposition is the text itself asserting, and what proposition is it merely reporting as having been spoken?**

That question belongs especially to the hermeneutical discipline developed later in this book.

---

# 13. Error and the Need for Definitions

Many disputes that appear to concern truth actually concern undefined terms.

Two people may argue over “faith,” “knowledge,” “authority,” “science,” “religion,” “Scripture,” or “truth” while using the words differently.

The disagreement may therefore be partly verbal before it is substantive.

A disciplined investigation begins by asking:

> **What does the term mean in this argument?**

Then:

> **What proposition is actually being asserted?**

Then:

> **What evidence supports it?**

Then:

> **Does the conclusion follow?**

Definitions are therefore not academic decoration.

They determine what proposition is actually under examination.

This is why the later treatment of **γραφή (*graphē*)**, Holy Writ, Scripture, Bible, revelation, inspiration, authentication, canon, transmission, and infallibility will begin by defining the terms rather than allowing familiar words to carry undefined assumptions.

---

# 14. The Biblical Imperative Toward Discernment

The biblical record itself repeatedly requires distinction and judgment.

The reader is not instructed to accept every proposition indiscriminately.

The New Testament contains commands and examples involving:

- proving;
- trying;
- discerning;
- judging;
- examining;
- searching;
- comparing;
- remembering;
- understanding;
- and rightly handling.

These categories will receive fuller treatment in later chapters, particularly the chapters on knowledge, reason, dianoetic thinking, Isaiah 1:18, and rightly dividing.

The important point here is methodological:

> **The biblical pursuit of truth requires discrimination between what is true and what is false.**

That means intellectual passivity is not a sufficient method for handling a text that itself requires understanding and judgment.

---

# 15. Truth, Error, and the Investigator's Own Fallibility

There is another necessary distinction.

To recognize that human beings can err does not mean that truth cannot be known.

It means that the investigator must exercise discipline.

The appropriate response to human fallibility is not intellectual paralysis.

It is improved method.

If perception can err, examine perception.

If memory can err, examine records and corroboration.

If inference can err, examine premises and logical form.

If interpretation can err, examine language and context.

If testimony can err, examine witnesses and evidence.

If institutions can err, examine their claims.

If traditions can err, examine their origins and grounds.

If one's own reasoning can err, submit one's reasoning to examination.

This produces an important intellectual posture:

> **Humility about the investigator does not require skepticism about reality.**

One can acknowledge:

> “I may be mistaken.”

while still insisting:

> **“There is a fact of the matter, and I should investigate it carefully.”**

That is the posture required for serious pursuit of truth.

---

# 16. A Practical Test for Truth Claims

A disciplined investigator can therefore subject a truth claim to a series of questions.

### 1. What exactly is being claimed?

State the proposition precisely.

### 2. What does the proposition refer to?

Identify the reality, event, person, object, or relationship under consideration.

### 3. What evidence bears upon it?

Separate relevant evidence from irrelevant information.

### 4. What is the quality of that evidence?

Examine directness, reliability, independence, consistency, completeness, and corroboration where applicable.

### 5. What assumptions are being introduced?

Identify premises that have not yet been demonstrated.

### 6. What inference connects the evidence to the conclusion?

Do not leave the inferential step hidden.

### 7. Does the conclusion actually follow?

A conclusion can be plausible without being established.

### 8. What alternative explanations exist?

Consider competing explanations rather than protecting a preferred conclusion from examination.

### 9. Is the conclusion proportionate to the evidence?

Do not infer more than the evidence warrants.

### 10. What would count against the claim?

A claim that cannot even theoretically be examined has not been made sufficiently precise for rational investigation.

This procedure does not eliminate every possibility of error.

It makes error **detectable and correctable**.

That is a major difference.

---

# 17. From Error to Rational Warrant

We can now see why Chapter 3 must lead directly into Chapter 4.

If truth is correspondence to reality, then knowledge cannot be established merely by preference.

If error is failure of correspondence, then we require means of distinguishing correspondence from non-correspondence.

That requires:

- evidence;
- testimony;
- perception;
- information;
- reasoning;
- inference;
- comparison;
- corroboration;
- and judgment.

But even this list is not enough.

The investigator must also ask:

> **When are these grounds sufficient to warrant a conclusion?**

That is the question of **rational warrant**.

A piece of evidence can be relevant without being sufficient.

A witness can be sincere without being accurate.

A quotation can be authentic without proving every conclusion drawn from it.

A premise can be true while an inference from it is invalid.

An argument can sound persuasive while containing an unstated assumption.

Therefore the next stage of the investigation must determine what separates **mere support** from **adequate rational warrant**.

---

# Chapter Conclusion

Truth and error are not distinguished by popularity, sincerity, institutional status, tradition, repetition, or confidence.

They are distinguished ultimately by the relationship between a proposition and reality.

Yet human beings encounter reality through perception, testimony, records, language, interpretation, inference, and judgment. Error can therefore enter at multiple stages.

The answer is not to abandon reason.

Nor is it to abandon the possibility of truth.

The answer is disciplined investigation.

> **Define the claim. Examine the evidence. Identify the premises. Test the inference. Compare the alternatives. Determine whether the conclusion actually follows.**

This is the intellectual discipline required before the investigation can responsibly ask what constitutes knowledge.

And that question is now unavoidable:

> **What is knowledge, what counts as evidence, and what makes evidence sufficient to warrant a conclusion?**

That is the subject of the next chapter.

|---|---|
| Truth / error | Truth corresponds to reality; error does not. |

## Authoring Guardrail

> **Do not merely tell the reader that error exists. Show how error can arise, how truth and error are distinguished, and why the proposed method is rationally capable of correcting error.**

> **Do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture. Develop the intellectual discipline that will later enable the reader to reason through Scripture rather than merely repeat it.**

> **Do not use an institution, tradition, majority, commentator, or personal experience as a substitute for the underlying question: does the proposition correspond to reality?**

> **Do not allow the reader to confuse a person's sincerity with the truth of the proposition being asserted.**

> **Do not manufacture contradictions, but do not explain away genuine incompatibilities without examining them.**
