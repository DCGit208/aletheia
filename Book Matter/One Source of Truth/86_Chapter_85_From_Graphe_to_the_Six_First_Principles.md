# Chapter 85 — From Graphē to the Six First Principles

## Movement X — The Scholarly Labour of the Graphē

### Central Objective

Conclude by demonstrating why the Doctrine of the Graphē functions as the foundation/gate for entering the Six First Principles without becoming a seventh principle.

---

## 85.1 The Question at the End of the Work

The workman has been prepared.

The source has been examined.

The authority of the written Word has been established within the architecture of the book.

The reader has been led through truth, knowledge, evidence, reasoning, revelation, Scripture, authentication, authority, interpretation, diligence, exercise, maturity, discernment, right handling, and approved workmanship.

One question remains.

What does the prepared workman do with this foundation?

The answer cannot be:

> “Add another principle.”

The answer is:

> **Enter the principles that have already been identified as the foundation of the doctrine of Christ.**

This is the final movement of *One Source of Truth — The Doctrine of the Graphē*.

The question of this chapter is therefore precise:

> **Why does the Doctrine of the Graphē come before the Six First Principles, and why must it remain a foundation and gate rather than being counted as a seventh first principle?**

That distinction is not administrative.

It is architectural.

---

## 85.2 The Book Has Reached Its Boundary

A book must know where its work ends.

The present work has not been written to replace the Foundation School.

It has not been written as a seventh course.

It has not been written to absorb the Six First Principles into the Doctrine of the Graphē.

It has been written to establish the learner's relationship to the written authority from which those principles are to be studied.

This distinction protects both works.

If the Doctrine of the Graphē were turned into a seventh principle, the architecture would be altered.

If the Six First Principles were absorbed into the Doctrine of the Graphē, the present book would exceed its assigned task.

The proper relationship is therefore:

> **Graphē → preparation → readiness → Six First Principles.**

The Graphē is the foundation and gate.

The Six First Principles remain the Six First Principles.

---

## 85.3 Hebrews Creates the Transition

The transition is not invented by this book.

It arises from the biblical sequence in Hebrews.

Hebrews 5:11–14 addresses dullness of hearing, the need for growth, the first principles of the oracles of God, milk, strong meat, maturity, and trained discernment.

Hebrews 6:1 then continues:

> **“Therefore leaving the principles of the doctrine of Christ, let us go on unto perfection.”**

The movement is visible.

There is a foundation.

There is a need for maturity.

There is a movement from foundational instruction toward fuller development.

The present book has spent its scholarly labour preparing the learner to enter that movement responsibly.

It does not replace the foundation.

It prepares the learner to handle the foundation.

---

## 85.4 The First Principles Are Named in Scripture

Hebrews 6:1–2 identifies the foundational matters in a sequence.

They are:

1. **Repentance from dead works**
2. **Faith toward God**
3. **The doctrine of baptisms**
4. **Laying on of hands**
5. **Resurrection of the dead**
6. **Eternal judgment**

These are the Six First Principles.

They are not six topics invented by the present manuscript.

They arise from the text of Hebrews.

The responsibility of the present chapter is therefore not to redefine them, replace them, or create another principle beside them.

Its responsibility is to explain why the Doctrine of the Graphē must precede their serious study.

---

## 85.5 “The First Principles of the Oracles of God”

Hebrews 5:12 speaks of:

> **“the first principles of the oracles of God.”**

The language is important.

The first principles are not presented as disposable material.

They are foundational instruction.

The problem in Hebrews is not that the foundation is unnecessary.

The problem is that learners who ought to have progressed still require the foundation to be taught to them again.

Foundation and maturity therefore belong together.

A mature learner does not despise the foundation.

He carries it correctly.

He understands it more deeply.

He builds upon it.

He moves forward because the foundation has become secure enough to support further development.

---

## 85.6 “Leaving” Does Not Mean Abandoning

Hebrews 6:1 says:

> **“Therefore leaving the principles of the doctrine of Christ...”**

The word “leaving” must not be interpreted as though the writer were instructing believers to discard the principles.

The surrounding argument does not support that conclusion.

The movement is toward maturity.

The writer is not saying:

> Forget repentance.

> Forget faith.

> Forget baptisms.

> Forget laying on of hands.

> Forget resurrection.

> Forget judgment.

The movement is instead from repeatedly laying the same foundation toward building upon it.

The foundation is not denied by progress.

Progress depends upon it.

---

## 85.7 The Foundation Must Be Laid Before One Builds

A foundation has a particular function.

It establishes what supports what follows.

A building does not become more advanced by pretending that its foundation does not matter.

Likewise, intellectual development does not become more mature by despising foundational propositions.

The first principles provide foundational doctrinal material.

The Doctrine of the Graphē provides something different.

It establishes the authority relationship by which the learner approaches that doctrinal material.

That difference is decisive.

---

## 85.8 Two Different Questions

The Six First Principles answer one class of questions.

The Doctrine of the Graphē answers another.

The first principles concern foundational matters within the doctrine of Christ.

The Doctrine of the Graphē concerns the written authority by which the learner is to know, examine, understand, and rightly handle what is taught.

Therefore the two should not be confused.

The question:

> **What are the first principles?**

is answered by Hebrews 6:1–2.

The question:

> **By what written authority should I establish, examine, interpret, and handle these principles?**

belongs to the Doctrine of the Graphē.

The second question prepares the learner for the first.

It does not become a replacement for it.

---

## 85.9 The Graphē Is Not Another Principle

This distinction can now be stated plainly:

> **The Doctrine of the Graphē is not the seventh first principle.**

It is the doctrine that establishes the learner's relationship to the written authority from which the first principles are to be received and handled.

Calling it a “seventh principle” would therefore confuse category and function.

A gate is not automatically one of the rooms to which it gives access.

A foundation is not automatically one of the courses built upon it.

A governing authority is not automatically another proposition inside the curriculum it governs.

The Graphē occupies this different structural position.

---

## 85.10 Foundation and Principle Are Not Synonyms

The word “foundation” can be used in more than one sense.

A foundation may refer to foundational doctrine.

It may refer to the supporting basis of a structure.

It may refer to the starting point from which further construction occurs.

These meanings must not be collapsed.

The Six First Principles are foundational doctrinal matters.

The Doctrine of the Graphē is foundational to the learner's relationship with the written source and authority of those doctrines.

Thus:

> **Foundational does not mean identical in category.**

Something can be foundational without being one of the propositions contained in the foundation it prepares the learner to study.

---

## 85.11 Source and Subject Must Be Distinguished

The distinction becomes clearer through another pair:

> **source**

and

> **subject.**

The Graphē is the written source.

The Six First Principles are subjects of foundational instruction.

The present book has concentrated upon the nature, authority, handling, and scholarly engagement of that written source.

The Foundation School proceeds into the subjects named in Hebrews.

The source and the subjects are related.

They are not identical.

---

## 85.12 The Written Word Governs the Study

The central relationship is therefore:

> **Holy Writ governs the study of the Six First Principles.**

The learner does not approach the principles as though the teacher, textbook, institution, tradition, method, or personal experience were the final authority.

The learner approaches them under the authority of the written Word.

This is the practical fruit of the Doctrine of the Graphē.

The book has not spent eighty-five chapters establishing an academic technique merely so that the learner can substitute the technique for Scripture.

Method remains subordinate.

Teacher remains subordinate.

Textbook remains subordinate.

Institution remains subordinate.

Scholarship remains subordinate.

The Graphē remains primary.

---

## 85.13 The Book Is Not the Authority

This point must include the present book itself.

*One Source of Truth* does not become the authority merely because it has spent the entire book teaching about authority.

Its conclusions must remain accountable to the Graphē.

The reader should therefore never be taught:

> “The book says it, therefore it is true.”

The proper relationship is:

> **The book presents an argument from Scripture and relevant evidence; the reader must examine the argument and the written Word.**

The book is a servant.

It is not the source.

---

## 85.14 The Teacher Is Not the Authority

The same principle applies to the teacher.

A teacher may possess greater experience.

A teacher may possess greater knowledge.

A teacher may be skilled in explanation.

A teacher may have spent years studying the subject.

None of these qualities make the teacher the primary written authority.

The teacher's task is to help the learner see what the source establishes.

A faithful teacher therefore directs the learner toward the Word rather than permanently toward himself.

---

## 85.15 The Method Is Not the Authority

The preceding chapters have developed method.

Perception.

Learning.

Understanding.

Reasoning.

Comparison.

Distinction.

Inference.

Testing.

Conclusion.

Application.

This method is valuable.

But it is not Scripture.

A method can help the learner handle Scripture responsibly.

It cannot become a rival source of revelation.

It cannot make an erroneous conclusion true.

It cannot confer infallibility upon the interpreter.

The method is a servant of the work.

The Graphē remains the authority.

---

## 85.16 Scholarship Is Not the Authority

Scholarship has also received serious attention.

Lexicons can assist.

Historical studies can assist.

Manuscript studies can assist.

Commentaries can assist.

Technical resources can assist.

Teachers can assist.

Digital tools can assist.

Artificial intelligence can assist.

But assistance is not authority.

A secondary source can clarify.

A secondary source can challenge.

A secondary source can expose an error.

A secondary source can provide evidence.

A secondary source can even reveal that a commonly held interpretation is weak.

But the secondary source does not thereby become the primary written authority.

---

## 85.17 Why the Distinction Matters for the Six Principles

The Six First Principles are important enough that careless handling of them would be serious.

If the learner approaches repentance from dead works through tradition alone, he may inherit an incomplete definition.

If he approaches faith through slogans alone, he may mistake emotional confidence for the biblical subject.

If he approaches baptisms without distinguishing the relevant scriptural categories, he may collapse distinctions that the source preserves.

If he approaches laying on of hands without examining its occasions and purposes, he may confuse different practices.

If he approaches resurrection without attending to the biblical teaching concerning the dead and resurrection, he may reduce a substantial doctrine to a phrase.

If he approaches eternal judgment without examining its scriptural scope, he may substitute inherited imagery for the written teaching.

The gate therefore matters.

The learner must first understand what it means for the written Word to govern the study.

---

## 85.18 The Graphē Establishes the Epistemic Posture

The Doctrine of the Graphē establishes a posture before it establishes a curriculum.

The learner comes as a hearer.

As a student.

As a workman.

As a servant.

As one accountable to God.

He does not come as an editor of Scripture.

He does not come as a judge above Scripture.

He does not come to make the text agree with his preferred system.

He comes to understand what has been written and to handle it faithfully.

That posture is essential before entering the first principles.

---

## 85.19 The Learner Must Know What Kind of Statement He Is Hearing

The scholarly labour developed in Movement X now becomes practical.

When the learner encounters a statement in a lesson, he should ask:

> Is this directly stated in Scripture?

> Is this an interpretation?

> Is this a necessary inference?

> Is this a possible inference?

> Is this a theological synthesis?

> Is this an application?

> Is this historical background?

> Is this the teacher's explanation?

Those questions protect the learner from confusing the source with commentary about the source.

This is one of the most important outcomes of the Doctrine of the Graphē.

---

## 85.20 The Gate Protects Against Doctrinal Substitution

Without this discipline, a curriculum can gradually replace Scripture.

The teacher's explanation becomes the doctrine.

The textbook's wording becomes the doctrine.

The institution's official statement becomes the doctrine.

The tradition's formula becomes the doctrine.

The learner eventually remembers what the teacher said but cannot distinguish it from what Scripture says.

The Doctrine of the Graphē refuses that substitution.

The written Word remains the governing source.

---

## 85.21 The Gate Also Protects Against Private Authority

The opposite error is equally serious.

A learner may reject every teacher, every scholarly resource, every historical witness, and every disciplined interpretive method in the name of “just reading the Bible.”

That is not automatically maturity.

The preceding chapters have shown why perception, learning, reasoning, comparison, lexical attention, context, exercise, and correction matter.

The Graphē is primary.

But the learner still needs skill to handle it.

Therefore the proper posture is neither blind dependence nor arrogant isolation.

It is disciplined submission to the written Word.

---

## 85.22 The Six Principles Remain the Six

The architecture can therefore be stated without ambiguity:

> **One Source of Truth — The Doctrine of the Graphē**

> ↓

> **Study, labour, mastery, and demonstrated readiness**

> ↓

> **The Six First Principles**

The Six remain six.

Nothing in the present book changes that number.

Nothing in the Doctrine of the Graphē should be inserted into Hebrews 6:1–2 as an additional principle.

Nothing should be removed from Hebrews because the present book has been written.

The architecture is preserved by refusing both addition and subtraction.

---

## 85.23 The Six Principles in Their Biblical Order

The learner should enter them in the order given by the text:

### First — Repentance from Dead Works

The first principle concerns repentance from dead works.

The learner must examine what the phrase means from Scripture rather than importing a definition from popular religious usage.

### Second — Faith Toward God

The second concerns faith toward God.

The learner must examine faith as the text and wider biblical witness present it, distinguishing biblical faith from assumptions about faith.

### Third — The Doctrine of Baptisms

The third concerns baptisms.

The plural matters.

The learner must preserve the textual distinction rather than flattening the subject into one undifferentiated practice.

### Fourth — Laying on of Hands

The fourth concerns laying on of hands.

The learner must examine its biblical occasions and purposes rather than assigning one meaning to every occurrence.

### Fifth — Resurrection of the Dead

The fifth concerns resurrection of the dead.

The learner must examine the biblical teaching rather than reducing resurrection to a general symbol of hope.

### Sixth — Eternal Judgment

The sixth concerns eternal judgment.

The learner must examine the biblical testimony concerning judgment and its implications with the same discipline.

These brief descriptions do not constitute the full teaching of the six courses.

They establish the destination.

---

## 85.24 Why This Chapter Must Not Teach All Six Principles

A temptation now arises.

The book has reached the gate.

Why not simply begin teaching the six principles here?

Because that would violate the architecture.

The assigned task of this chapter is to explain the transition.

The Six First Principles have their own instructional field.

Their detailed treatment belongs to the Foundation School and its corresponding course and textbook architecture.

If this chapter attempted to exhaust them, the gate would become the school.

The boundary would disappear.

A rigorous work must know not only what to say, but what not to consume prematurely.

---

## 85.25 The Difference Between Introduction and Exhaustion

A gate can identify what lies beyond it without becoming everything beyond it.

This chapter may therefore name the six principles.

It may establish their biblical order.

It may explain why the learner must enter them under the authority of the Graphē.

It may explain why the foundation must be handled correctly.

But it should not pretend that naming a subject equals teaching it exhaustively.

The reader should leave this chapter prepared to study.

Not falsely convinced that the study is already complete.

---

## 85.26 Readiness Is More Than Enrollment

Entering the Six First Principles is not merely a matter of being enrolled in a course.

The preceding chapters have established a stronger idea of readiness.

Readiness involves capacity.

The learner should know how to perceive.

He should know how to learn.

He should know how to reason.

He should know how to distinguish quotation from interpretation.

He should know how to examine context.

He should know how to identify inference.

He should know how to test conclusions.

He should know how to recognize his own limitations.

He should know how to return to the text.

He should know how to accept correction.

This is why the Doctrine of the Graphē is a gate.

It prepares the learner's relationship to the source before the learner enters the doctrinal curriculum.

---

## 85.27 The Gate Is a Formation of Responsibility

The purpose of the gate is not merely intellectual.

It forms responsibility.

Once the learner understands that the written Word is authoritative, he becomes responsible for how he handles it.

The question is no longer simply:

> “What do I believe?”

It becomes:

> **“What does the written Word establish, and have I handled it faithfully?”**

That is the question of the workman.

It is also the question the learner carries into the Six First Principles.

---

## 85.28 The Gate Connects Knowledge and Obedience

The Doctrine of the Graphē also protects against the separation of knowledge from life.

The written Word is not merely an archive.

The learner is expected to hear, understand, believe, obey, and live according to what the Word establishes.

But application must remain downstream from interpretation.

The learner should not first decide what he wants to do and then recruit Scripture to justify it.

The order remains:

> **Word → understanding → reasoning → conclusion → obedience.**

The gate therefore establishes both intellectual and moral responsibility.

---

## 85.29 The Foundation Is Not a Barrier to Maturity

A learner can misunderstand “foundation” as though the foundation were a permanent stopping place.

Hebrews does not teach that.

The movement is:

> **foundation → maturity.**

The learner does not remain forever at the first stage.

But neither does maturity permit him to discard the foundation.

The mature learner carries foundational truth into more developed understanding.

He becomes capable of strong meat because his faculties have been exercised.

He becomes capable of teaching because he has learned to handle the Word.

He moves forward without moving away from the authority that made forward movement possible.

---

## 85.30 The Six Principles Are Not the End of Learning

The Six First Principles are foundational.

They are not the whole of Christian knowledge.

The language of Hebrews itself points toward progress.

The reader should therefore understand the relationship:

> **First principles are foundational, not exhaustive.**

The Doctrine of the Graphē prepares the learner to enter them.

The principles then become a foundation from which further maturity can develop.

This is why the architecture matters.

The present book is a gate into a foundation.

The foundation itself becomes part of the learner's progression toward maturity.

---

## 85.31 The Graphē and the Doctrine of Christ

Hebrews calls them:

> **“the principles of the doctrine of Christ.”**

The phrase must remain visible.

The Six First Principles are not presented as six disconnected religious subjects.

They belong to the doctrine of Christ.

The Doctrine of the Graphē therefore does not replace the doctrine of Christ.

It prepares the learner to approach its foundational principles from the authoritative written source.

This preserves both sides of the relationship:

> **Graphē remains the authority.**

> **Christ remains the subject of the foundational doctrine named in Hebrews.**

---

## 85.32 The Gate Does Not Become the Destination

This can be expressed simply:

> **A gate is important precisely because it gives access to something beyond itself.**

If the learner spends all his time arguing about the gate and never enters the school, the gate has not fulfilled its purpose.

Likewise, if the learner becomes so fascinated with questions about Scripture that he never proceeds into the foundational doctrine the Scripture teaches, he has misunderstood the function of the gate.

The Doctrine of the Graphē should produce readiness.

Readiness should produce entry.

Entry should produce labour.

Labour should produce maturity.

---

## 85.33 The Gate Also Prevents a False Entry

But the opposite problem is possible.

A learner may rush into doctrine without learning how to handle the source.

Then the curriculum can become a collection of assertions.

He may memorize conclusions without understanding their premises.

He may repeat teachers without examining Scripture.

He may quote passages without following their arguments.

He may confuse tradition with text.

He may defend a position without knowing why he holds it.

The Doctrine of the Graphē slows that rush.

It asks the learner to become responsible before becoming confident.

---

## 85.34 Confidence Must Follow Grounding

The preceding chapters repeatedly distinguished confidence from maturity.

A person may be confident and wrong.

A person may possess credentials and be unskilful.

A person may quote many verses and misunderstand them.

A person may know a theological system and still fail to see what the text establishes.

The gate therefore changes the order:

> **Grounding before confidence.**

> **Understanding before assertion.**

> **Reasoning before conclusion.**

> **Skill before responsibility to teach.**

This is the intellectual environment into which the Six First Principles should be entered.

---

## 85.35 The Reader Has Been Trained to Ask Better Questions

The most important evidence that the gate has done its work is not that the reader has memorized more terminology.

It is that the reader asks better questions.

He asks:

> What is written?

> What does it mean here?

> What does it establish?

> What does it not establish?

> What is explicit?

> What is inferred?

> What premise am I adding?

> What is the context?

> Who is speaking?

> To whom?

> What is the scope?

> What alternative interpretation exists?

> What evidence supports this conclusion?

> Where does the certainty come from?

These questions are not obstacles to faith.

They are disciplines of responsible engagement with the written Word.

---

## 85.36 The Learner Must Also Know When to Stop

A mature learner does not force certainty where the text does not provide it.

The preceding chapters have established the discipline of stopping.

Therefore, when entering the Six First Principles, the learner must be willing to say:

> **The text establishes this.**

or:

> **This is a warranted inference.**

or:

> **This is a possible interpretation.**

or:

> **The evidence does not settle the question.**

This is not weakness.

It is intellectual honesty.

The authority of Scripture does not require the interpreter to pretend that his own understanding is infallible.

---

## 85.37 The Gate Preserves the Source-Interpreter Boundary

The fundamental boundary is:

> **Graphē → interpreter.**

Not:

> **Graphē = interpreter.**

Not:

> **Interpreter = Graphē.**

Not:

> **Teacher = Graphē.**

Not:

> **Institution = Graphē.**

The interpreter remains downstream.

The authority remains upstream.

This boundary must remain intact throughout the Six First Principles.

---

## 85.38 The Learner Can Be Corrected

Because the Graphē remains above the learner, correction remains possible.

A learner may discover:

> “I misunderstood the passage.”

That is not failure of the source.

It is correction of the interpreter.

A teacher may discover:

> “My explanation was too broad.”

That is not a failure of Scripture.

It is correction of the teacher.

A textbook may require revision.

A course may require clarification.

An institution may need to correct an inherited formulation.

The written Word remains the standard against which the human work is examined.

This is one of the great protections of the gate.

---

## 85.39 The Graphē Creates Reproducibility

The learner should be able to show another learner how he reached a conclusion.

He should be able to identify the passage.

He should be able to explain the context.

He should be able to state the proposition.

He should be able to expose the inferential step.

He should be able to identify additional premises.

He should be able to distinguish text from interpretation.

This makes learning reproducible.

The learner is not merely saying:

> “God told me.”

He is able to show what the written Word says and how he reasoned from it.

The Doctrine of the Graphē therefore creates accountability in the study of the Six First Principles.

---

## 85.40 The Gate Protects the Teacher From Becoming the Source

This protection extends to the teacher.

A teacher can say:

> “Here is what the text says.”

> “Here is how I understand it.”

> “Here is the inference I am drawing.”

> “Here is where the evidence is less certain.”

Such transparency strengthens teaching.

The teacher no longer needs to hide interpretive decisions behind authoritative language.

The learner can examine the work.

The source remains visible.

---

## 85.41 The Gate Protects the Student From Becoming a Mere Follower

The purpose is also not to create permanent dependence.

A student should eventually be able to open the text and work.

He should be able to read.

Compare.

Reason.

Test.

Explain.

Correct.

Teach.

The goal is not:

> “Always believe what your teacher says.”

The goal is:

> **“Learn to handle the Word faithfully.”**

That is why Hebrews can speak of learners who ought to be teachers.

Maturity changes responsibility.

---

## 85.42 The Six Principles Require This Maturity

The first principles are foundational, but they are not trivial.

A foundational doctrine can be conceptually deep.

A short phrase can contain a large field of teaching.

The learner therefore should not equate “first” with “easy.”

“First” concerns foundational position.

The workman must still labour.

The first principles should be studied with the same discipline developed in this book:

> **Perceive → Learn → Understand → Reason → Compare → Distinguish → Infer → Test → Conclude → Apply.**

The gate prepares the learner to do exactly that.

---

## 85.43 Repentance Must Be Studied From the Word

The first principle is repentance from dead works.

The present chapter does not exhaust its meaning.

It establishes the method by which it must be studied.

The learner should ask:

> What does Hebrews mean by “repentance”?

> What are “dead works”?

> What does the surrounding argument establish?

> How does the broader biblical record illuminate the subject?

> Which statements are explicit?

> Which are inferences?

> Which definitions come from Scripture and which come from teachers?

The Doctrine of the Graphē requires those questions.

---

## 85.44 Faith Must Be Studied From the Word

The second principle is faith toward God.

Again, the gate does not provide the complete course.

It establishes the discipline.

The learner must distinguish the biblical testimony from popular definitions.

He must ask what faith means in context.

He must examine how faith is related to God.

He must distinguish faith from mere optimism, emotional intensity, or unsupported confidence.

The written Word governs the inquiry.

---

## 85.45 Baptisms Must Be Studied From the Word

The third principle is the doctrine of baptisms.

The plural should immediately signal the need for careful examination.

The learner should not assume that every occurrence of baptism refers to exactly the same object, purpose, or context.

The subject requires distinction.

That is precisely what the scholarly labour of the Graphē has trained the learner to do.

The present chapter therefore points toward the course without consuming it.

---

## 85.46 Laying on of Hands Must Be Studied From the Word

The fourth principle is laying on of hands.

The phrase names a practice, but a phrase alone does not provide every biblical meaning or occasion.

The learner must examine where the practice occurs, who performs it, upon whom it is performed, why it is performed, and what the relevant passages establish.

The method is already known.

The source remains primary.

---

## 85.47 Resurrection Must Be Studied From the Word

The fifth principle is resurrection of the dead.

The learner must not reduce the subject to a single sentence about life after death.

The biblical record must be examined.

Terms must be understood.

Passages must be compared.

Claims must be distinguished.

The scope of the doctrine must be established from the Word.

Again, the present chapter identifies the destination without pretending to have completed the journey.

---

## 85.48 Eternal Judgment Must Be Studied From the Word

The sixth principle is eternal judgment.

The learner must approach the subject with the same seriousness.

What does Scripture establish?

What distinctions does it make?

What does it explicitly say?

What is inferred?

What belongs to historical or theological interpretation?

What remains uncertain?

The gate prepares the learner to ask those questions rather than merely inherit answers.

---

## 85.49 The Six Form a Foundation

The six principles should therefore be understood as a foundation.

They are not six unrelated lessons.

They form a foundational body of teaching identified in Hebrews.

The learner is expected to progress from them toward maturity.

The Doctrine of the Graphē stands before them as the governing written-authority framework.

This gives the complete architecture:

> **Written Authority → Foundational Doctrine → Further Maturity.**

That is the movement.

---

## 85.50 The Graphē Is the Governing Written Authority

The phrase “governing written authority” is important.

It does not mean that every question can be answered by finding a verse containing the exact words of the question.

It means that the written Word remains the authoritative source against which claims concerning doctrine are examined.

The workman reasons from Scripture.

He compares Scripture.

He interprets Scripture.

He identifies what Scripture establishes.

He does not manufacture a verse for every modern question.

The authority of the source therefore does not eliminate reasoning.

It disciplines reasoning.

---

## 85.51 The Graphē and Reason

The book has deliberately refused the false choice between Scripture and reason.

The learner is not asked to stop thinking.

He is taught to think under the authority of what is written.

The sequence remains:

> **Scripture → perception → understanding → reasoning → conclusion.**

Reason does not create revelation.

Reason follows the evidence.

The written Word supplies authoritative material.

The learner's responsibility is to handle that material faithfully.

This is exactly the kind of intellectual posture needed for the Six First Principles.

---

## 85.52 The Graphē and Faith

Nor does scholarly labour destroy faith.

The learner does not become less faithful because he examines context.

He does not become less spiritual because he studies grammar.

He does not become less obedient because he distinguishes quotation from inference.

Faith does not require intellectual disorder.

The proper relationship is:

> **truth → knowledge → understanding → faithfulness → obedience.**

The learner should believe what God has established, not what confusion has made difficult to examine.

---

## 85.53 The Graphē and Obedience

The purpose of interpretation is not merely intellectual victory.

The learner is being prepared to live.

A correct interpretation that produces no obedience has not yet fulfilled its practical purpose.

But obedience must follow what has actually been established.

The learner should not use the language of obedience to justify an interpretation the text does not support.

Thus:

> **Interpretation precedes faithful application.**

The gate protects this order.

---

## 85.54 The Graphē and the Six First Principles

The relationship can now be summarized:

The Graphē establishes the authoritative written source.

> **The Six First Principles are the foundational doctrinal field into which the prepared learner now proceeds.**

Therefore:

> **The Doctrine of the Graphē is the foundation/gate, not a seventh first principle.**

---

## 85.114 From Source to Foundation

The title of this final chapter is therefore deliberate:

> **From Graphē to the Six First Principles.**

Not:

> Graphē plus six equals seven.

Not:

> Graphē replaces the six.

Not:

> Graphē becomes the first of seven.

Rather:

> **Graphē → gate → Six First Principles.**

The movement is from authoritative written source to prepared doctrinal study.

He has been taught to rightly divide.

He has been shown the responsibility of approval.

Now he must work.

Not merely talk about Scripture.

Not merely quote Scripture.

Not merely defend Scripture.

Not merely possess Scripture.

He must handle it.

## 85.120 The Final Conclusion

The Doctrine of the Graphē does not stand beside the Six First Principles as another principle.

It stands before them.

It establishes the learner's relationship to the authoritative written Word.

It teaches why the Word must be taken seriously.

It develops the intellectual discipline required to perceive, understand, reason through, compare, distinguish, infer, test, conclude, and apply what is written.

It trains the workman to remain accountable.

It prepares the learner for foundational doctrine.

And then it yields the stage.

The Six First Principles remain the Six First Principles named in Hebrews 6:1–2:

> **Repentance from Dead Works.**

> **Faith Toward God.**

> **The Doctrine of Baptisms.**

> **Laying on of Hands.**

> **Resurrection of the Dead.**

> **Eternal Judgment.**

The Doctrine of the Graphē is not the seventh.

It is the gate.

The book therefore does not end by adding another principle.

It ends by opening the way to the principles already given.

And the prepared learner now stands where Hebrews calls him to stand:

> **“Let us go on unto perfection.”**

The foundation has been established.

The workman has been prepared.

The source remains the authority.

The next work is to enter, study, exercise, and build.

> **From Graphē to the Six First Principles.**
