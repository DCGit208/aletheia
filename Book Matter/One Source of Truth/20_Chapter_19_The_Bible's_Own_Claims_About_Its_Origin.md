# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 19
# The Bible's Own Claims About Its Origin

> **Movement III — What Is the Bible?**

---

## Chapter Assignment

The preceding chapter established the Bible as the written object under investigation and began identifying its historical and literary character. Chapter 19 now asks the next necessary question:

> **What does the Bible itself claim about the origin of the writings that constitute it?**

The chapter therefore moves from identifying **what the Bible is as a collection of writings** to examining **what those writings themselves claim concerning their divine and human origin**.

This question must be handled carefully. The Bible contains claims about God speaking, prophets receiving and declaring God's word, apostles bearing witness, revelation being given, messages being written, and Scripture being described as God-breathed. These claims are related, but they are not synonymous.

The chapter therefore has a deliberately bounded objective.

It will:

1. establish the Bible's own testimony concerning divine communication;
2. distinguish divine revelation from the written record;
3. examine prophetic and apostolic mediation;
4. examine the relationship between revelation, testimony, proclamation, and writing;
5. examine the Bible's explicit claims concerning Scripture;
6. introduce the significance of **2 Timothy 3:15–17** and **2 Peter 1:20–21** without prematurely exhausting their later doctrinal treatment;
7. establish the distinction between divine Source and human instrument;
8. distinguish revelation, inspiration, writing, Scripture, and canon;
9. establish the preliminary biblical architecture necessary for the later investigation of *graphē*, inspiration, canon, and authority;
10. prepare the reader to ask what the biblical term **γραφή (*graphē*)** actually means.

---

## Central Objective

> **To gather, examine, and reason through the Bible's own claims concerning divine communication, revelation, prophetic speech, apostolic testimony, writing, Scripture, and inspiration—without prematurely collapsing these distinct categories into one another.**

---

## 19.1 The Question Before Us

The preceding chapters have progressively narrowed the investigation. We have moved from truth and knowledge to evidence and rational warrant, from God as the ground of truth to the Bible as a historical record, and from historical testimony to eyewitness and apostolic testimony.

The next question is therefore:

> **What does the Bible itself claim about the origin of what it communicates and what becomes written Scripture?**

This chapter is not yet the full investigation of *graphē*. That begins in Chapter 20. It is not yet the detailed exposition of every mode by which the written record was given; that belongs to Movement IV. Nor is it yet the complete authentication of the canon.

Its task is to establish the Bible's **own testimony concerning its origin**.

The central question is:

> **What happened, according to the biblical record, between God communicating truth and that truth becoming authoritative written testimony?**

That question requires several distinctions:

- divine communication;
- revelation;
- reception;
- prophetic speech;
- apostolic testimony;
- writing;
- Scripture;
- inspiration;
- canon.

These concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable.

---

## 19.2 The Fundamental Distinction: Communication Is Not Yet the Written Record

A divine communication is not automatically identical to a completed biblical book.

God may speak audibly without that speech itself constituting the entire written corpus.

A prophet may receive a revelation without personally witnessing the historical event revealed.

An apostle may proclaim what he has seen and heard before that testimony is written.

A writer may investigate eyewitness testimony and compose an orderly account.

A vision may be received before it is recorded.

Therefore the basic architecture is:

> **God communicates**
>
> ↓
>
> **A recipient receives or encounters the communication**
>
> ↓
>
> **The recipient speaks, witnesses, prophesies, or proclaims**
>
> ↓
>
> **The material may be written**
>
> ↓
>
> **The written material may be identified as Scripture**
>
> ↓
>
> **The authoritative corpus is recognized and preserved**

The detailed mechanisms require further investigation. But the distinctions must be established now so that later chapters do not collapse revelation, inspiration, writing, and canon into a single word.

---

## 19.3 Hebrews 1:1–2 — God Is the One Who Speaks

Hebrews begins with an extraordinarily comprehensive statement:

> “God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son...” — Hebrews 1:1–2

The ultimate subject is **God**.

God speaks.

The fathers receive.

The prophets mediate.

The communication occurs at different times and in different ways.

Then the Son appears as the climactic mode of divine speech.

The architecture is therefore:

> **One divine Source**
>
> → **different modes of communication**
>
> → **human recipients and mediators**
>
> → **a history of divine speech**
>
> → **the Son as the climactic revelation**

Hebrews is consequently a foundational text for the whole doctrine.

It prevents the false assumption that God communicates through only one mechanism. At the same time, the diversity of mechanisms does not imply a diversity of ultimate Sources.

The biblical claim is that **God speaks**.

The next question is how that speech is received, authenticated, transmitted, and written.

---

## 19.4 Divine Speech

The Bible repeatedly presents God as speaking.

Exodus 19:19 records the Sinai context in which Moses speaks and God answers by a voice. Deuteronomy 5 records the covenantal speech associated with Sinai. Matthew 3:16–17 records the heavenly voice at Jesus' baptism. John 12:28 records Jesus praying and receiving a divine response.

The immediate conclusion is not yet a complete doctrine of Scripture.

It is the more basic proposition:

> **The Bible claims that God can and does communicate through speech.**

That establishes one genuine mode of divine communication.

It does not establish that every statement ever claimed to be God's speech is authentic. The Bible itself contains warnings concerning false prophets and false testimony.

Therefore:

> **A claim to divine speech is a proposition requiring authentication.**

This distinction will remain important throughout the book.

---

## 19.5 Prophetic Speech — “Thus Saith the LORD”

The prophetic formula **“Thus saith the LORD”** is a source claim.

The prophet is not merely saying:

> “This is my religious opinion.”

The claim is:

> **This message comes from the LORD.**

That makes prophecy an epistemological claim. It identifies an alleged source of knowledge.

The reader must therefore distinguish:

1. the prophet as a human person;
2. the prophet's actual words;
3. the prophet's claimed source;
4. the evidence for the claim.

This prevents a serious error:

> **The presence of a divine formula does not automatically prove that the speaker truly speaks for God.**

The biblical record itself recognizes false prophecy. Consequently, divine-source claims must be authenticated.

---

## 19.6 Prophets as Recipients and Messengers

Jeremiah 1 illustrates a recurring biblical pattern: God commissions a prophet, communicates with him, and sends him to speak.

The basic structure is:

> **God speaks**
>
> → **prophet receives**
>
> → **prophet speaks**
>
> → **message enters history**

Ezekiel provides another example through visions, divine speech, and commands to communicate what was received.

The important point is that Scripture presents prophetic speech as **mediated divine communication**.

Yet the human messenger is not erased.

The prophet genuinely speaks.

The prophet uses human language.

The prophet operates in a historical setting.

The doctrine must therefore account for both:

> **divine source and human instrumentality.**

---

## 19.7 Habakkuk 2:2 — Revelation Becoming Writing

Habakkuk 2:2 gives an unusually clear bridge between revelation and writing:

> “Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables...”

The sequence is significant:

> **vision**
>
> → **reception**
>
> → **writing**
>
> → **communication**

The vision is not identical to the written tablet.

The written record preserves what was received.

Thus:

> **revelation and writing are related but distinguishable.**

This distinction becomes indispensable later when the book examines inspiration.

---

## 19.8 Dreams and Visions

Daniel 2, Daniel 7–8, Isaiah 6, and Ezekiel 1 demonstrate that the biblical record does not restrict divine communication to ordinary speech.

The recipient may receive:

- a dream;
- a vision;
- symbolic imagery;
- an interpretation;
- or a combination of these.

Daniel 2 is especially instructive because revelation and interpretation are both involved.

The existence of symbolic revelation introduces another epistemological question:

> **What makes the interpretation authoritative?**

The answer cannot simply be “because somebody interpreted it.”

The biblical narrative presents divine involvement in the revelation and its interpretation.

This is why the later doctrine must distinguish:

> **what God reveals**
>
> from
>
> **how a human recipient understands and records what is revealed.**

---

## 19.9 Angels as Mediators

Acts 7:38 and Hebrews 2:2 contribute to the biblical presentation of angelic mediation.

This establishes another principle:

> **Ultimate source and immediate messenger are not necessarily identical.**

A messenger can transmit a message without becoming its ultimate source.

This principle is relevant to the doctrine of Scripture because it demonstrates that mediation does not, by itself, destroy divine origin.

The detailed examination of angelic communication belongs to Chapter 24. Here it establishes the broader biblical pattern of mediated divine communication.

---

## 19.10 Christ — God Has Spoken in the Son

Hebrews 1:2 moves beyond ordinary prophetic mediation:

> **God has spoken in the Son.**

Christ is not presented merely as another prophet added to the list.

Hebrews immediately assigns Him a unique status in relation to creation and inheritance.

This means that the doctrine of Scripture cannot ultimately be separated from Christ.

Christ is both:

- the subject of extensive biblical testimony;
- and the climactic manifestation of divine revelation.

This prepares the way for the later chapters examining Christ's own treatment of the written Scriptures.

---

## 19.11 Christ, the Spirit, and Apostolic Remembrance

John 14:26 records Christ's promise concerning the Holy Spirit:

> “He shall teach you all things, and bring all things to your remembrance, whatsoever I have said unto you.”

John 16:13 likewise describes the Spirit of truth guiding the disciples into truth and declaring what is to come.

These statements are significant for the apostolic dimension of the New Testament.

They connect:

> **Christ's teaching**
>
> → **apostolic remembrance**
>
> → **the Spirit's activity**
>
> → **apostolic proclamation**

But context must control the conclusion.

These passages should not be converted into a claim that every later Christian's thought is automatically inspired.

The immediate context concerns Christ's disciples and the Spirit's role in their mission.

The narrower and warranted conclusion is:

> **The biblical record presents divine assistance in the disciples' remembrance, teaching, and proclamation of Christ's revelation.**

This becomes important when apostolic authority is examined.

---

## 19.12 Apostolic Testimony — Encounter to Proclamation

Chapter 18 established that the apostles are presented as witnesses.

Chapter 19 now follows that testimony toward its written form.

First John 1:1–3 describes what was heard, seen, observed, and touched and then connects those experiences with proclamation.

The sequence is:

> **encounter**
>
> → **testimony**
>
> → **proclamation**

This is important because the apostolic message is not presented as detached philosophical speculation.

It is testimony concerning Christ.

But apostolic testimony is not merely raw sensory description. The apostles also:

- interpret events;
- reason from Scripture;
- explain meaning;
- connect events to God's purposes;
- formulate doctrine;
- proclaim conclusions.

Therefore the biblical model does not reduce knowledge to quotation.

Witness and reasoning operate together.

---

## 19.13 Luke — Investigation and Ordered Writing

Luke 1:1–4 is one of the most important methodological passages in the entire investigation.

Luke identifies earlier accounts, refers to those who were eyewitnesses and servants of the word, and then explains that he investigated the matter carefully and wrote an orderly account so that Theophilus might know the certainty of what he had been taught.

The sequence is:

> **events**
>
> → **eyewitnesses**
>
> → **transmitted testimony**
>
> → **investigation**
>
> → **ordered writing**
>
> → **certainty**

This is decisive for the intellectual architecture of the book.

The Bible itself presents responsible investigation as compatible with the production of a written account.

Therefore:

> **Reason and Scripture are not enemies.**

Luke demonstrates that a biblical writer can investigate sources, order material, and write while the resulting account belongs within the biblical record.

The doctrine of inspiration must therefore be capable of accounting for historical research rather than requiring every biblical book to have been produced by mechanical dictation.

---

## 19.14 Received and Delivered Testimony

First Corinthians 15:3–8 supplies another important pattern.

Paul says that he **received** and **delivered** the central apostolic proclamation concerning Christ's death, burial, resurrection, and appearances.

This means the apostolic tradition has both:

- a received dimension;
- and a delivered dimension.

The structure is:

> **received**
>
> → **preserved**
>
> → **delivered**
>
> → **proclaimed**
>
> → **written**

This is an important safeguard against treating biblical origin as though every writer were independently inventing everything he records.

The biblical record allows for:

- received tradition;
- personal testimony;
- apostolic commission;
- investigation;
- revelation;
- and writing.

A comprehensive doctrine must account for all of them.

---

## 19.15 1 Thessalonians 2:13 — Human Speech and God's Word

Paul gives another significant formulation:

> “when ye received the word of God which ye heard of us, ye received it not as the word of men, but as it is in truth, the word of God...” — 1 Thessalonians 2:13

The message was heard **from human messengers**.

Yet Paul describes what was received as God's Word.

This establishes an important distinction:

> **human messenger ≠ merely human source**

The presence of human speech does not automatically reduce a message to merely human origin.

At the same time, the passage must not be generalized into:

> “Everything Paul ever said was automatically Scripture.”

The contextual claim concerns the apostolic message being received by the Thessalonians.

This is exactly why context matters.

---

## 19.16 First Corinthians 2 — Divine Things Communicated Through Words

First Corinthians 2:10–13 connects divine revelation, the Spirit, and communication.

Paul describes the Spirit as searching the deep things of God and then speaks of communicating what has been received.

This is relevant because the doctrine of inspiration concerns not merely abstract content but the communication of content.

The biblical picture includes:

> **God**
>
> → **Spirit**
>
> → **divine things**
>
> → **human recipients**
>
> → **communicated words**

This prepares the ground for a much deeper treatment of inspiration.

The question is not merely whether biblical authors had “good ideas.”

It is whether the biblical record claims that divine activity stands behind the communication itself.

---

## 19.17 Second Peter 1:20–21 — Prophecy and Divine Initiative

Second Peter 1:20–21 is central:

> prophecy did not come by the will of man;

and:

> “holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost.”

Several propositions must be kept distinct.

### The first

Prophecy is not presented as originating merely from human will.

### The second

Human beings genuinely speak.

### The third

Their speaking is associated with divine initiative.

### The fourth

The Holy Spirit is presented as active in the process.

Thus the text rejects two opposite reductions.

It rejects:

> **“Prophecy is simply human religious invention.”**

But it also does not require:

> **“The human prophet is an irrelevant recording machine.”**

Men **spoke**.

The Spirit was active.

The doctrine must preserve both facts.

The Greek participle **φερόμενοι (*pheromenoi*)**, associated with being borne or carried, contributes to the description of this divine activity. Lexical evidence illuminates the term, but the doctrine must be derived from the whole sentence and context, not from etymology alone.

---

## 19.18 Second Timothy 3:15–17 — Scripture Is God-Breathed

We now reach the most explicit New Testament statement concerning Scripture's divine character.

Paul tells Timothy that he has known the **Holy Scriptures**, which are able to make him wise unto salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.

Then:

> **“All scripture is given by inspiration of God...”**

The Greek text contains:

- **πᾶσα (*pasa*)** — all, every, the whole;
- **γραφή (*graphē*)** — writing, Scripture, Holy Writ;
- **θεόπνευστος (*theopneustos*)** — God-breathed.

The lexical data confirms that **γραφή** can mean a writing and, in biblical usage, Scripture/Holy Writ, while **θεόπνευστος** describes the Scripture as God-breathed. 

This is not merely a statement that Scripture is useful.

Its usefulness follows from what it is.

It is profitable for:

- teaching;
- reproof;
- correction;
- training in righteousness.

The passage concludes that the person of God may be complete and equipped for every good work.

Thus the biblical claim concerns both:

> **origin**

and

> **function.**

---

## 19.19 “All Scripture” — A Boundary We Must Not Weaken

The phrase **πᾶσα γραφή (*pasa graphē*)** deserves special attention.

Whatever questions remain concerning the precise historical scope of the term “Scripture” in Paul's immediate setting, the text cannot responsibly be weakened into:

- “some Scripture”;
- “only the doctrinal parts”;
- “only the passages directly spoken by God”;
- or “only selected inspired sections.”

The statement concerns Scripture as a category.

The exact scope and canonical implications will be treated later.

Chapter 19 therefore establishes the claim.

Chapter 31 will examine the scope of **“all Scripture”** in detail.

This is an example of disciplined knowledge flow: the argument is introduced when its premise becomes necessary, and exhausted only when the TOC assigns it its proper place.

---

## 19.20 *Theopneustos* Is Not Merely “Inspirational”

The English word “inspired” can be misunderstood because modern English may use it to describe emotional excitement or creative stimulation.

That is not the force of Paul's claim.

The term **θεόπνευστος (*theopneustos*)** is associated with God and breathing and describes Scripture as God-breathed.

What does theopneustos tell us?

In 2 Timothy 3:16, Paul explicitly characterizes γραφή (graphē) as θεόπνευστος (theopneustos)—“God-breathed.”

That establishes an important proposition:
Scripture has a uniquely stated relationship to God as its divine Source.
We should not weaken that.

Therefore:

> **The biblical doctrine of inspiration concerns Scripture's relationship to God, not merely the psychological experience of its human authors.**

This is why saying:

> “The writers were inspired because they felt inspired”

is inadequate.

The claim concerns the Scripture itself.


### How Does Divine Agency Relate to Human Authorship?


The Bible contains writings produced through genuinely human activity:

- Moses writes;
- David writes;
- prophets speak and write;
- Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John write;
- Paul writes letters;
- Luke investigates sources and composes an orderly account;
- John records what he saw and heard.

These are not incidental features of the biblical record. They are part of its evidential character.

The biblical writings come to us through human beings who lived in history, encountered events, received testimony, witnessed persons and circumstances, investigated what had occurred, remembered what they had experienced, proclaimed what they believed to be true, and committed their testimony to writing.

This makes the biblical claims historically situated and therefore, in principle, historically investigable.

The Christian claim is not that God had to bypass humanity in order to communicate truth. The biblical record presents the opposite pattern. God repeatedly communicates through human persons and within human history. The supreme expression of this pattern is the Incarnation itself:

> **“And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us”** (John 1:14).

The Word entered history in the person of Jesus Christ. Those who encountered Him could therefore hear, see, observe, touch, remember, testify, and proclaim what they had encountered (1 John 1:1–3).

The same historical character is evident in the written testimony. Luke explicitly identifies eyewitnesses and describes his careful investigation of the available testimony before producing his orderly account (Luke 1:1–4). John presents his testimony as arising from what he had seen and heard (1 John 1:1–3; John 19:35; 21:24). Paul appeals to witnesses of Christ's resurrection and identifies himself as one who encountered the risen Christ (1 Corinthians 15:3–8).

The human authorship of Scripture therefore should not be treated merely as an obstacle that a doctrine of inspiration must overcome.

**It is also a ground of historical accountability and evidential investigation.**

Because God communicated through persons situated in history, the resulting claims can be examined against:

- the persons involved;
- the places described;
- the events reported;
- the chronology asserted;
- the customs and institutions mentioned;
- the testimony of witnesses;
- the relationship between independent accounts;
- and the external historical and archaeological record.

This is especially significant where multiple witnesses and authors testify concerning the same historical realities. Their testimony can be compared rather than simply accepted in isolation. Where accounts converge on central events while retaining the characteristics expected of distinct witnesses and authors, that convergence becomes relevant evidence to be assessed.

The question is therefore not whether the presence of human authors weakens divine revelation.

Rather, the biblical model gives us something remarkable:

> **A divine claim communicated within history through human witnesses, human authors, and human language, thereby making the claim accessible to historical examination.**

The biblical record simultaneously attributes Scripture to God.

Paul declares:

> **“All scripture is given by inspiration of God”** (2 Timothy 3:16).

Peter declares that prophecy did not originate merely in human will, but that holy men of God spoke as they were moved by the Holy Spirit (2 Peter 1:20–21).

Paul likewise says that the Thessalonians received the apostolic message not merely as the word of men, but as **“the word of God”** (1 Thessalonians 2:13).

The resulting biblical picture is therefore not:

> **God or human authors.**

It is:

> **God communicating through human authors and witnesses within history.**

That combination is not a reason to suspend judgment. It is one of the reasons the biblical claim can be investigated.

The divine Source explains the claim to revelation.

The human witnesses and authors provide historical mediation.

The written record preserves the testimony.

And the convergence of testimony, where independently established, provides evidence that can be examined.

Thus the human dimension of Scripture is not merely something that must be reconciled with its divine character. **It is part of the very historical form through which the Bible presents its claim to divine revelation.**

---

## 19.21 Revelation, Inspiration, Writing, and Canon Are Distinct

The book must now establish these four distinctions firmly.

### Revelation

What God makes known.

### Inspiration

The divine character or operation associated with Scripture's production.

### Writing

The historical act of committing material to written form.

### Canon

The authoritative corpus of writings recognized as Scripture.

These are related but not synonymous.

A person may receive revelation without producing a canonical book.

A person may witness an event without becoming an inspired biblical author.

A written work may claim authority without that claim automatically establishing canon.

Therefore:

> **revelation ≠ inspiration ≠ writing ≠ canon.**

Their relationship must be demonstrated, not assumed.

---

## 19.22 Divine Source and Human Instrument

A recurring biblical pattern is that God communicates through human persons.

The human writer is real.

The human language is real.

The historical setting is real.

The literary activity is real.

Yet the biblical record can still attribute divine origin or authority to the resulting Scripture.

This gives us a crucial principle:

> **Ultimate source is not identical to immediate instrument.**

A messenger can genuinely speak without becoming the ultimate source of the message.

The analogy must not be pushed too far because ordinary human messengers can introduce error. The biblical doctrine of inspiration makes a stronger claim concerning Scripture.

That stronger claim is why inspiration must be investigated carefully rather than reduced to ordinary communication.

---

## 19.23 The Bible's Language Is More Nuanced Than Mechanical Dictation

The evidence now includes:

- direct divine speech;
- prophetic mediation;
- angelic mediation;
- dreams;
- visions;
- Christ's words;
- apostolic testimony;
- received tradition;
- historical investigation;
- Spirit-guided communication;
- writing;
- Scripture described as God-breathed.

These are not all the same process.

Therefore the book must not begin with the assumption that the Bible was produced through one mechanical method.

Some passages involve direct divine speech being recorded.

Other passages involve eyewitness testimony.

Luke explicitly investigates earlier testimony.

Paul transmits what he received.

Prophets speak from God.

John records what he saw and heard.

The biblical evidence therefore requires a doctrine capable of explaining **divine agency together with genuine human agency**.

---

## 19.24 The Internal Claim and the External Case

An important anti-circularity guardrail is necessary.

The Bible says Scripture is God-breathed.

That is an internal claim.

But if we ask:

> **Why should we believe the biblical writings are truly divine Scripture?**

we cannot simply answer:

> “Because the Bible says so.”

The proper investigation is cumulative.

The internal claim is examined alongside:

- historical testimony;
- eyewitness testimony;
- apostolic testimony;
- prophetic claims;
- Christ's treatment of Scripture;
- historical correspondence;
- manuscript evidence;
- transmission;
- canonical recognition;
- fulfilled prophecy;
- and other relevant evidence.

Thus:

> **Biblical self-testimony is evidence within the cumulative case, not a substitute for authentication.**

This preserves both biblical seriousness and intellectual honesty.

---

## 19.25 Source, Message, Messenger, Recipient, Record

The investigation can now distinguish six categories:

### Source

Who ultimately originates the truth?

### Message

What is communicated?

### Messenger

Through whom is it communicated?

### Recipient

Who receives it?

### Witness

Who sees, hears, experiences, or encounters it?

### Record

How is it preserved in writing?

Then comes the next layer:

### Scripture

Which written material is identified as authoritative Holy Writ?

And later:

### Canon

Which writings constitute the recognized authoritative corpus?

These questions must be answered in order.

## 19.27 The Ninefold Field of Divine Communication

The later Movement IV must give full treatment to the following nine areas:

1. **Audible voice of God** — Exodus 19:19; Deuteronomy 5; Matthew 3:16–17; John 12:28.
2. **Angels** — Acts 7:38; Hebrews 2:2.
3. **Prophets** — Acts 3:21; Hebrews 1:1.
4. **Jesus Christ** — Hebrews 1:1–2; Revelation 1.
5. **Apostles** — Acts 1:2; Ephesians 4.
6. **Visions** — Isaiah 6; Daniel 7–8; Ezekiel 1.
7. **Dreams** — Daniel 2; Matthew 1:20; Matthew 2:12.
8. **Revelation** — including the biblical category *apokalypsis*.
9. **Inspiration** — 2 Timothy 3:15–17.

These are not nine competing explanations for the Bible.

They are nine areas within the broader biblical field of divine communication and the production of written testimony.

Their detailed treatment belongs to Chapters 23–32.

## 19.28 Revelation as Unveiling

The Greek term **ἀποκάλυψις (*apokalypsis*, G602)** belongs to the semantic field of disclosure or unveiling.

It is used in contexts involving revelation, manifestation, future events, judgment, glory, and truths previously hidden.

The important conceptual distinction is:

> **Revelation concerns what God makes known.**

But a lexical definition is not itself a complete doctrine.

The meaning of *apokalypsis* in a particular passage must be determined by context.

This illustrates a general methodological rule:

> **Lexical data supplies semantic possibilities; context determines the sense; broader biblical reasoning establishes doctrine.**

The book must repeatedly maintain this discipline.

---

## 19.29 What the Bible Claims—and What It Does Not Yet Prove

The evidence now warrants several conclusions.

### It does establish:

- The Bible presents God as the ultimate Source of divine communication.
- God communicates through multiple means.
- Prophets function as mediators of divine communication.
- Apostles are commissioned witnesses.
- Christ is the climactic revelation of God.
- The Holy Spirit is presented as active in divine communication.
- Some divine communication becomes written.
- Scripture is explicitly described as God-breathed.
- Human agency is real within the biblical production of testimony.

### It does not yet, by itself, complete:

- the lexical study of *graphē*;
- the full doctrine of inspiration;
- the exact scope of “all Scripture”;
- the canon;
- the historical authentication of every writing;
- the complete argument for infallibility.

Those conclusions belong later.

This is not an omission.

It is disciplined organization.

---

## 19.30 Why the Next Question Is γραφή

We have repeatedly encountered the term “Scripture.”

But what is Scripture?

What is the Greek word?

What did the word originally mean?

How does an ordinary writing become Holy Writ?

How is *graphē* used throughout the New Testament?

Why can Jesus speak of “the scripture” as an authoritative written category?

Why can Paul say:

> “All scripture is given by inspiration of God”?

The answer cannot simply be:

> “Scripture means the Bible.”

That would leave the fundamental category undefined.

Therefore the next chapter must begin with the word itself:

> **γραφή — *graphē* — G1124.**

Only after its lexical range and biblical usage are established can the book properly explain:

> **writing → Holy Writ → Scripture → authoritative written Word.**

# Lexical Notes

## γραφή — *graphē* — G1124

Basic lexical field: **writing, written thing**; in biblical usage, Scripture/Holy Writ.

It must not be defined merely as “the Bible.” Its basic lexical field concerns writing, while context establishes the specific referent.

In 2 Timothy 3:16, *graphē* is the noun described as *theopneustos*.

## θεόπνευστος — *theopneustos* — G2315

A term describing Scripture as **God-breathed**.

The lexical observation matters, but the doctrine must be established from the sentence, context, and wider biblical evidence rather than from etymology alone.

## ἀποκάλυψις — *apokalypsis* — G602

A term associated with disclosure, unveiling, revelation, and manifestation. Its precise sense must be determined contextually.

## φερόμενοι — *pheromenoi*

The participle in 2 Peter 1:21 associated with being borne/carried. It contributes to Peter's description of divine activity in prophetic speech, but lexical data alone does not settle the entire doctrine of inspiration.

# Chapter Summary

The chapter's central conclusion:
The Bible presents its written testimony as originating from God while being communicated, witnessed, transmitted, and written through genuine human agents in history. This divine-human character is foundational to understanding both the Bible's claim to Scripture and the evidential form in which that claim comes to us.
