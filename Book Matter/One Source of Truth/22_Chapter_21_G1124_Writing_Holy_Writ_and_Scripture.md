# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 21 — G1124 — Writing, Holy Writ, and Scripture

> **Movement III — What Is the Bible?**

---

## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 20 established the lexical and biblical significance of **γραφή (*graphē*)**, Strong's G1124. It established that the word belongs fundamentally to the field of writing and what is written, while biblical usage gives it the distinctive sense of Scripture or Holy Writ.

Chapter 21 now develops the next necessary question:

> **How does a writing come to be designated Holy Writ, and how does biblical usage identify authoritative written material as Scripture?**

The chapter therefore moves from the lexical identity of *graphē* to the biblical category of **holy writings and authoritative Scripture**.

The purpose is not to collapse every written document into Scripture, nor to begin the later investigation of canon before its proper place.

The purpose is to establish the biblical distinction between:

- writing in general;
- holy writings;
- Scripture;
- particular Scriptural passages;
- and the authoritative written testimony recognized in the biblical record.

---

## Central Objective

> **To establish from the biblical record and relevant lexical evidence why Scripture is distinguished from writing in general, how the category of Holy Writ is expressed, and how authoritative written material is identified as Scripture.**

---

# 21.1 Writing Is a Broad Category

Chapter 20 established that *graphē* belongs to the field of writing and what is written.

That immediately gives us an essential distinction:

> **Not everything that is written is Scripture.**

The Bible itself contains many kinds of writing.

There are:

- historical records;
- genealogies;
- laws;
- songs;
- letters;
- prophetic writings;
- personal communications;
- royal records;
- and other forms of written material.

Therefore the existence of writing does not by itself establish the existence of Scripture.

The question is more precise:

> **What distinguishes the writings that are Holy Writ?**

That question is necessary because the biblical category of Scripture is narrower than the generic category of written material.

---

# 21.2 Holy Writings — ἱερὰ γράμματα

Paul gives us an important starting point in 2 Timothy 3:15:

> **“And that from a child thou hast known the holy scriptures...”**

The Greek expression is:

> **ἱερὰ γράμματα**  
> *hiera grammata*

The noun **γράμματα (*grammata*)** belongs to the field of letters, writings, or written material.

The adjective **ἱερά (*hiera*)** identifies something as holy or sacred.

Paul therefore describes the writings Timothy has known as:

> **holy writings.**

This is already more precise than the generic category of writing.

The text does not merely say:

> “you have known writings.”

It identifies a particular body of writings as **holy**.

---

# 21.3 The Holy Writings Were Known

Paul says Timothy had known these holy writings:

> **“from a child.”**

The statement places the sacred writings within an existing historical and educational context.

Timothy had encountered them before Paul's letter.

They were not being created by Paul's statement.

They were an already existing body of sacred writings known to Timothy.

Paul then identifies their function:

> **“which are able to make thee wise unto salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus.”**

The writings therefore possess a specific theological function.

They communicate knowledge capable of making Timothy wise unto salvation.

The significance is substantial:

> **Holy Writ is not merely writing distinguished by age or religious interest; it is sacred written testimony possessing a defined divine purpose.**

---

# 21.4 Holy Writ and Scripture

Paul immediately continues:

> **“All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable...”**

The Greek text uses:

> **πᾶσα γραφή**  
> *pasa graphē*

The relationship between verses 15 and 16 is therefore central.

Verse 15:

> **ἱερὰ γράμματα — holy writings**

Verse 16:

> **πᾶσα γραφή — all Scripture**

The terms are not identical.

That distinction must be preserved.

Yet they occur in immediate literary connection.

Paul has just described Timothy's sacred writings and then speaks of *graphē* in the statement concerning Scripture.

The passage therefore gives us an important biblical bridge between:

> **holy writings**

and:

> **Scripture.**

The detailed doctrine of **θεόπνευστος (*theopneustos*)**, and the scope of “all Scripture,” will be developed in their designated later chapters.

Here the relevant conclusion is:

> **The biblical record distinguishes sacred written material and identifies Scripture as a specific category of written testimony.**

---

# 21.5 Sacred Status Is Not Created by Human Preference

The biblical category of Holy Writ is not presented as a matter of personal preference.

A writing does not become Scripture because:

- someone likes it;
- a community finds it inspiring;
- a ruler approves it;
- it becomes popular;
- or generations happen to preserve it.

The biblical record presents Scripture as possessing a status grounded in its relationship to God's revelation.

This is why the question of **origin** remains important.

Chapter 19 examined the Bible's own claims concerning divine communication and origin.

Chapter 21 now sees the consequence in the written category:

> **A written testimony can be treated as Holy Writ because of what it is and the authority it bears, not merely because it is a surviving ancient document.**

---

# 21.6 Scripture as Authoritative Written Testimony

The biblical treatment of Scripture demonstrates its authority repeatedly.

Jesus asks:

> **“Have ye not read in the scriptures?”**

(Matthew 21:42).

He says:

> **“Ye do err, not knowing the scriptures”**

(Matthew 22:29).

He tells His hearers:

> **“Search the scriptures”**

(John 5:39).

The apostles reason from:

> **“the scriptures”**

(Acts 17:2).

The Bereans search:

> **“the scriptures daily”**

(Acts 17:11).

These passages reveal an established category.

Scripture is not treated as ordinary literature whose relevance must first be invented by the reader.

It is already recognized as authoritative written testimony.

---

# 21.7 Scripture as a Standard for Examination

Acts 17:11 is especially important.

The Bereans receive Paul's message with readiness of mind, but they do not stop with hearing.

They examine the Scriptures:

> **“whether those things were so.”**

This establishes a significant relationship between:

> **apostolic proclamation**

and:

> **written Scripture.**

The written Scripture functions as a standard against which the proclamation can be examined.

This is not hostility to revelation.

It is the biblical model of disciplined examination.

The sequence is:

> **hearing → examining → comparing → determining.**

The existence of an authoritative written standard makes such examination possible.

---

# 21.8 Jesus' Use of Scripture

Jesus repeatedly appeals to Scripture in His own teaching.

During His temptation, He answers Satan with:

> **“It is written”**

(Matthew 4:4, 7, 10).

In Matthew 21:42, He appeals to Psalm 118.

In Matthew 22:31–32, He appeals to the Scriptures concerning the resurrection.

In John 10:34–36, He appeals to Psalm 82:6 and explicitly states:

> **“the scripture cannot be broken.”**

In Luke 24:27, He explains what was written concerning Himself from Moses and all the Prophets.

In Luke 24:44–46, He connects His death and resurrection with what was written in:

- the Law of Moses;
- the Prophets;
- and the Psalms.

The pattern is consistent.

Scripture functions as authoritative written testimony.

---

# 21.9 “The Scripture Cannot Be Broken”

John 10:34–36 deserves particular attention.

Jesus cites Psalm 82:6:

> **“I said, Ye are gods.”**

His argument depends upon the written wording.

He then says:

> **“the scripture cannot be broken.”**

The statement occurs within an argument, not as an isolated slogan.

Jesus is appealing to a written text whose authority constrains the argument being made against Him.

The conclusion relevant here is:

> **Jesus treats the Scriptural text as authoritative and binding within theological reasoning.**

The full implications of Scriptural authority belong to later development.

But the evidence establishes that Scripture is not being treated as ordinary writing.

---

# 21.10 Scripture and Fulfillment

Another defining feature of Scripture is its relationship to fulfillment.

The Gospel writers repeatedly introduce events with language such as:

> **“that the scripture might be fulfilled.”**

This is particularly prominent in the accounts of Christ's ministry, suffering, death, and resurrection.

The written Scripture therefore functions as a prior testimony against which later historical events can be understood.

The pattern is:

> **written testimony → historical occurrence → claimed fulfillment.**

The validity of any individual fulfillment claim requires examination of:

- the original passage;
- its literary context;
- its historical setting;
- the chronology;
- the nature of the prediction;
- the subsequent event;
- and the correspondence between them.

But the category itself is clear.

Scripture is treated as written testimony that can bear authoritative witness concerning God's purposes in history.

---

# 21.11 Prophetic Writing and Scripture

The New Testament explicitly connects Scripture with the prophets.

Romans 16:26 speaks of:

> **“the scriptures of the prophets.”**

Matthew 26:56 similarly refers to:

> **“the scriptures of the prophets.”**

This establishes an important relationship.

A prophet may receive and speak a divine message.

That message may subsequently be written.

The written prophetic testimony can then become part of the Scriptural record.

Therefore:

> **Prophecy and Scripture are related categories, but they are not identical categories.**

Prophecy concerns prophetic communication.

Scripture concerns the authoritative written testimony.

This distinction will become essential in Movement IV, where the various ways God gave the written record are examined.

---

# 21.12 Scripture Can Preserve Earlier Revelation

The relationship between prophecy and Scripture reveals something broader.

The Bible preserves communications that originated earlier in history.

God speaks.

Prophets proclaim.

Events occur.

Writings preserve the testimony.

Later generations read those writings as Scripture.

Thus Scripture serves as a bridge across generations.

The original speaker may no longer be alive.

The original audience may have disappeared.

The political circumstances may have changed completely.

Yet the written testimony remains available.

This is one of the defining evidential characteristics of the written record.

---

# 21.13 Scripture as a Continuing Witness

A spoken testimony can be heard by those present.

A written testimony can continue to testify after the original speaker has died.

This is why Scripture repeatedly remains present as a witness to later generations.

Jesus can appeal to Moses.

The apostles can appeal to the Prophets.

Later Christians can read the same writings.

The historical distance does not erase the existence of the written testimony.

The writing preserves the witness.

Therefore:

> **The written Scripture functions as a continuing historical and theological witness across generations.**

This helps explain why the biblical writers repeatedly appeal to things that were:

> **“written.”**

---

# 21.14 The Authority of the Written Word

The Bible's treatment of Scripture demonstrates several dimensions of authority.

Scripture can:

- command;
- testify;
- correct;
- instruct;
- establish an argument;
- expose error;
- announce promises;
- record prophecy;
- bear witness to Christ;
- and provide a standard for examination.

The authority is therefore not merely ceremonial.

Scripture actually performs authoritative work in the biblical argument.

It is used to establish what is true, expose what is false, and interpret historical events.

---

# 21.15 Scripture and Christ

The relationship between Christ and Scripture is especially important.

Jesus does not present Himself as abolishing the Scriptures.

He says:

> **“Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets”**

(Matthew 5:17).

He repeatedly interprets His own mission through the written Scriptures.

After His resurrection, He teaches His disciples concerning:

> **“the things concerning himself.”**

Luke 24:27 describes Him beginning with Moses and all the Prophets and expounding the things concerning Himself in all the Scriptures.

The relationship is therefore profound:

> **Christ is not presented as an alternative to the authority of Scripture; He confirms and fulfills the Scriptural testimony concerning Himself.**

This becomes particularly important when the book later examines Christ as the decisive manifestation and communication of God.

---

# 21.16 Scripture and the Apostolic Witness

The apostles likewise appeal to Scripture as the written framework for their proclamation.

Paul summarizes the Gospel in 1 Corinthians 15:3–4:

> **“Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures”**

and:

> **“he rose again the third day according to the scriptures.”**

The apostolic proclamation is therefore not presented as detached from the written testimony that preceded it.

The apostles proclaim historical events and interpret those events in relation to Scripture.

The structure is:

> **historical event + apostolic testimony + Scriptural correspondence.**

This connects the historical and evidential dimensions developed in Chapters 17 and 18 with the written category being examined here.

---

# 21.17 The Apostolic Writings and “the Other Scriptures”

A particularly important text is 2 Peter 3:15–16.

Peter refers to Paul's letters and then says that unstable people distort them:

> **“as they do also the other scriptures.”**

The phrase:

> **τὰς λοιπὰς γραφάς**

means:

> **“the other Scriptures.”**

The passage therefore places Paul's writings in a relationship with an already recognized Scriptural category.

This is significant internal evidence.

It does not by itself give the entire history of the New Testament canon.

But it does establish that, within the New Testament itself, apostolic writings could be spoken of in relation to:

> **the other Scriptures.**

That is an important development in the biblical evidence for the Scriptural category.

---

# 21.18 What 2 Peter 3:16 Establishes

The argument must remain precise.

Peter establishes that:

1. Paul wrote letters.
2. Some things in those letters are difficult to understand.
3. Unstable people distort those writings.
4. They do so in the same manner in which they distort:
   > **“the other scriptures.”**

The comparison is significant.

It means that Paul's writings are not being treated merely as private correspondence with no Scriptural standing.

At minimum, the passage provides strong internal evidence that apostolic writings were being placed within the recognized category of Scripture.

The complete question of the canonical corpus remains a later historical investigation.

But the textual evidence here belongs to the present argument.

---

# 21.19 Scripture Is Not Created by the Canonical List

Another distinction is necessary.

A writing does not become Scripture merely because a later list contains its name.

The historical recognition of a writing and the underlying identity of the writing are related but distinct questions.

The Bible's own language concerns writings already functioning as sacred and authoritative testimony.

The later historical question is:

> **How were the writings belonging to that category identified, received, preserved, and recognized?**

That is the question of canon.

Therefore:

> **Scripture must first be understood as a category before the historical process of recognizing the canonical corpus can be fully examined.**

---

# 21.20 Holy Writ Is Not the Same as Religious Literature

The category of Holy Writ is more precise than:

> **religious literature.**

Religious literature can include:

- theological reflections;
- sermons;
- devotional works;
- historical accounts;
- philosophical arguments;
- traditions;
- commentaries;
- confessions;
- and countless other writings.

These may discuss God, Scripture, faith, morality, or religion.

But they are not thereby Scripture.

The distinction is therefore essential:

> **Religious writing is a broad human category. Holy Writ is a specific Scriptural category.**

This is precisely why the Bible's own terminology must be examined rather than allowing modern categories to determine the argument.

---

# 21.21 Scripture and Human Authorship

The conclusion established in Chapter 19 remains fully operative.

Scripture comes to us through genuine human authorship.

Moses wrote.

The prophets wrote.

The psalmists wrote.

The Gospel writers wrote.

Paul wrote letters.

Peter wrote.

John wrote.

Yet some of these writings are identified as Scripture.

Therefore:

> **Human authorship does not prevent a writing from being Holy Writ.**

The biblical record does not require us to choose between:

> **divine origin**

and:

> **human authorship.**

The written Scripture comes through human literary activity while being presented as sacred and authoritative.

That is the historical form in which the written Word comes to humanity.

---

# 21.22 Scripture and Historical Particularity

Because the writings come through human authors, they possess historical particularity.

A writing has:

- a language;
- a historical setting;
- a literary form;
- an intended audience;
- a communicative purpose;
- and a historical context.

Understanding those features does not diminish Scriptural authority.

It enables accurate reading.

A prophetic oracle must be understood in its historical setting.

A Gospel narrative must be read according to its narrative and historical context.

An apostolic letter must be understood in relation to its recipients and circumstances.

Thus:

> **Historical context is not an enemy of Scripture; it is part of the evidence required to understand the Scripture correctly.**

---

# 21.23 Scripture as Written Testimony About Reality

The Bible's written Scriptures do not merely preserve theological abstractions.

They record claims concerning:

- persons;
- places;
- nations;
- kings;
- wars;
- covenants;
- births;
- deaths;
- journeys;
- judgments;
- promises;
- miracles;
- and historical events.

They therefore make claims that intersect with reality.

This is important because the book's investigation began with truth and reality.

If Scripture claims:

> **“this happened,”**

then the claim is capable of historical examination.

If Scripture claims:

> **“this was spoken,”**

the claim can be examined in relation to its historical and textual setting.

If Scripture claims:

> **“this was fulfilled,”**

the claimed correspondence can be examined.

Thus Holy Writ remains an evidentially accessible written record.

---

# 21.24 The Written Record Is Not Beyond Examination

The authority of Scripture does not make examination meaningless.

The Bereans examined.

The apostles reasoned.

Jesus argued from Scripture.

The biblical writers interpreted earlier written testimony.

Therefore the proper response to Scripture includes:

- reading;
- examination;
- comparison;
- interpretation;
- reasoning;
- and obedience.

This is consistent with the intellectual architecture established from Chapter 1 onward.

Truth is not established by repetition.

Knowledge is not reduced to quotation.

Scripture is not reduced to isolated proof texts.

And authority is not a substitute for understanding.

The Bible itself models serious engagement with the written Word.

---

# 21.25 The Distinction Between Scripture and Interpretation

A final methodological distinction is essential.

There is:

> **what Scripture says**

and:

> **what a person says Scripture means.**

Those are not automatically identical.

The biblical text is the primary object.

A commentary is a secondary witness.

A theological system is an interpretive construction.

A sermon is an exposition.

A study Bible contains editorial judgments.

A lexicon provides lexical information.

The manuscript must therefore preserve the distinction between:

> **Scripture**

and:

> **human commentary about Scripture.**

This principle is fundamental to the Doctrine of the Graphē.

---

# 21.26 The Cumulative Biblical Picture

The evidence can now be assembled.

### First

Writing is a broad category.

### Second

The Bible identifies a category of **holy writings**.

### Third

The New Testament uses **γραφή** as a designation for Scripture.

### Fourth

Jesus and the apostles treat Scripture as authoritative.

### Fifth

Scripture is used as a standard for examining claims.

### Sixth

Scripture bears witness to God's purposes and historical fulfillment.

### Seventh

Prophetic writings can function as Scripture.

### Eighth

The New Testament provides evidence that apostolic writings could be included within the Scriptural category.

The conclusion is therefore not based on one isolated verse.

It arises from the cumulative pattern.

This chapter has established:

# 21.29 What This Chapter Has Not Yet Attempted

This chapter has not attempted to:

Those questions have their proper places.

The present chapter has established the category itself:

> **Holy Writ is sacred and authoritative written testimony, and γραφή is a principal biblical designation for Scripture.**

# Lexical and Evidence Notes

## γραφή (*graphē*) — G1124

The lexical field of **γραφή** concerns writing or what is written. In New Testament usage, the term is repeatedly used for Scripture or Holy Writ and can also designate a particular Scriptural passage.

The lexical meaning must not be expanded into a claim that every occurrence of γραφή means “the Bible.” Context determines the scope.

## γράμματα (*grammata*)

In 2 Timothy 3:15, Paul speaks of:

> **ἱερὰ γράμματα**

holy writings.

The expression is significant because it provides a direct biblical description of sacred written material immediately before the statement concerning:

> **πᾶσα γραφή**

all/every Scripture.

## 2 Peter 3:16

Peter's description of Paul's letters alongside:

> **“the other scriptures”**

provides important internal evidence concerning apostolic writings and the Scriptural category.

The passage should be used for what it actually establishes, without making it carry the entire later history of canonical recognition.
