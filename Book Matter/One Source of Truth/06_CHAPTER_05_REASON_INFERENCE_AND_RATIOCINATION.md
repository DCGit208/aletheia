# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT I — TRUTH AND THE POSSIBILITY OF KNOWLEDGE

# Chapter 5 — Reason, Inference, and Ratiocination

> **Central question:** How does the mind move from evidence and established premises to a conclusion, and what makes that movement rationally valid?

The previous chapter established that evidence does not become a conclusion by itself. Evidence must be apprehended, examined, connected to propositions, and interpreted through reasoning. A conclusion is warranted only when the grounds offered for it adequately support what is being concluded.

The present chapter therefore examines the intellectual movement itself:

> **How do we reason from what is known to what is concluded?**

This is the place for a crucial distinction:

> **An assertion tells us what someone claims. An inference tells us what is concluded from something else.**

The difference matters everywhere, but it becomes decisive when Scripture is studied. A learner may quote an accurate text and still draw a conclusion that the text does not establish. Conversely, a learner may draw a necessary conclusion from propositions that Scripture has already established without merely repeating the same words.

The purpose of reasoning is therefore not to place human intellect above Scripture.

It is to teach the intellect to **work faithfully with what is true**.

---

# 1. Reason Begins With Something to Be Reasoned About

Reason does not manufacture reality.

It does not create facts.

It does not turn a false proposition into a true one.

Reason operates upon information, propositions, evidence, relationships, and premises.

If the premises are true and the reasoning is valid, the conclusion can follow from them.

This is why the previous chapters had to establish truth and evidence before beginning an extended treatment of reasoning.

The sequence is cumulative:

> **Reality → truth → evidence → premises → reasoning → inference → conclusion**

If the first stages are neglected, the later stages become unstable.

A brilliant argument cannot make false premises true.

A valid inference cannot rescue inaccurate information.

And a correct quotation cannot guarantee a correct conclusion.

The investigator must therefore examine both:

1. **the truth of the premises**, and
2. **the validity of the movement from premises to conclusion.**

---

# 2. What Is an Assertion?

An assertion is a proposition put forward as true.

For example:

> “The event occurred.”

That sentence asserts something.

So does:

> “God is faithful.”

So does:

> “This writing is authoritative.”

But the fact that a proposition has been asserted does not establish its truth.

The investigator must ask:

> **What grounds the assertion?**

This is precisely where assertion differs from inference.

An assertion may stand alone rhetorically.

An inference is presented as following from other propositions.

For example:

> Premise 1: God cannot lie.

> Premise 2: God has spoken this proposition.

> Conclusion: The proposition God has spoken is true.

The conclusion is not merely another isolated assertion. It is offered as following from the premises.

The quality of the conclusion therefore depends upon:

- whether the premises are true;
- whether the terms are being used consistently;
- whether the inference is valid;
- and whether the conclusion says no more than the premises establish.

---

# 3. What Is an Inference?

An inference is the intellectual movement by which one proposition is accepted as following from other propositions.

The simplest structure is:

> **Premise → Premise → Conclusion**

For example:

1. All human beings are mortal.
2. Socrates is a human being.
3. Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

The conclusion follows because the premises establish the relationship required for the conclusion.

The important word is **therefore**.

It marks the inferential movement.

Without the inferential relationship, there is merely a collection of statements.

This is why serious reasoning must expose the steps between evidence and conclusion.

A reader should not be forced to accept:

> “Therefore X.”

without being shown:

> **Why does X follow?**

That is one of the central standards of this book.

---

# 4. What Is Ratiocination?

**Ratiocination** is deliberate reasoning: the process of working through propositions and their relationships in order to arrive at conclusions.

It involves more than spontaneous thought.

It requires the mind to:

- identify premises;
- distinguish terms;
- compare propositions;
- recognize relationships;
- detect implications;
- test consistency;
- follow consequences;
- and judge conclusions.

Ratiocination is therefore disciplined intellectual labour.

It is not intellectual ornament.

It is what happens when the mind refuses to leap from:

> “I have read this”

to:

> “Therefore I know what follows.”

Instead, it asks:

> **What exactly has been established, and what follows from it?**

This distinction becomes especially important when handling a large, interconnected body of writing.

---

# 5. Deduction: When the Conclusion Necessarily Follows

One of the strongest forms of inference is deduction.

A deductive argument is structured so that, if the premises are true and the reasoning is valid, the conclusion necessarily follows.

For example:

1. All things created have a creator.
2. This object is created.
3. Therefore, this object has a creator.

The logical form determines whether the conclusion follows.

Another familiar structure is:

1. If A, then B.
2. A.
3. Therefore B.

The investigator must distinguish the **form of an argument** from the truth of its premises.

A formally valid argument can still have a false premise.

Therefore:

> **Validity concerns whether the conclusion follows from the premises.**

> **Truth concerns whether the propositions themselves correspond to reality.**

Both questions matter.

---

# 6. Inductive Reasoning: From Observed Cases Toward a General Conclusion

Not every legitimate inference is deductive.

Inductive reasoning moves from observed instances or evidence toward a broader conclusion.

For example:

> Numerous independently examined samples of a particular substance display a common property.

The investigator may infer that the property is characteristic of that substance under specified conditions.

The conclusion is not necessarily established in exactly the same way as a deductive conclusion.

It is supported by the evidence.

Therefore the investigator must ask:

- How many cases were examined?
- Were they representative?
- Were the observations independent?
- Are there counterexamples?
- Were the conditions controlled?
- Is the generalization stronger than the observations permit?

Induction therefore requires careful proportionality.

> **Do not infer a universal conclusion from evidence that establishes only a limited pattern.**

This principle will matter when evaluating historical and empirical evidence later in the book.

---

# 7. Abductive Reasoning: The Best Explanation of the Evidence

A third form of reasoning is explanatory inference.

When several facts are known, the investigator may ask:

> **Which explanation best accounts for the evidence?**

For example, if multiple independent pieces of evidence converge upon one historical explanation, that explanation may possess greater explanatory power than alternatives requiring numerous unsupported assumptions.

This does not mean:

> “Whatever seems most satisfying is true.”

It means the investigator compares explanations according to their ability to account for the evidence.

Relevant questions include:

- Does the explanation account for the major facts?
- Does it fit independently established evidence?
- How many unsupported assumptions does it require?
- Does it explain evidence that competing hypotheses leave unexplained?
- Does it generate contradictions?
- Does it require ad hoc additions whenever contrary evidence appears?

Abductive reasoning is therefore especially relevant to cumulative historical investigation.

It will later help organize questions concerning the Bible's historical record, testimony, preservation, prophecy, and authentication.

---

# 8. A Conclusion Cannot Be Stronger Than Its Premises

This is one of the most important rules of rational inference.

Suppose the evidence establishes:

> “A document existed by the first century.”

That does not automatically establish:

> “Every statement in the document is true.”

Nor does it automatically establish:

> “The document is divinely inspired.”

Nor does it automatically establish:

> “Every interpretation of the document is correct.”

Each of those is a larger proposition.

Each requires its own grounds.

Thus:

> **A conclusion must not outrun the evidence or premises from which it is derived.**

This principle protects the investigation from overstatement.

It also protects the biblical record from being burdened with conclusions that a particular passage does not actually establish.

---

# 9. The Difference Between Implication and Entailment

A proposition can be relevant to another proposition without logically entailing it.

For example:

> “The witness was present.”

may support:

> “The witness could have observed the event.”

But it does not automatically entail:

> “Everything the witness says about the event is accurate.”

Additional premises concerning perception, memory, honesty, competence, and reporting may be required.

Similarly:

> “This writing contains fulfilled predictions.”

may be relevant to:

> “This writing has an extraordinary claim to divine origin.”

But the inferential bridge must be examined.

The investigator must ask:

> **What additional premises connect the evidence to the conclusion?**

This question exposes hidden assumptions.

A powerful argument is not one that hides its assumptions.

It is one that makes them visible and defensible.

---

# 10. Necessary and Contingent Conclusions

Another important distinction concerns the kind of conclusion being reached.

Some conclusions are **necessary** given their premises.

Others are **contingent** upon the evidence and circumstances.

If:

1. God cannot lie.
2. God has spoken proposition P.

then, if both premises have been established and the terms are understood correctly, the truth of P follows necessarily from the character of God.

By contrast, the conclusion:

> “This manuscript is the earliest surviving copy”

depends upon historical evidence about surviving manuscripts and therefore remains a contingent historical conclusion subject to the evidence.

The investigator should therefore not use the same language of certainty for every kind of claim.

Precision in the strength of a conclusion is part of intellectual honesty.

---

# 11. Hidden Premises

Many weak arguments appear strong because a necessary premise has been concealed.

Consider:

> “Jesus quoted Scripture; therefore every text we call Scripture is necessarily identical in every respect to the form we possess today.”

The conclusion requires additional premises.

The investigator must expose them.

Likewise:

> “The Bible says God is true; therefore the Bible is God's Word.”

This conclusion reverses an argument that has not yet been established.

It assumes what it is trying to prove.

The correct question is:

> **What premises establish that the biblical record is genuinely God's revelation?**

That question belongs to the later authentication argument.

The lesson here is methodological:

> **Whenever a conclusion appears suddenly, identify the premises that must connect it to the evidence.**

---

# 12. Circular Reasoning

Circular reasoning occurs when the conclusion is smuggled into the premises.

For example:

> “The Bible is infallible because the Bible says it is infallible.”

The statement may accurately report a biblical claim.

But as a complete authentication argument, it is circular because the authority of the source has already been assumed.

The proper investigation must instead distinguish:

### What the Bible claims

from:

### What establishes the credibility of that claim.

This does not mean that the Bible's internal testimony is irrelevant.

It means its evidential role must be correctly identified.

The investigation can ask:

- What does the writing claim?
- What historical evidence bears upon the claim?
- What evidence concerns its witnesses?
- What evidence concerns its preservation?
- What evidence concerns fulfilled prophecy?
- What evidence concerns divine manifestations?
- What evidence concerns Christ and the apostolic witness?
- How do these lines of evidence converge?

This produces a cumulative argument rather than a circular assertion.

---

# 13. Equivocation: When a Word Changes Meaning Mid-Argument

An argument can appear valid because one term silently changes meaning.

For example, suppose someone says:

> “Scripture is authoritative.”

Then:

> “Therefore my interpretation of Scripture is authoritative.”

The word **authoritative** has changed its object.

The authority of the text does not automatically transfer infallibility to the interpreter.

Likewise:

> “The Word is truth.”

does not mean:

> “Everything I say about the Word is truth.”

The distinction between:

- **God's Word**,
- **the written record**,
- **our understanding of the record**,
- and **our statements about the record**

must remain intact.

This distinction will become central in the later treatment of interpretation.

---

# 14. False Alternatives

Reasoning can also fail by creating a false choice.

For example:

> “Either you trust Scripture or you trust reason.”

This is a false alternative within the architecture of this book.

The question is not whether reason should replace Scripture.

The question is whether the human mind can use reason **in engaging what Scripture says**.

Likewise:

> “Either a proposition is mathematically demonstrable or it cannot be known.”

That is another false alternative.

Historical knowledge, testimonial knowledge, logical knowledge, empirical knowledge, and revealed knowledge do not all possess exactly the same evidential form.

The appropriate question is:

> **What kind of claim is being made, and what kind of evidence and reasoning are appropriate to that claim?**

---

# 15. The Principle of Non-Contradiction

Reasoning requires distinctions between compatible and incompatible propositions.

A proposition cannot simultaneously be true and false in the same respect at the same time.

For example:

> “The object is entirely inside the room.”

and:

> “The object is entirely outside the room.”

cannot both be true in the same respect at the same time.

This principle is not an arbitrary rule imposed upon Scripture.

It is a condition of meaningful assertion itself.

If a statement could be both true and false in precisely the same sense, then affirming and denying it would cease to distinguish anything.

The principle therefore matters when evaluating alleged contradictions in Scripture.

But its proper use requires precision.

A contradiction must actually be demonstrated.

Two statements that appear different are not necessarily contradictory.

This connects directly to the distinction established in Chapter 3.

---

# 16. Context Is Part of Reasoning

A proposition cannot always be interpreted independently of its context.

Context may include:

- surrounding sentences;
- paragraph structure;
- speaker;
- audience;
- historical setting;
- grammatical construction;
- literary form;
- temporal sequence;
- and related statements.

Removing a proposition from its context can change the apparent meaning.

This is not unique to Scripture.

It is a general principle of language.

If someone quotes:

> “I said, Ye are gods,”

the investigator should not stop at the isolated sentence.

The surrounding argument in John 10:34–36 must be examined.

Likewise, if a writer says:

> “Let us reason together,”

the surrounding passage must be examined before a theological or philosophical conclusion is drawn from the phrase.

Reason therefore requires more than extracting words.

It requires reconstructing the proposition actually being communicated.

---

# 17. Comparison Is an Instrument of Reason

Comparison allows the investigator to determine relationships among propositions.

When two passages appear related, compare:

- terminology;
- subject;
- context;
- argument;
- chronology;
- audience;
- and conclusion.

Comparison can reveal:

- agreement;
- qualification;
- contrast;
- development;
- repetition;
- analogy;
- or actual contradiction.

This is especially important for a large corpus of writings.

A single statement may be illuminated by another.

But comparison must not become arbitrary cross-referencing.

The question remains:

> **What legitimate relationship exists between the passages?**

A citation does not create a relationship merely because two verses contain a similar word.

Reason must establish the connection.

---

# 18. From Premise to Consequence

One of the most powerful forms of reasoning is to follow a proposition to its consequences.

Suppose someone claims:

> “Truth is whatever an institution declares.”

Then ask:

- What happens when two institutions contradict one another?
- Does the larger institution automatically win?
- What if the institution changes its position?
- Was the former position true before the change?
- What makes the institution's present declaration true?

The consequences expose the weakness of the premise.

Likewise, if someone claims:

> “Every interpretation is equally valid because Scripture is difficult.”

then ask:

- Can contradictory interpretations both be correct?
- If yes, in what sense?
- If no, how is the correct interpretation distinguished?
- What governs the distinction?

Reasoning therefore does not merely ask whether a claim sounds attractive.

It follows the claim through its implications.

---

# 19. Reasoning Through Scripture Is Not Reasoning Against Scripture

This distinction must be kept explicit.

To reason **against** Scripture would mean treating human reasoning as an independent authority capable of overruling a proposition that has already been established as true revelation.

That is not the objective here.

To reason **through Scripture** means:

> **to understand what Scripture says, identify its propositions, examine their relationships, follow the arguments it presents, distinguish what is asserted from what is reported, and draw conclusions that genuinely follow from the written record.**

This is a fundamentally different activity.

The intellect is not being established as the final authority over the Word.

It is being disciplined under the Word.

That is why the book's later treatment of **dianoetic thinking**, Isaiah 1:18, sound judgment, and scholarly labour must follow this chapter rather than precede it.

---

# 20. The Bible Itself Contains Reasoned Argument

The biblical writings do not consist solely of isolated propositions.

They contain arguments.

They ask questions.

They draw conclusions.

They compare cases.

They appeal to precedent.

They reason from premises.

They expose contradictions.

They infer consequences.

They establish claims through testimony.

They appeal to written records.

They reason from what God has previously said and done.

This means that a reader who simply collects individual sentences without following the arguments can miss what the writing is actually doing.

A passage may contain its own inferential structure.

The reader's task is to discover it accurately.

Therefore:

> **Reasoning is not an alien method imposed upon Scripture. It is necessary for understanding reasoning that is already present in Scripture.**

---

# 21. Jesus' Reasoning Must Be Examined, Not Merely Quoted

This becomes particularly important when the book reaches the doctrine of the Graphē.

In John 10:34–36, Jesus does not merely mention Psalm 82:6.

He reasons from the written text.

He says:

> **“Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?”**

and then:

> **“If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came, and the scripture cannot be broken; Say ye of him, whom the Father hath sanctified, and sent into the world, Thou blasphemest; because I said, I am the Son of God?”**

The point for the present chapter is methodological.

Jesus' argument has premises, a textual citation, an inferential relationship, and a conclusion.

Therefore the reader must learn to ask:

> What did Jesus identify as written?

> What proposition did He derive from it?

> What relationship did He establish between the written statement and His opponents' accusation?

> Why did the argument work?

Those questions belong to rational reading.

Later, when the book examines why **“the scripture cannot be broken,”** the question will not be treated as an isolated slogan.

Its force will be examined within Jesus' argument and within the prior establishment of what Scripture is.

---

# 22. Scripture and the Discipline of the “Therefore”

A mature reader should learn to identify the places where Scripture moves from one proposition to another.

Words such as:

- therefore;
- for;
- because;
- if;
- then;
- wherefore;
- so;
- that;
- lest;
- but;
- and;
- neither;
- rather;

can mark logical or argumentative relationships.

The presence of an inferential word does not automatically make the argument valid, but it alerts the reader that a relationship must be examined.

The learner should therefore ask:

> **What is the premise?**

> **What is the conclusion?**

> **What connects them?**

> **Is the relationship necessary, probable, analogical, explanatory, conditional, or something else?**

This is how the reader moves from reading words to understanding thought.

---

# 23. Ratiocination and the Danger of Doctrinal Drift

The significance of this discipline extends beyond abstract logic.

If a learner repeatedly makes conclusions that exceed the text, those conclusions can accumulate into doctrine.

The process may look like:

> **Text → partial reading → hidden assumption → inference → repeated conclusion → tradition → institutional doctrine**

At the end of the process, the final doctrine may appear ancient and authoritative even though the inferential steps that produced it have never been examined.

This is one reason the later chapters will investigate:

- unskillfulness;
- dull hearing;
- sound judgment;
- doctrinal turning aside;
- aridity;
- accepted heresies;
- and the need for diligent labour in the Word.

The solution is not hostility toward doctrine.

The solution is disciplined derivation.

> **Show how the doctrine follows.**

---

# 24. The Difference Between Understanding and Inventing

There is a critical boundary between discovering an implication in a text and inventing an implication that the text does not contain.

A legitimate inference must be traceable.

The reader should be able to say:

> “This conclusion follows because these propositions are established, and this relationship connects them.”

An invented conclusion often has a different structure:

> “The text mentions A. I associate A with B. B reminds me of C. Therefore the text teaches C.”

That is association, not necessarily inference.

The fact that two ideas can be connected in one's mind does not establish that the text connects them.

This is why:

> **Association is not entailment.**

A disciplined interpreter must distinguish what the text says, what it implies, what it permits, and what the reader merely imagines.

---

# 25. Reason Requires Intellectual Honesty

Good reasoning requires willingness to abandon a conclusion when its grounds fail.

The objective is not to win an argument.

The objective is to discover what is true.

Therefore the investigator should be prepared to say:

> “This conclusion does not follow.”

or:

> “The evidence is insufficient.”

or:

> “The premise has not been established.”

or:

> “I interpreted the statement too broadly.”

or:

> “This apparent contradiction disappears when the propositions are properly distinguished.”

That is not intellectual weakness.

It is intellectual discipline.

The same discipline must apply to conclusions favorable to one's existing convictions.

A person who protects a preferred conclusion from examination is not reasoning faithfully.

---

# 26. The Master Formula Applied to Reasoning

The governing manuscript formula can now be made operational:

> **Claim → Definition → Evidence → Analysis → Inference → Conclusion → Consequence**

### Claim

What exactly is being asserted?

### Definition

What do the important terms mean?

### Evidence

What establishes or bears upon the claim?

### Analysis

What does the evidence actually show?

### Inference

What logical movement connects the grounds to the conclusion?

### Conclusion

What follows?

### Consequence

What changes if the conclusion is true?

This formula prevents several recurring failures:

- unsupported assertion;
- hidden premises;
- circular reasoning;
- equivocation;
- overstatement;
- quotation without analysis;
- evidence without inference;
- inference without evidence.

It will remain a governing discipline throughout the remainder of the book.

---

# 27. The Transition to Dianoetic Thinking

Reasoning establishes the mechanics of inference.

But human reasoning is not merely a mechanical sequence of premises.

A serious investigation also requires the ability to:

- perceive distinctions;
- analyze wholes into parts;
- reconnect parts to wholes;
- compare propositions;
- recognize relationships;
- identify relevant evidence;
- detect contradictions;
- follow implications;
- and judge competing conclusions.

That broader intellectual discipline is the subject of the next chapter.

It is what the book will call **dianoetic thinking and sound judgment**.

The present chapter therefore establishes the foundation:

> **Reasoning explains how conclusions follow.**

The next chapter asks:

> **What kind of disciplined thinking is required to see, organize, compare, and judge those relationships correctly?**

---

# Chapter Conclusion

Truth requires more than assertion.

Knowledge requires more than possession of information.

Evidence requires more than relevance.

And rational warrant requires more than confidence.

The investigator must know how conclusions are formed.

That requires the discipline of reasoning.

> **An assertion states a proposition.**

> **Evidence bears upon a proposition.**

> **A premise provides a ground.**

> **Inference connects grounds to conclusions.**

> **Ratiocination deliberately works through those relationships.**

> **A rational conclusion must not outrun its premises.**

The implications for Scripture are profound.

The written Word is not honoured merely by quoting it.

It is honoured by **understanding what is written, preserving the distinctions it makes, following the arguments it presents, examining the relationships among its propositions, and refusing to attribute to it conclusions that cannot be derived from it**.

This is not reason replacing Scripture.

It is reason being exercised in the service of understanding Scripture.

And that brings the investigation to the next necessary question:

> **What kind of disciplined intellectual activity enables a person to analyze, compare, connect, distinguish, infer, and judge correctly?**

That is the subject of Chapter 6:

# Dianoetic Thinking and Sound Judgment

# Chapter Reference Notes

## Core distinctions established

| Distinction | Principle |
|---|---|
| Assertion / inference | An assertion states a proposition; an inference derives a conclusion from grounds. |
| Truth / validity | Truth concerns propositions; validity concerns whether a conclusion follows from premises. |
| Evidence / conclusion | Evidence bears upon a claim; it does not become a conclusion automatically. |
| Deduction / induction | Deduction can yield necessary conclusions from true premises; induction generalizes from observed evidence. |
| Abduction / deduction | Explanatory inference compares which account best explains the evidence; it is not identical to deduction. |
| Implication / entailment | Relevance or suggestiveness does not by itself establish logical entailment. |
| Necessary / contingent | Some conclusions follow necessarily from established premises; historical conclusions are often contingent upon evidence. |
| Text / interpretation | The authority of the text does not transfer infallibility to the interpreter. |
| Scripture / statement about Scripture | God's Word must not be conflated with every human claim made about it. |
| Association / inference | Mental association does not establish textual entailment. |
| Reason against Scripture / reasoning through Scripture | Human reason is not established as a rival authority; it is disciplined to understand and work through the written record. |

## Reasoning audit

For a major argument, ask:

1. What is the claim?
2. What do its terms mean?
3. What are the premises?
4. Which premises are established?
5. What evidence establishes them?
6. Is any premise hidden?
7. What inference connects the premises?
8. Is the inference valid?
9. Is the conclusion necessary, probable, or merely possible?
10. Does the conclusion exceed the evidence?
11. Are alternative explanations being ignored?
12. Has any term changed meaning during the argument?
13. Has a quotation been mistaken for the conclusion?
14. Has association been mistaken for entailment?
15. What consequences follow?

## Biblical reasoning discipline

When examining a biblical argument:

- identify the speaker;
- identify the audience;
- identify the proposition;
- identify the cited text;
- read the surrounding context;
- identify the premises;
- identify the conclusion;
- identify the inferential connection;
- distinguish what is asserted from what is reported;
- distinguish what the passage establishes from what the interpreter adds.

## Authoring Guardrails

> **Do not merely tell the reader that an argument is logical. Show the premises, the inferential movement, and why the conclusion follows.**

> **Do not use “reason” as a vague synonym for personal opinion. Reason is disciplined movement from grounds toward conclusions.**

> **Do not make reason a rival authority over Scripture. Teach the reader to reason through Scripture.**

> **Do not make Scripture a collection of disconnected proof texts. Follow its arguments.**

> **Do not infer more than the evidence establishes.**

> **Do not confuse a possible explanation with an established explanation.**

> **Do not confuse association with inference or relevance with entailment.**

> **Do not use the authority of Scripture to smuggle the interpreter's conclusion into the text.**

> **Do not use the existence of difficult passages as permission to abandon reasoning. Difficulties require better analysis.**

> **Do not protect a conclusion merely because it is familiar, traditional, institutional, or personally preferred.**
