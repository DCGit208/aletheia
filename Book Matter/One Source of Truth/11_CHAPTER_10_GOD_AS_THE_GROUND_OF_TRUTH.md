# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT II — GOD, TRUTH, AND REVELATION

# Chapter 10 — God as the Ground of Truth

> **Central question:** If truth requires an ultimate ground, in what sense is God that ground?

Chapter 9 brought the investigation to the question of ultimate grounding.

That question cannot be answered merely by attaching the word **God** to the conclusion.

If one says:

> **“God is the ground of truth,”**

the statement contains several distinct propositions that must be examined:

1. that God exists;
2. that God is ultimate rather than one contingent reality among others;
3. that God is the Creator and source of contingent reality;
4. that God is rationally adequate as the ground of reality's intelligibility;
5. that truth is not external to God as though God merely possesses it;
6. that God's nature is intrinsically truthful;
7. that God's Word is therefore trustworthy;
8. and, eventually, that the Bible can be authenticated as a written revelation proceeding from that God.

The present chapter concentrates on the first major transition:

> **from the problem of an ultimate ground to the biblical conception of God as the ultimate ground of truth and reality.**

It is important not to confuse this with the later question of biblical authentication.

The argument must remain ordered.

> **God's existence and nature → divine truthfulness → revelation → authentication of the written record.**

The Bible will eventually become the principal written authority under investigation. But at this stage, the argument must not simply use the Bible as an unexamined premise to establish the very authority for which the Bible will later be examined.

At the same time, neither should the biblical record be treated as though it were merely one opinion among competing religious texts.

The investigation must distinguish **the existence of a claim**, **the evidence for the claim**, and **the authority of the source once authenticated**.

---

# 1. What Would It Mean for God to Be the Ground of Truth?

To call God the ground of truth is stronger than saying:

> “God knows what is true.”

It is also stronger than saying:

> “God usually tells the truth.”

The first describes knowledge.

The second describes reliable communication.

Neither by itself explains the ultimate status of truth.

The stronger proposition is that objective truth is grounded in the reality and nature of God.

That means truth is not an arbitrary rule imposed by God from outside Himself.

Nor is God subject to a higher truth existing independently above Him.

Both alternatives create difficulties.

If truth exists independently of God as a standard above Him, then God is not ultimate with respect to truth.

If God can make contradictory propositions true simply by declaring them true, then truth becomes arbitrary.

The biblical conception points in another direction:

> **God is true.**

This is not merely a statement that God possesses correct information.

It concerns His nature.

---

# 2. God Is Not Being Introduced as a Mere Hypothesis

The word **God** can be used in many different ways.

A philosopher may use it to designate a necessary being.

A theologian may use it within a doctrinal system.

A commentator may describe divine attributes.

A culture may use the word according to inherited tradition.

None of these uses, by themselves, establishes what God actually is.

The present investigation therefore has to distinguish:

> **God Himself**

from

> **human descriptions of God.**

This distinction is particularly important because the question is not:

> “What have theologians said about God?”

The question is:

> **What is the reality to which the term God refers, and what grounds warrant the claims being made about Him?**

That is why the investigation must not be controlled by theological vocabulary alone.

Words such as *omnipotent*, *omniscient*, *immutable*, *transcendent*, *personal*, and *necessary* can be useful.

But definitions are not demonstrations.

A proposition concerning God must still be established.

---

# 3. From Contingent Reality to an Ultimate Ground

The previous chapter distinguished contingent reality from what is necessary.

A contingent reality is one that does not contain within itself a sufficient explanation of why it exists rather than not existing.

The ordinary world presents us with contingent realities everywhere.

Things come into existence.

Things cease to exist.

Things depend upon conditions outside themselves.

A tree depends upon prior causes and sustaining conditions.

A human being depends upon parents, biological processes, environmental conditions, and countless other realities.

Planets, stars, organisms, institutions, and civilizations likewise exhibit dependence.

If every explanation terminates only in another contingent reality, the question remains:

> **Why does the entire dependent order exist at all?**

An ultimate explanation cannot simply be another member of the same dependent order.

If it were, the explanatory question would remain.

This is the force of the classical move toward a necessary ground.

It is not yet a complete demonstration of every divine attribute.

It is a demand for an adequate ultimate explanation.

---

# 4. Why “Necessary” Matters

A necessary reality is not merely a thing that happens to exist for a very long time.

It is a reality whose nonexistence is not a genuine alternative in the relevant sense.

This distinction matters because infinite duration does not by itself equal necessity.

Something could conceivably exist eternally while still requiring an explanation for why it exists.

The issue is not merely:

> **How long has it existed?**

The issue is:

> **Why does it exist at all?**

An ultimate ground must therefore possess a mode of existence adequate to terminate the explanatory dependence of contingent reality.

This is why the argument cannot stop with:

> “The universe exists.”

The existence of the universe is precisely what requires examination.

---

# 5. The Biblical Record Does Not Present God as a Dependent Being

The biblical conception of God is radically different from the conception of God as merely one powerful entity inside the universe.

The opening declaration of Scripture is:

> **“In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.”**  
> — Genesis 1:1

The claim is not that God is one object among the objects He created.

God is presented as the Creator.

The distinction is fundamental:

> **Creator and creation are not equivalent categories.**

If God created all things, then God cannot be merely one contingent component of the created order.

John's Gospel develops this claim concerning the Word:

> **“In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.”**  
> — John 1:1

And:

> **“All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made.”**  
> — John 1:3

The argument is striking.

Everything that was made belongs to the category of things made.

The Word is presented as the agent through whom all things were made.

Therefore the biblical presentation does not place God or the Word within the class of created things.

This is not merely a statement about God's power.

It concerns the very structure of reality.

---

# 6. Why Creation Matters to the Truth Question

The connection between creation and truth is easy to miss.

If God is the Creator of all contingent reality, then reality itself is not an unexplained collection of independent facts.

It has an ultimate source.

And if reality proceeds from an intelligent Creator, the intelligibility of reality is no longer inexplicable in the same way.

The world can be investigated rationally because it is not being treated as the ultimate source of its own intelligibility.

This does not mean every human explanation is correct.

It means that the rational structure of reality is compatible with its being grounded in an intelligent source.

The biblical statement:

> **“In the beginning God created...”**

therefore has implications far beyond cosmology.

It establishes a relationship between:

> **God → creation → reality → intelligibility → knowledge.**

This relationship becomes important when we later ask how finite human beings can know truth about the world and, more importantly, how they can know what God has revealed.

---

# 7. The Word and the Nature of Reality

John 1:1–3 deserves particular attention because of the connection between **the Word** and **creation**.

The text does not present the Word as merely a collection of written sentences.

The Word is presented personally and eternally in relation to God, and John declares:

> **“the Word was God.”**

Then:

> **“All things were made by him.”**

This gives the investigation a profound conceptual connection.

The One through whom all things were made is not detached from truth as though truth were an arbitrary human category imposed upon an otherwise unintelligible universe.

The biblical conception connects divine reality, divine Word, and created reality.

That does not yet establish every doctrine concerning Scripture.

It does, however, establish why the relationship between **God, Word, and truth** cannot be treated superficially.

---

# 8. “I Am the Truth”

The connection becomes even more explicit in the words of Jesus Christ:

> **“I am the way, the truth, and the life.”**  
> — John 14:6

Notice the form of the declaration.

Christ does not merely say:

> “I know the truth.”

Nor:

> “I teach some truths.”

He says:

> **“I am ... the truth.”**

This is a claim about identity, not merely information.

If Christ is truth, then truth is not presented merely as an abstract proposition existing independently of divine reality.

The claim points toward truth as inseparable from the nature and identity of God revealed in Christ.

That is why Christology cannot be postponed indefinitely.

The investigation of the ground of truth eventually encounters the person of Christ.

---

# 9. Is God Merely a Being Who Tells the Truth?

No—that formulation is inadequate to the biblical conception.

A human being can tell the truth while still being capable of falsehood.

A human witness may accurately report one event and misunderstand another.

Human truthfulness is therefore contingent and fallible.

The biblical presentation of God is different.

The relevant question is not:

> “Has God ever happened to tell the truth?”

It is:

> **Is falsehood compatible with God's nature?**

Hebrews 6:17–18 is therefore central:

> **“Wherein God, willing more abundantly to shew unto the heirs of promise the immutability of his counsel, confirmed it by an oath:  
> That by two immutable things, in which it was impossible for God to lie, we might have a strong consolation...”**

The phrase **“it was impossible for God to lie”** is not merely a statement that God has a good record.

It places falsehood in opposition to God's nature.

That proposition will be developed more fully in Chapter 12.

For the present chapter, its importance is this:

> **If God is the ultimate ground of reality and falsehood is impossible to Him, then truthfulness is not an accidental feature of divine communication.**

It belongs to the identity of the One from whom revelation would proceed.

---

# 10. God as the Unchanging Source

James 1:17 adds another dimension:

> **“Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning.”**

The text presents God as without the variability associated with changing human conditions.

This matters because an ultimate ground must not itself be merely unstable.

If truth were grounded in a being whose nature continually changed, the investigation would again face the question of what ultimately explains the standard.

The biblical description instead presents God as constant.

That constancy is not merely emotional reliability.

It concerns the character of the One from whom reality and communication proceed.

---

# 11. “Thou Art True”

The biblical record also contains direct recognition of God's truthfulness.

In Mark 12:14, those addressing Jesus say:

> **“Master, we know that thou art true, and carest for no man...”**

The statement is significant because **true** is being attributed to Christ.

It is not merely saying that He knows facts.

It concerns His character and reliability.

This aligns with John 14:6:

> **“I am the way, the truth, and the life.”**

The cumulative picture therefore becomes stronger:

> **God is true.**

> **Christ identifies Himself with truth.**

> **The Word is God.**

> **The Word is the agent of creation.**

> **God does not change.**

> **It is impossible for God to lie.**

These propositions must eventually be brought together carefully.

---

# 12. Truth Is Not Something God Must Discover

There is a crucial philosophical consequence.

If God is the ultimate ground of reality, God does not stand outside reality as a finite investigator attempting to discover whether His own existence corresponds to something else.

Nor does God require a higher authority to tell Him what is true.

If a higher standard existed above God and determined whether God were true, that higher standard would occupy a more ultimate position with respect to truth.

The biblical conception therefore directs the argument toward:

> **truth grounded in God Himself.**

This does not mean that God arbitrarily creates logical contradictions and then calls them true.

Rather, the biblical conception presents God as true and faithful in His own being.

Truth is therefore not an external measuring stick by which God is reluctantly judged.

---

# 13. Is This Circular Reasoning?

A serious objection must be addressed.

Someone may say:

> “You are using the Bible to establish that God is truthful, and then you will use God's truthfulness to establish the Bible.”

If that were the whole argument, it would indeed be circular.

But that is not the intended structure.

The investigation distinguishes two levels.

### Level One — Ground

What rational and evidential considerations establish concerning God, reality, causation, necessity, intelligibility, and divine attributes.

### Level Two — Revelation

Once the existence and nature of God have been sufficiently established, what evidence establishes that a particular claimed revelation actually proceeds from that God?

### Level Three — Written record

Once revelation is established as genuinely divine, what establishes that the particular writings under examination accurately preserve and communicate that revelation?

### Level Four — Authority and interpretation

Once the written record has been authenticated, what does its authority require of the reader, and how should it be interpreted?

This ordering prevents the argument from simply assuming the conclusion.

---

# 14. Does Reason Replace Revelation?

No.

This distinction is essential.

Reason can investigate whether a proposition is coherent.

Reason can compare evidence.

Reason can evaluate testimony.

Reason can infer causes from effects.

Reason can distinguish necessary from contingent explanations.

Reason can test whether a conclusion follows from premises.

But reason cannot manufacture a historical event it did not witness.

Nor can reason simply deduce every particular fact God may choose to reveal.

For example, human reason can ask whether it is possible for God to communicate.

It cannot, merely by abstract reasoning, determine everything God has actually communicated at a particular historical moment.

That requires revelation and evidence concerning the revelation.

Thus:

> **Reason is not the replacement for revelation.**

Rather:

> **Reason is necessary for evaluating the claim that revelation has occurred and for understanding what that revelation communicates.**

This is precisely why the book refuses the false choice between reason and Scripture.

---

# 15. Natural Knowledge and Revealed Knowledge Must Be Distinguished

This distinction also helps prevent confusion concerning the existence of God.

Human beings encounter the created world.

They observe order.

They encounter causation.

They experience moral obligation.

They reason about existence.

They encounter consciousness, rationality, contingency, and intelligibility.

Such considerations can generate arguments concerning God's existence and attributes.

But these arguments are not identical with revelation.

A person may reason from creation toward a Creator.

That does not mean the person has thereby learned every revealed truth concerning God's purposes, covenant, redemption, Christ, or Scripture.

Conversely, revelation does not make rational evaluation irrelevant.

The two domains are related:

> **Reason can investigate reality and assess revelation.**

> **Revelation can disclose realities that human investigation could not independently discover.**

This distinction will become indispensable when the book examines how the Bible was given.

---

# 16. What About the Classical Arguments for God?

The classical arguments remain relevant, but they must be properly classified.

The cosmological argument asks about the ultimate cause or explanation of contingent reality.

The teleological argument examines order, purposiveness, and apparent design.

The moral argument examines objective moral obligation, moral consciousness, and moral order.

These arguments can contribute to a cumulative case.

But none should be made to carry conclusions beyond what its premises establish.

For example:

> **An argument from contingency is not, by itself, a complete demonstration of biblical inspiration.**

Nor is:

> **An argument from design, by itself, a demonstration of the biblical canon.**

The proper role of such reasoning is to investigate the existence and attributes of an ultimate reality.

The question of revelation comes afterward.

This is precisely why the book can engage philosophical arguments without becoming a philosophical apologetics manual.

---

# 17. Why the Biblical Account Still Matters Decisively

There is, however, another danger.

Once philosophical reasoning is introduced, one may begin treating philosophy as though it were a higher authority than the biblical record.

That would reverse the intended architecture.

Philosophical arguments can investigate propositions concerning God.

They cannot replace what God has revealed.

If God has actually spoken, the content of that revelation is not to be reconstructed from human speculation as though revelation were unnecessary.

The crucial distinction is:

> **Arguments can lead us toward conclusions concerning God's existence and attributes; revelation tells us what God has actually revealed.**

Therefore the philosophical investigation is preparatory.

The biblical record becomes decisive once the question changes from:

> **“What can human reason establish concerning God?”**

to:

> **“What has God actually revealed?”**

---

# 18. Why God Is the Proper Candidate for the Ultimate Ground

The cumulative argument can now be stated.

An adequate ultimate ground of truth and reality must be:

- not merely contingent;
- sufficient to account for contingent existence;
- capable of grounding intelligibility;
- adequate to the rational order encountered in reality;
- not dependent upon a higher human authority;
- and, if it is to ground trustworthy revelation, intrinsically truthful.

The biblical conception of God answers these requirements by presenting God as:

> **Creator**

> **ultimate**

> **unchanging**

> **true**

> **faithful**

> **incapable of falsehood**

and as the One through whom all things were made.

The argument is therefore not:

> “The Bible says God is the ground of truth; therefore God is the ground of truth.”

It is:

> **The problem of objective truth and contingent reality requires an adequate ultimate ground; the conception of God presented in the biblical record identifies such a ground; the philosophical and evidential case for God's existence and attributes must then be examined alongside the biblical record; and, once the divine nature is established, the question of divine revelation can be investigated without circularity.**

That is the architecture of the argument.

---

# 19. The Difference Between Grounding Truth and Knowing Truth

Another distinction must remain explicit.

Suppose God is objectively the ground of truth.

That does not mean every human being automatically knows every truth.

The **ground of truth** and the **means of knowing truth** are different questions.

God may ground reality.

Human beings still require appropriate means of knowledge.

Those means include:

- perception;
- memory;
- testimony;
- rational inference;
- historical investigation;
- and, where God communicates what could not otherwise be known, revelation.

Therefore:

> **God's role as the ground of truth is ontological.**

> **Human investigation of truth is epistemological.**

> **Evidence concerns the warrant by which the investigator recognizes truth.**

> **Interpretation concerns the correct understanding of an authoritative communication.**

These distinctions preserve the four-question architecture established at the beginning of the book.

---

# 20. What Does This Do to the Question of the Bible?

It changes the question profoundly.

The investigation is no longer asking:

> “Which religious book do people prefer?”

Nor:

> “Which tradition has the most followers?”

Nor:

> “Which theologian gives the most persuasive explanation?”

The question becomes:

> **Has the God who is the ground of truth actually revealed Himself and communicated truth to humanity?**

If the answer is yes, then the next question is:

> **How was that revelation given?**

And then:

> **How do we know that the writings called the Bible genuinely preserve and communicate that revelation?**

This is where the distinction between **God**, **revelation**, **inspiration**, **written record**, and **canon** becomes essential.

They must not be collapsed into one word.

---

# 21. A Necessary Correction to the Language of “God as Subject”

The phrase “God is the subject of the Bible” is inadequate if it is intended to mean merely that God is a topic discussed by human writers.

The biblical record presents something much stronger.

God is presented as:

- Creator;
- speaker;
- revealer;
- actor in history;
- giver of commandments;
- sender of prophets;
- Father;
- judge;
- and, in the New Testament, revealed in Christ.

Thus God is not merely **the subject about whom the book speaks**.

He is presented as **the living reality who acts, speaks, reveals, and stands behind the communication being recorded**.

That distinction becomes decisive in the next movements.

The question will no longer be merely:

> “What does the Bible say about God?”

It will become:

> **“How did God communicate what He intended humanity to know, and what makes the resulting record trustworthy?”**

---

# 22. What This Chapter Has Established

The chapter has established the conceptual necessity of distinguishing several propositions.

### First

Objective truth cannot simply be grounded in human preference, convention, institutional declaration, or finite cognition.

### Second

Contingent reality requires an adequate ultimate explanation.

### Third

The biblical conception of God presents Him not as a contingent object within creation but as Creator.

### Fourth

John 1:1–3 connects God, the Word, and creation in a manner directly relevant to the relationship between reality and divine Word.

### Fifth

Jesus Christ's declaration:

> **“I am ... the truth”**

makes truth a matter of divine identity rather than merely divine information.

### Sixth

The biblical record presents God as true, faithful, unchanging, and incapable of falsehood.

### Seventh

Those divine attributes are directly relevant to the possibility of trustworthy revelation.

### Eighth

God's existence and nature must not be confused with the later authentication of Scripture.

### Ninth

Reason does not replace revelation; it is required to investigate claims concerning revelation and to understand its content.

### Tenth

The investigation must now move from **God as the ground of truth** to **the attributes that make divine communication trustworthy**.

---

# 23. What Has Not Yet Been Established?

Intellectual discipline requires us to mark the boundary.

This chapter has not yet established:

- the complete biblical doctrine of God's attributes;
- the full argument that God cannot lie;
- the historical authentication of the Bible;
- the modes by which revelation was communicated;
- the meaning of inspiration;
- the authenticity of the biblical documents;
- the canon;
- or the authority of *graphē*.

Those questions belong to subsequent chapters.

The conclusion reached here is narrower but foundational:

> **If truth requires an ultimate ground, the biblical conception of God presents the kind of ultimate reality capable of grounding truth, reality, intelligibility, and trustworthy communication; the truthfulness and immutability of that God must now be examined directly.**

---

# 24. The Question That Must Now Be Answered

We have reached the next necessary question.

If God is the ground of truth, and if divine revelation is to be trusted because it proceeds from God, then we must know more than that God exists.

We must know **what kind of God He is**.

Is He truthful?

Is He faithful?

Does He change?

Can He contradict Himself?

Can He deceive?

Can He lie?

Can His counsel fail?

Can His Word prove false?

These are not secondary theological curiosities.

They determine whether divine revelation could possess the epistemic reliability claimed for it.

The next chapter therefore examines:

> **The Truthfulness, Faithfulness, and Immutability of God.**

## Conceptual distinctions preserved

| Distinction | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **God** | The ultimate divine reality under investigation |
| **Ground of truth** | That upon which objective truth ultimately depends |
| **Truthfulness** | The conformity of God's nature and communication to truth |
| **Revelation** | Divine communication of what is made known |
| **Inspiration** | The divine superintendence involved in the communication and recording of Scripture |
| **Written record** | The documentary form in which revelation/testimony is preserved |
| **Authentication** | The evidential process by which a claimed revelation or document is established as genuine |
| **Authority** | The right or standing to govern judgment |
| **Interpretation** | Correctly understanding what an authoritative communication means |

## Argumentative guardrails

- Do not use Scripture circularly to establish Scripture before its authenticity is examined.
- Do not use philosophy as a replacement for revelation.
- Do not confuse an argument for God's existence with an argument for biblical inspiration.
- Do not infer every divine attribute from one argument.
- Do not treat God as merely a topic invented by biblical writers.
- Do not reduce Christ's claim “I am ... the truth” to “Christ teaches true things.”
- Do not reduce God's truthfulness to a statistical history of accurate statements.
- Do not confuse the ground of truth with the human means by which truth is known.
- Do not allow the existence of human interpretations to obscure the distinction between God and human accounts about God.
- Do not turn cumulative evidence into a collection of disconnected proof texts.
