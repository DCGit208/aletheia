# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT I — TRUTH AND THE POSSIBILITY OF KNOWLEDGE

# Chapter 6 — Dianoetic Thinking and Sound Judgment

> **Central question:** What kind of disciplined thinking enables a person to perceive, analyze, compare, connect, distinguish, infer, and judge correctly?

Chapter 5 established the movement from premises to conclusions. It examined inference and ratiocination and showed why a conclusion must not outrun the grounds from which it is derived.

But reasoning does not occur in a vacuum.

Before a person can reason well, he must be capable of **seeing what is there to be reasoned about**.

He must perceive distinctions.

He must recognize relationships.

He must identify what belongs together and what does not.

He must separate a proposition from an interpretation of that proposition.

He must distinguish evidence from assumption.

He must recognize when two statements actually contradict one another and when they merely appear different.

He must be able to compare ideas without confusing comparison with identity.

He must be able to connect propositions without inventing connections that the evidence does not support.

And, finally, he must judge.

This is the intellectual territory of **dianoetic thinking**.

The concern is therefore broader than formal logic.

> **The question is not merely whether a person can construct an argument. The question is whether he can correctly perceive, organize, examine, relate, and judge the material from which the argument is constructed.**

That capacity becomes indispensable when the material under investigation is the written Word.

---

# 1. Thinking Is Not the Same as Thinking Well

Every person thinks.

That fact alone establishes very little.

People can think accurately or inaccurately.

They can reason carefully or carelessly.

They can perceive a distinction or overlook it.

They can notice evidence or ignore it.

They can connect propositions legitimately or connect them merely by association.

They can reach a conclusion because it follows—or because they already wanted the conclusion.

Therefore:

> **The existence of thought does not establish the quality of thought.**

The objective of intellectual formation is not simply to increase the amount of thinking.

It is to improve the **discipline, accuracy, coherence, and judgment** of thinking.

This distinction is especially important in the study of Scripture.

A person may read extensively and still misunderstand.

A person may possess a large vocabulary of biblical terminology and still reason poorly.

A person may memorize hundreds of passages and still fail to distinguish what one passage establishes from what he has added to it.

Knowledge of words and development of judgment are related, but they are not identical.

---

# 2. Dianoetic Thinking as Disciplined Intellectual Movement

The term **dianoetic** is used in this work to describe disciplined thinking through which the mind works **through and among ideas**—examining their distinctions, relations, implications, and grounds.

It is not being introduced as a substitute for Scripture.

Nor is it being introduced as an independent philosophical authority.

It names a **mode of intellectual engagement**.

The essential activities are those identified by the architecture of this book:

> **Perception → analysis → comparison → connection → distinction → inference → judgment**

Each activity has a purpose.

### Perception

What is actually present?

### Analysis

What are its constituent parts?

### Comparison

How does it resemble or differ from something else?

### Connection

What genuine relationship exists between the parts or propositions?

### Distinction

What must not be conflated?

### Inference

What follows from what has been established?

### Judgment

What conclusion should therefore be accepted?

This sequence is not a rigid mechanical algorithm.

It is a discipline for preventing intellectual shortcuts.

---

# 3. Perception Comes Before Interpretation

A person cannot accurately interpret what he has not accurately perceived.

This sounds obvious, but it is frequently violated.

A reader may overlook:

- a qualifier;
- a condition;
- a pronoun;
- a contrast;
- a temporal marker;
- a stated reason;
- a stated consequence;
- a change of speaker;
- a change of audience;
- or the boundaries of an argument.

Once something has been missed at the level of perception, subsequent reasoning may be perfectly consistent and still produce the wrong conclusion.

The first question is therefore:

> **What is actually there?**

Before:

> **What does it mean?**

And before:

> **What follows from it?**

This is one reason serious study requires careful reading.

The learner must first receive the object of study accurately.

---

# 4. Observation Must Be Distinguished From Interpretation

Consider two statements:

> **“The text uses the word X.”**

and:

> **“The author therefore means Y by X.”**

The first is an observation about the text.

The second is an interpretation.

The interpretation may be correct.

But it must be demonstrated.

Likewise:

> **“Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6.”**

is an observation.

> **“Jesus therefore establishes proposition P from that citation.”**

is an interpretive and inferential claim.

The reader must examine the argument to determine whether P actually follows.

This distinction prevents interpretation from being smuggled into the description of the text.

A disciplined investigator therefore keeps separate:

> **What is written**

> **What it appears to mean**

> **What it actually means after examination**

> **What follows from it**

Those are related stages, not interchangeable statements.

---

# 5. Analysis: Taking a Complex Whole Seriously

Analysis breaks a complex subject into intelligible components.

Suppose a person makes the claim:

> “Scripture is authoritative.”

That statement contains several questions.

What is meant by:

- Scripture?
- authority?
- authoritative?
- the scope of that authority?
- the grounds for that authority?
- the relationship between the text and the interpreter?

Without analysis, a person may assume he and his opponent are discussing the same proposition when they are not.

Analysis therefore prevents vague agreement and vague disagreement.

It asks:

> **What exactly are we talking about?**

This is particularly important in theological and philosophical disputes, where a single word may carry several meanings.

---

# 6. Comparison: Similarity Is Not Identity

Comparison places two or more things alongside one another so that similarities and differences can be examined.

But comparison must not collapse distinctions.

Two statements may both mention God without teaching the same thing.

Two passages may use the same word without using it in the same sense.

Two books may both discuss Scripture without having equal authority.

Two witnesses may report the same event without being the same witness.

Two interpretations may overlap in one respect while diverging fundamentally in another.

Therefore:

> **Similarity establishes a point of comparison; it does not establish identity.**

This simple rule protects the reader from a great deal of faulty reasoning.

---

# 7. Connection: Not Every Relationship Is a Logical Relationship

Human beings naturally associate ideas.

One word reminds us of another.

One doctrine reminds us of another.

One passage appears similar to another.

But association alone does not establish a legitimate connection.

A genuine intellectual connection must be demonstrated.

For example:

> A passage mentions “light.”

Another passage mentions “light.”

That similarity may justify comparison.

It does not automatically prove that the passages are discussing the same subject or establishing the same proposition.

The reader must examine context, terminology, argument, audience, and purpose.

Thus:

> **Connection requires grounds.**

This is especially important in biblical study because extensive cross-reference can either illuminate Scripture or create artificial relationships.

The difference is disciplined judgment.

---

# 8. Distinction: The Protection Against Conflation

Distinction is one of the most important intellectual operations in this book.

The investigator must distinguish:

- truth from belief;
- reality from appearance;
- evidence from assertion;
- Scripture from interpretation;
- God's Word from human commentary about God's Word;
- authentication from authority;
- authority from interpretation;
- quotation from understanding;
- possibility from probability;
- implication from entailment;
- observation from inference;
- institution from truth;
- tradition from revelation;
- and possession of Scripture from skill in Scripture.

Why are these distinctions necessary?

Because when unlike things are conflated, conclusions are transferred illegitimately.

For example:

> “The text is authoritative.”

does not entail:

> “My interpretation is authoritative.”

Likewise:

> “A church teaches X.”

does not entail:

> “X is therefore true.”

The institution's existence is one fact.

The truth of its doctrine is another.

Sound judgment keeps them distinct.

---

# 9. Whole and Part

Another essential intellectual discipline is understanding the relationship between a part and the whole.

A sentence belongs to a paragraph.

A paragraph belongs to an argument.

An argument belongs to a larger discourse.

A passage belongs to a book.

A book belongs to the larger body of writings.

Therefore an isolated sentence may not carry the entire meaning of the larger argument.

This does not mean that individual propositions are meaningless.

It means their meaning and function may depend upon their relations to the surrounding whole.

A disciplined reader therefore asks both:

> **What does this part say?**

and:

> **How does this part function within the whole?**

This is a major safeguard against proof-texting.

---

# 10. Context Is an Intellectual Discipline, Not a Decoration

Context is often mentioned as though it were an optional supplement to interpretation.

It is not.

Context can determine:

- who is speaking;
- who is being addressed;
- what question is being answered;
- what proposition is being asserted;
- what proposition is being rejected;
- what has already been established;
- and what conclusion is being drawn.

Therefore context participates directly in reasoning.

A statement removed from the argument that gives it meaning can be made to appear to say something it never intended.

This is why the mature reader does not merely collect quotations.

He reconstructs the argument.

---

# 11. Distinguishing Contradiction From Difference

Not every difference is a contradiction.

Two statements contradict one another only when they affirm incompatible propositions in the same respect and under the same conditions.

For example:

> “The door is open.”

and:

> “The door is closed.”

may contradict one another if they refer to the same door at the same time and in the same respect.

But:

> “The door was open at noon.”

and:

> “The door was closed at six.”

do not contradict one another.

The temporal distinction changes the proposition.

This principle is essential when investigating alleged contradictions in Scripture.

The correct method is not:

> “These statements look different, therefore Scripture contradicts itself.”

The correct method is:

1. identify each proposition precisely;
2. identify its context;
3. identify its time;
4. identify its subject;
5. identify its sense;
6. determine whether the propositions are genuinely incompatible.

Only then can contradiction be established.

---

# 12. Sound Judgment Requires Proportionality

Judgment is the point at which the investigator decides what conclusion should be accepted.

But judgment must be proportionate to the grounds.

If the evidence establishes a possibility, do not announce certainty.

If it establishes probability, do not announce necessity.

If it establishes necessity, do not weaken the conclusion merely because it is inconvenient.

This gives a basic rule:

> **The strength of the judgment should correspond to the strength and kind of the grounds.**

This is intellectual proportionality.

It prevents both exaggeration and unnecessary skepticism.

---

# 13. Sound Judgment Requires Considering Alternatives

A conclusion should not be judged in isolation when serious alternatives exist.

Suppose an event has been established.

The investigator should ask:

> **What explanations account for this event?**

Then:

- Which explanation fits the evidence?
- Which requires fewer unsupported assumptions?
- Which conflicts with known facts?
- Which explains the total evidence?
- Which depends upon ad hoc exceptions?
- Which merely describes a logical possibility without evidential support?

This is not indecision.

It is comparative judgment.

A conclusion becomes stronger when competing explanations have been seriously examined and found inadequate.

---

# 14. Intellectual Humility Is Not Intellectual Weakness

Sound judgment requires the ability to distinguish:

> **“I do not yet know.”**

from:

> **“It cannot be known.”**

These are not equivalent.

Likewise:

> **“I cannot currently explain this.”**

is not equivalent to:

> **“No explanation exists.”**

And:

> **“This is difficult.”**

is not equivalent to:

> **“This is contradictory.”**

The first statements recognize the limits of present knowledge.

The second statements make much larger claims.

A disciplined investigator does not convert personal difficulty into a universal impossibility.

---

# 15. The Difference Between Complexity and Confusion

A subject may be complex without being confused.

A long argument may contain many propositions while remaining coherent.

A large body of writing may contain many genres while maintaining identifiable relationships.

A difficult doctrine may require extensive reasoning without being irrational.

The proper response to complexity is therefore not premature simplification.

It is analysis.

> **Complexity requires more careful organization, not less thought.**

This principle becomes increasingly important as the book moves from the foundations of truth into the nature, history, transmission, authentication, and authority of the biblical writings.

---

# 16. Dianoetic Thinking and Biblical Investigation

The value of dianoetic thinking becomes clearest when applied directly to Scripture.

Suppose the reader encounters a claim:

> **“The scripture cannot be broken.”**

The disciplined investigator does not stop at repeating the sentence.

He asks:

### Perception

Where is the statement?

### Context

Who said it, to whom, and in response to what?

### Analysis

What propositions surround it?

### Comparison

What written text has been cited?

### Connection

How does the cited text relate to the argument?

### Distinction

What is Scripture, and what is the interpretation being made about Scripture?

### Inference

What conclusion does Jesus draw from the written statement?

### Judgment

Why does the argument establish what Jesus says?

This is exactly the kind of intellectual work that the book is training the reader to perform.

---

# 17. Jesus' Treatment of Scripture Demonstrates the Discipline

John 10:34–36 provides a particularly important example.

Jesus says:

> **“Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?”**

He then proceeds:

> **“If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came, and the scripture cannot be broken; Say ye of him, whom the Father hath sanctified, and sent into the world, Thou blasphemest; because I said, I am the Son of God?”**

A superficial reader may extract only the phrase:

> **“the scripture cannot be broken.”**

A dianoetic reader examines the entire movement.

Jesus identifies something as **written**.

He identifies it as belonging to **“your law.”**

He cites the actual wording:

> **“I said, Ye are gods.”**

He identifies those to whom the word of God came.

He then makes an argument concerning the accusation being brought against Him.

And within that argument He states:

> **“the scripture cannot be broken.”**

The significance is therefore not merely lexical.

It is argumentative.

Jesus treats the written statement as decisive within His reasoning.

The reader must understand **why**.

That is dianoetic reading.

---

# 18. The Intellect Must Be Able to Follow the Argument

This has a direct consequence for biblical education.

If a learner can repeat:

> “The scripture cannot be broken,”

but cannot explain:

- what Scripture refers to;
- what writing Jesus is citing;
- what proposition He is using;
- how the citation functions in His argument;
- why the conclusion follows;
- and what the statement establishes concerning Scripture,

then the learner has not yet demonstrated full intellectual command of the argument.

The problem is not lack of vocabulary.

It is lack of developed judgment.

This distinction will become increasingly important later when the book examines **unskillfulness in the Word**.

---

# 19. Dianoetic Thinking Does Not Grant the Interpreter Final Authority

There is a danger on the opposite side.

Once people discover the power of reasoning, they may begin treating their own reasoning as the final authority.

That is not the objective.

The disciplined reader must be able to say:

> **“This is what the text establishes.”**

rather than:

> **“This is what I have decided the text must mean.”**

Reason is therefore a servant of truth.

And when the authoritative written Word has been established as truth, reasoning must be exercised **under that authority**.

This is not anti-intellectual.

It is intellectually ordered.

---

# 20. The Difference Between Sound Judgment and Personal Preference

Personal preference is subjective.

Sound judgment is accountable to grounds.

A person may prefer one interpretation.

That preference does not establish its truth.

A person may dislike a conclusion.

That does not make the conclusion false.

A person may have grown up hearing a particular doctrine.

That does not establish the doctrine.

A person may belong to a respected institution.

That does not make every institutional proposition true.

Sound judgment asks:

> **What are the grounds?**

> **What follows from them?**

> **What competing conclusion has been considered?**

> **Why should this judgment be accepted?**

This is the difference between preference and reasoned judgment.

---

# 21. Dianoetic Thinking Is Transferable

The intellectual discipline developed here is not limited to biblical study.

The same structure applies when evaluating:

- scientific claims;
- historical claims;
- legal arguments;
- institutional policies;
- political claims;
- philosophical propositions;
- educational theories;
- economic arguments;
- technological claims;
- and public testimony.

In every case the investigator must ask:

> What is the claim?

> What are the grounds?

> What distinctions matter?

> What relationships exist?

> What follows?

> What does not follow?

> What competing explanation exists?

> What conclusion is warranted?

This is why the method has significance beyond one academic field.

It is a discipline of intellectual judgment.

---

# 22. The Scholarly Character of the Word Requires This Capacity

The Bible's scope makes superficial engagement inadequate.

The written record addresses:

- God;
- creation;
- humanity;
- history;
- morality;
- law;
- prophecy;
- nations;
- salvation;
- wisdom;
- judgment;
- divine purpose;
- and the future.

It contains narrative, law, poetry, wisdom, prophecy, correspondence, testimony, instruction, argument, and proclamation.

A body of writing with this scope demands serious intellectual engagement.

This is part of what the source material identifies as the **scholarly impetus of Scripture**.

The point is not to turn Scripture into an academic specimen.

The point is to recognize the intellectual seriousness demanded by the nature and scope of its claims.

If the learner approaches such a corpus without the capacity to distinguish, compare, analyze, reason, and judge, the difficulty will not necessarily lie in the absence of information.

It may lie in the inadequacy of the learner's intellectual handling.

---

# 23. Dianoetic Thinking and the Coming Doctrine of Reasonability

The present chapter prepares directly for Chapter 7:

> **Isaiah 1:18 — “Let Us Reason Together.”**

Chapter 6 has established the intellectual architecture.

Chapter 7 will place that architecture against a major biblical statement:

> **“Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD.”**

The significance of that movement must not be misunderstood.

The question is not whether human reason should replace the Word.

The question is whether engagement with the Word requires reasonability.

The answer prepared here is yes.

To understand a proposition, one must perceive it.

To understand its relation to another proposition, one must compare and connect.

To distinguish one conclusion from another, one must judge.

To determine whether a conclusion follows, one must reason.

Therefore:

> **Reasonability is not the enemy of Scripture. Undisciplined reasoning is.**

And:

> **The authority of Scripture does not eliminate intellectual responsibility; it gives that responsibility an authoritative object to understand and faithfully handle.**

---

# 24. Chapter Conclusion

The mature intellectual does not merely think.

He learns to **think accurately**.

He perceives before interpreting.

He analyzes before generalizing.

He compares without conflating.

He connects only where a real relationship exists.

He distinguishes what must remain distinct.

He follows implications without inventing them.

He tests apparent contradictions rather than announcing them prematurely.

He compares competing explanations.

He judges proportionately to the evidence.

And he remains willing to revise a conclusion when its grounds fail.

This is the discipline designated here as **dianoetic thinking and sound judgment**.

Its purpose in the present work is not to establish human intellect as an authority over Scripture.

Its purpose is to prepare the intellect to **encounter truth responsibly** and, ultimately, to handle the written Word with the seriousness that its claims require.

The progression is now clear:

> **Truth must be known.**

> **Knowledge requires evidence and warrant.**

> **Warrant requires reasoning.**

> **Reasoning requires disciplined perception, analysis, comparison, connection, distinction, inference, and judgment.**

The biblical question that naturally follows is therefore not merely whether people reason.

It is whether the Lord Himself calls people to reason in their encounter with His Word.

That question brings us to Isaiah 1:18.

## Critical distinctions

- observation ≠ interpretation;
- interpretation ≠ inference;
- similarity ≠ identity;
- association ≠ logical connection;
- relevance ≠ entailment;
- difference ≠ contradiction;
- possibility ≠ probability;
- complexity ≠ confusion;
- difficulty ≠ contradiction;
- preference ≠ judgment;
- possession of Scripture ≠ skill in Scripture;
- quotation ≠ complete understanding;
- Scripture ≠ interpretation of Scripture;
- authority of the text ≠ authority of the interpreter.

## Biblical reasoning model

When examining a biblical argument:

1. Locate the actual statement.
2. Read the surrounding context.
3. Identify the speaker and audience.
4. Identify the proposition.
5. Identify the cited or related text.
6. Determine the relationship among the propositions.
7. Identify the inference.
8. Determine the conclusion.
9. Test whether the conclusion follows.
10. Distinguish the biblical conclusion from the interpreter's addition.

## Application to John 10:34–36

The passage should be examined as an argument, not merely as a source of isolated quotations:

> **“Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?”**

and:

> **“If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came, and the scripture cannot be broken…”**

The later doctrine of the Graphē will return to this argument when establishing why Jesus treats the written Scripture as incapable of being broken.

## Authoring Guardrails

> **Do not merely tell the reader that a distinction exists. Demonstrate the distinction and explain why it matters to the argument.**

> **Do not call something a contradiction until the propositions have been precisely identified and shown to be incompatible in the same respect and under the same conditions.**

> **Do not turn similarities into identities or associations into logical connections.**

> **Do not allow the interpreter's conclusion to enter the text unnoticed.**

> **Do not treat context as decorative background. Context is part of determining what a proposition means and how it functions.**

> **Do not make human reasoning the ultimate authority. Teach disciplined reasoning under established truth.**

> **Do not reduce dianoetic thinking to formal syllogisms. The discipline includes perception, analysis, comparison, connection, distinction, inference, and judgment.**

> **Do not reduce Scripture to disconnected quotations. Follow its arguments.**

> **Do not merely state that Jesus reasoned from Scripture. Reconstruct enough of the argument for the reader to see how He reasoned.**

> **Do not confuse the reader's inability to explain a passage with proof that the passage is irrational or contradictory.**

> **Do not protect familiar interpretations from examination.**

> **Do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture. Teach the reader to reason through Scripture.**
