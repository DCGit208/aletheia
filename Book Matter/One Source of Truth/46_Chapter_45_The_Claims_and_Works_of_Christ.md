# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 45 — The Claims and Works of Christ

> **Movement VI — Christ as the Central Witness**

> **Central objective:** Examine what Christ claimed and did, and how those claims and works function within the biblical evidential argument. Show, with full force and from the authentic testimony now attached to a historical Person already identified as the Truth and the decisive revealer of the Father, that His public claims and public works belong together as a central authentication axis of Movement VI: the claims interpret the works, the works interpret the claims, and the conjunction is the record’s own offered grounds for recognizing who He is.

Chapter 44 identified the historical Person of Chapter 43 as the personal locus of truth and the decisive revealer of God. He claims identity with the Truth. He reveals the Father. Through Him God’s speech and acts are climactically disclosed. John 14:6 is an identity-claim of access to the Father, not a pedagogical slogan. That identification is already in force.

Chapter 44 ended by naming the next necessary question, not a new curiosity:

> **What did Jesus Christ claim and do, and how do those claims and works function within the biblical evidential argument concerning the One who is the Truth and the revealer of the Father?**

That is this chapter’s assignment.

The locked Table of Contents states the work in one sentence:

> **Examine what Christ claimed and did, and how those claims and works function within the biblical evidential argument.**

The chapter does not re-prove historical existence. It cites Chapter 43 as established. It does not re-argue identity with the Truth and decisive revelation of the Father. It cites Chapter 44 as established. It does not try the death and resurrection as a completed evidential case. It does not treat the eyewitness file, Christ’s confirmation of Scripture, or apostolic authority as finished arguments. It does not consummate canon, infallibility, or the Graphē as primary written authority. It performs the examination without which those later investigations would be investigations of a named identity whose public career had not yet been taken as an authentication axis.

A Person who is the Truth still acts and speaks in public. Identity does not replace deeds. It makes deeds the deeds of that identity. John 14:11 has already pointed forward: “believe me for the very works’ sake.” Chapter 26 already recorded, as the record’s own presentation, that Christ speaks with distinctive authority, that His teaching is public, that His works belong to the revelation, and that particular narratives join claim to visible act. Chapter 36 already established the general evidential category of manifestation, sign, wonder, and mighty work, and noted the special concentration of that category in Jesus. Those are premises. The new function is this:

> **To take the claims and the works of the historical Person, already identified as the Truth, as a single public conjunction, and to state what that conjunction warrants as an authentication axis before Movement VI turns to His death and resurrection.**

The remaining questions of Movement VI follow by sequence. Sequence continues the investigation. It does not convert the present axis into hesitation.

## Central Objective

> **To define the question of Christ’s claims and works as an authentication axis; to distinguish that question from bare historical existence, from revelatory identity, from the death-and-resurrection case, and from the eyewitness, Scripture-confirmation, and apostolic-authority files; to examine the major classes of claims and of works in their biblical contexts rather than as slogans or marvel-lists; to show how claims and works mutually interpret each other in the record’s own argument; to expose the reductions that fail; and to state, with full force, that the claims-and-works conjunction is a central authentication axis of Movement VI in the sense this chapter defines.**

The governing movement is:

> **Identified Person → question of what He claimed and did → definition of claim, work, and conjunction → biblical record of claim-classes and work-classes → mutual interpretation → failure of reductions → inspectable inference → warranted authentication axis → sequence to death and resurrection.**

The chapter’s conclusion is affirmative:

> **Jesus Christ, already established as a historical figure of the early first century and already identified as the Truth and the revealer of the Father, is presented by the authentic written testimony as one whose public claims and public works belong together as a central authentication axis. He claimed authority to forgive sins, a unique relation to the Father, lordship over Sabbath, law, and Temple, eschatological judgment, messianic identity, and the kingdom of God. He healed, expelled demons, commanded nature, taught with authority, kept table with the excluded, confronted opposition, and raised the dead in the public ministry. Claim is not proof by assertion alone. Work is not magic spectacle detached from identity. The record joins them: the works interpret the claims, and the claims interpret the works. That conjunction is the testimony’s own positive case, stated here with full force. It is not yet the evidential case for His death and resurrection, or the later doctrine of the Graphē as primary written authority.**

The remaining questions follow by sequence. The axis is already in force.

# 45.1 The Question Created by the Preceding Chapter

Movement VI has located a Person and identified Him as the Truth. It has not yet examined the public career by which that identity is authenticated in the record’s own argument.

Chapter 43 established existence, publicity, chronology, Jewish setting, crucifixion under Pilate, and the failure of myth and coreless legend. Chapter 44 established identity with the Truth and decisive revelation of the Father, with John 14:6 as an identity-claim of access, and with Johannine, Synoptic, and Pauline texts converging on that architecture. Those conclusions are premises here.

A Person can be identified as the Truth and still leave a further question unanswered: what did He claim in public, what did He do in public, and how do those claims and works function as evidence concerning the identity now named? Identity that never acts is not the identity the Gospels present. Works that never mean anything are not the works the Gospels present. The writings that have now been used as authentic testimony do not stop at John 14:6. They present this man as claiming authority, performing works described as signs, forgiving sins, commanding nature and demons, teaching as one who has authority, eating with those His opponents excluded, confronting the institutions of His day, and raising the dead. Those are further claims about a career. They are the claims for which a historical baseline and a revelatory identification now exist.

The question is therefore not:

> **Did a man named Jesus occupy Galilee and Judea under Tiberius, and does the authentic testimony present Him as the Truth and the revealer of the Father?**

Those questions have been answered.

The question is:

> **What does the authentic testimony establish concerning this man’s public claims and public works, and how do those claims and works function within the biblical evidential argument?**

If the claims stood alone, Movement VI would be the investigation of assertion. If the works stood alone, Movement VI would be the investigation of marvels. If claims and works interpret each other as the record presents them, Movement VI is the investigation of a public authentication axis attached to the One who is the Truth.

That is a different operation from piety. It is the operation this chapter must perform.

# 45.3 Define the Question: Claims and Works as an Authentication Axis

A **claim**, in this chapter, is a public assertion concerning identity, authority, office, or relation, made by Jesus or immediately attached to His speech in the Gospel narratives, such that hearers in the narrative world can recognize what is being asserted and can contest it.

A **work**, in this chapter, is a public act of Jesus recorded as belonging to the same ministry: healing, expulsion of demons, command of nature, authoritative teaching as an act and not merely as a syllabus, table fellowship as a performed judgment about persons, confrontation as a performed judgment about institutions, and the raising of the dead in the course of the ministry.

An **authentication axis** is a structured pairing in which two classes of public facts interpret each other so that the identity under investigation is not left as bare assertion and is not left as uninterpreted spectacle.

The question of this chapter joins those three:

> **Does the authentic testimony present the claims and works of Jesus Christ as a public conjunction that functions as a central authentication axis concerning the Person already identified as the Truth?**

Several elements belong to the question as this chapter defines it.

First, **publicity**. The claims are not secret maxims whispered to one recipient. The works are not private interior states. Chapter 43 already established that the career is geographic, audible, social, controversial, and bounded. This chapter takes that publicity as the field in which claims and works occur.

Second, **classes, not slogans**. “Jesus claimed to be the Messiah” is a slogan until the contexts are examined: Nazareth’s synagogue, Caesarea Philippi, the Samaritan well, the Baptist’s question, the entry into Jerusalem, the hearing before the council. “Jesus worked miracles” is a slogan until healings, exorcisms, nature-signs, and raisings are distinguished, and until each class is attached to the claims it interprets.

Third, **mutual interpretation**. A claim interprets a work by saying what the work means. A work interprets a claim by exhibiting in the public field what the claim asserts. The record’s argument is this mutual bearing, not a list of titles beside a list of marvels.

Fourth, **evidential function**. The question is not merely what happened in a narrative. It is how the conjunction functions within the biblical evidential argument: what it is offered to establish, what reductions fail, and what conclusion is warranted at this stage.

Fifth, **examinability**. These are claims and acts inside a warranted written testimony about a historical Person. They can be defined, distinguished, compared with reductions, and reasoned through. They are not exempt from investigation because they are theological. They are not reduced to psychology because they are personal.

Thus:

> **The question of the claims and works of Christ is the question of the public conjunction by which the authentic testimony authenticates the identity already named, asked of the historical Person already established, under the writings already warranted as testimony.**

This is the object of Chapter 43. It includes a real person, a Jewish setting, Galilee and Judea, association with John the Baptist, public teaching, disciples, conflict, and execution under Pilate. Those conclusions are premises here. They are not this chapter’s assignment.

## 45.4.2 Christ as truth and divine revelation

This is the object of Chapter 44. It includes His claim of identity with the Truth, His revelation of the Father, the Word made flesh, the climactic speech of God in the Son, and the convergence of Johannine, Synoptic, and Pauline testimony on that architecture. Those conclusions are premises here. This chapter does not re-try John 14:6.

## 45.4.3 The claims-and-works conjunction

This is the object of the present chapter. It includes: the major classes of claims examined in context; the major classes of works as claimed public acts belonging to the same testimony; the mutual interpretation of claim and work; the biblical authentication pattern of claim, challenge, visible act, and interpretation; and the warrant for treating that conjunction as a central authentication axis of Movement VI.

## 45.4.4 Death and resurrection

Chapter 46 examines the central historical claims concerning Christ’s death and resurrection and their significance for apostolic testimony. Crucifixion under Pilate is already a historical baseline fact. Predictions of His own death and rising belong to the claim-class of the ministry and will be named as such. The evidential case that God raised Him is reserved. This chapter does not convert the raising of Jairus’s daughter, the widow’s son, or Lazarus into a substitute for His own resurrection, nor postpone the public career until resurrection is finished.

The governing distinction is therefore:

> **Historical existence ≠ identity with truth and decisive revelation ≠ claims-and-works authentication ≠ death-and-resurrection case ≠ eyewitness / Scripture-confirmation / apostolic-authority files.**

Each is a real object. Each has a chapter. This chapter takes the third.

# 45.6 Claim Defined

A **claim** is not a mood, a later title, or a community’s slogan. It is an assertion, in a setting, with a content that can be identified.

The Gospels report Jesus making claims in the first person, receiving claims made about Him in His presence, and performing acts that are themselves claims. “Thy sins be forgiven thee” is a claim in speech. Riding into Jerusalem in the manner the crowd received as royal is a claim in act. Cleansing the Temple is a claim in act. Eating with publicans is a claim in act about who belongs at the table of the kingdom. The chapter therefore does not restrict “claim” to sentences beginning with “I am.”

Several distinctions protect the definition.

First, **claim is not yet proof.** To record that Jesus claimed authority to forgive is to record a proposition asserted. Whether the assertion is true is a further question. The record itself does not treat the assertion as self-certifying. It attaches a visible work and an interpretation: “that ye may know.” The distinction is the record’s before it is this chapter’s.

Second, **claim is not later ecclesiastical vocabulary imposed backward.** The chapter examines what the narratives present Jesus as asserting in Synoptic and Johannine settings, and what early apostolic summaries present as the public career. Titles such as “Christ,” “Son of man,” “Lord of the Sabbath,” and “Son” are the record’s terms in those settings. They are not postponed until Nicaea, and they are not reduced to Nicaea.

Third, **claim is not isolated slogan.** Context, audience, opposition, and attached act belong to the claim. “The Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins” in a crowded house at Capernaum, spoken over a paralytic, before scribes who name the implication, is a different object from the same English words on a banner.

Fourth, **claim includes implicit assertion by act.** The chapter will not pretend that only explicit first-person sentences count. Table fellowship and Temple-cleansing are claims. They must still be interpreted by the speech that accompanies them, or they become mute gestures.

The Greek term **ἐξουσία (*exousia*, G1849)** is the ordinary Gospel word for the authority claimed and exercised: authority to teach, to forgive, to cast out demons, to judge. Lexically it denotes right, liberty, and delegated or possessed power to act. Context determines whether the authority is derived, unique, contested, or acknowledged. The chapter will not treat the lexicon as a Christology. It will treat the word as the narratives’ own name for the thing under dispute.

Thus:

> **A claim, in this chapter, is a public assertion of identity or authority, in speech or in act, in a specified setting, offered by the testimony as belonging to Jesus’ ministry and as capable of being understood, contested, and — in the record’s argument — authenticated by works.**

# 45.7 Work Defined

A **work** is not a private feeling, a later miracle-legend in the abstract, or a display of power for its own sake. It is a public act attributed to Jesus in the ministry, of a kind the narratives present as observable, as connected with His identity, and as belonging to the same testimony as His speech.

Chapter 36 already distinguished four biblical names that must remain in force.

**Sign** — **σημεῖον (*sēmeion*, G4592)** — an act that points beyond itself. John’s Gospel prefers this term. A sign is not exhausted by the marvel. It signifies.

**Wonder** — **τέρας (*teras*, G5059)** — an act as it strikes beholders. Wonder names the effect on observers. It does not by itself name the meaning.

**Mighty work** — **δύναμις (*dynamis*, G1411)** — an act as power. The term names the force exhibited. It does not by itself name the identity of the agent.

**Work** — **ἔργον (*ergon*, G2041)** — the broader term. In John, “the works” are repeatedly the Father’s works done by the Son. Teaching, as Matthew 7:28–29 presents it, is also a work of authority. Table fellowship is a work. Confrontation is a work. The chapter therefore does not restrict “works” to nature-miracles.

Several distinctions protect the definition.

First, **work is not magic.** Chapter 36 already forbade the collapse. Magic, in the sense here refused, is technique detached from the God of the record, aimed at control, indifferent to truth. Jesus’ works are attached to the Father, to the kingdom, to compassion, to judgment, and to identity. They are not sold, not coerced as spectacle on demand, and not detached from speech.

Second, **work is not proof by marvel alone.** An extraordinary event, even if granted, does not interpret itself. The Beelzebul controversy exists in the narratives precisely because opponents grant unusual power and contest the source. Meaning is required. The claims supply it.

Third, **work includes more than healing and nature.** If only marvels counted, the ministry’s table and Temple would drop out of the authentication axis. They belong. They are public acts that enact the claims.

Fourth, **work in this chapter is ministry-work, not the resurrection of Jesus Himself.** Raising others belongs here. His own rising belongs to Chapter 46. The distinction is of object, not of piety.

Thus:

> **A work, in this chapter, is a public act of the ministry — including healings, exorcisms, nature-signs, authoritative teaching, table fellowship, confrontation, and the raising of the dead — presented by the testimony as belonging to Jesus, as observable in the public field, and as significant for the identity He claims.**

---

# 45.8 The Conjunction: Why Claims and Works Must Be Examined Together

The biblical argument is not two parallel lists. It is a conjunction.

A claim without a work is an assertion about authority that the hearer is asked to accept on the speaker’s word alone. The record does not treat that as its offered authentication. The paralytic narrative does not end at “thy sins be forgiven thee.” John 14:11 does not end at “I am in the Father.” John 10:37–38 reasons: if I do not the works of my Father, believe me not; if I do, though ye believe not me, believe the works.

A work without a claim is an uninterpreted event. Storms cease. The sick rise. Crowds are fed. The question remains: by what authority, in whose name, toward what identity, as the sign of what? Matthew 11:2–6 exists because works are offered as the answer to a messianic question, not as a catalogue of marvels for their own sake. John 20:30–31 exists because signs are written so that the reader may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God.

The conjunction is therefore:

> **Claim supplies the meaning of the work. Work supplies the public exhibition of the claim. Together they constitute the authentication axis the record itself offers for the ministry.**

This is not a modern apologetic invention laid upon the texts. It is the texts’ own reasoning. Mark 2:10 states the purpose of the healing: “that ye may know.” John 5:36 names the works as a greater witness than John the Baptist. John 10:25: “the works that I do in my Father’s name, they bear witness of me.” Acts 2:22, in the first public Christian sermon in Jerusalem, summarizes the career as a man approved of God by miracles, wonders, and signs which God did by Him in the midst of the hearers. Acts 10:38 summarizes the same career as anointing with the Holy Ghost and with power, going about doing good and healing, “for God was with him.”

The investigator who separates the files — titles in one column, marvels in another — has already abandoned the record’s argument. The investigator who fuses them into a blur — so that any impressive act proves any impressive title — has also abandoned it. The discipline is to keep both, and to keep their relation.

Thus:

> **Claims and works are two poles of one public testimony. The chapter’s object is their conjunction, not either pole as a substitute for the other.**

# 45.9 Publicity Already Earned

Chapter 43 established that the ministry is public: geographic, audible, social, controversial, and bounded. That conclusion governs the present examination.

The claims this chapter treats are claims made in synagogues, in houses crowded to the door, on hillsides, in the Temple courts, at tables, on the lake, in villages, and before opponents who can report what they heard. John 18:20 remains in force: He spoke openly to the world; He taught in synagogue and Temple; in secret He said nothing as the definition of the ministry.

The works this chapter treats are works done before families, crowds, disciples, towns, and hostile observers. Chapter 36 already noted that unbelieving and hostile observers belong to the evidential field. The Pharisees who plot after a Sabbath healing, the witnesses who go from Bethany to the authorities after Lazarus, the scribes who sit in the crowded house at Capernaum — these are not later ecclesiastical ornaments. They are the narrative’s insistence that the career was available to contest.

Publicity does not by itself prove every reported act. It establishes the kind of object under investigation. A secret gnosis cannot be this ministry. A private illumination cannot be this ministry. A later community’s interior warmth cannot be this ministry. The authentication axis, if it exists, exists in the open.

The bounded span also remains in force. A few years of public activity, concentrated in Galilee and brought to a crisis in Jerusalem, is the field. The chapter is not an encyclopedia of later Christian wonder-stories. It is an examination of the career the Gospels present as His.

Thus:

> **The claims-and-works axis is an axis in public history, of a short and located career, already shown to be the kind of object historical method can locate. This chapter examines its interior as testimony.**

# 45.10 Authority to Forgive Sins

Mark 2:1–12, with its parallels in Matthew 9:1–8 and Luke 5:17–26, is the controlling narrative for this claim-class.

Jesus is in a house at Capernaum. The crowd fills the place. A paralytic is brought, lowered through the roof in Mark’s account. Jesus sees their faith and speaks first not to the legs but to the man:

> **“Son, thy sins be forgiven thee.”**
> — Mark 2:5

The scribes reason: “Why doth this man thus speak blasphemies? who can forgive sins but God only?” (Mark 2:7). The narrative therefore does not leave the implication for a later theologian to discover. The implication is named in the room, by opponents, as the meaning of the speech.

Jesus knows their reasoning and puts the question:

> **“Whether is it easier to say to the sick of the palsy, Thy sins be forgiven thee; or to say, Arise, and take up thy bed, and walk? But that ye may know that the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins, (he saith to the sick of the palsy,) I say unto thee, Arise, and take up thy bed, and go thy way into thine house.”**
> — Mark 2:9–11

The man rises, takes the bed, and goes before them all. They are amazed and glorify God.

The pattern is explicit:

> **claim → recognition of the claim’s implication → challenge → visible work → stated purpose of the work → public result.**

Several elements must be kept distinct.

First, **the claim is the forgiveness.** It is not a wish that God would forgive. It is not a priestly declaration in the Temple’s appointed system. It is Jesus’ own speech as the act of forgiveness. The scribes hear it as an encroachment upon what belongs to God. The narrative agrees that the implication is that high. It then offers the healing as the public exhibit “that ye may know.”

Second, **the title in the purpose-clause is “the Son of man.”** The authority claimed is authority “on earth.” The setting is not a later heavenly session. It is a house in Capernaum during the ministry. Eschatological overtones of “Son of man” are not denied. They are not required to make the present point: in the ministry, this man claims power on earth to forgive, and attaches a visible work to the claim.

Third, **the work does not replace the claim.** A healing without the word of forgiveness would be a healing. The narrative’s stated purpose is knowledge of the authority to forgive. The legs become evidence for a fact the eyes cannot directly see. That is the logic of a sign.

Fourth, **Luke 7:36–50** belongs to the same class in another setting. In a Pharisee’s house, a woman known as a sinner anoints Jesus. He says, “Her sins, which are many, are forgiven.” The guests reason, “Who is this that forgiveth sins also?” He then interprets her act as love corresponding to forgiveness. Table, controversy, and claim again coincide. The Capernaum pattern is not an isolated curiosity.

Fifth, **Matthew 9:8** records the crowd glorifying God “which had given such power unto men.” The crowd’s inference is not the ceiling of the claim. It is a response. The scribes’ inference — only God forgives — is the sharper reading of what was said. The chapter records both. It does not flatten the claim into a general human empowerment in order to avoid the scribes’ point.

Thus:

> **The authentic testimony presents Jesus as claiming authority on earth to forgive sins, in public, before opponents who name the implication, and as attaching a visible work whose stated purpose is that the authority may be known. This is a claim-class of the first order, not a pastoral metaphor.**

# 45.11 Relation to the Father

Chapter 44 already established that the Son reveals the Father, that seeing the Son is seeing the Father, and that the Son does what He sees the Father do. That identification is a premise. The present section examines the relation-to-the-Father as a public claim-class of the ministry, attached to works, contested in controversy, and offered as the meaning of the works.

John 5 begins with a healing at Bethesda on the Sabbath. The healed man carries his bed. Controversy follows. Jesus answers:

> **“My Father worketh hitherto, and I work.”**
> — John 5:17

The narrator states the implication: the Jews sought the more to kill Him, because He not only had broken the Sabbath, but said that God was His Father, making Himself equal with God (John 5:18). As in Mark 2, the opposition names the height of the claim. The discourse that follows is the interpretation of the work: the Son can do nothing of Himself but what He sees the Father do; the Father shows Him all things; as the Father raises up the dead and quickens, so the Son quickens whom He will; the Father has committed judgment to the Son; the works which the Father has given Him to finish bear witness that the Father has sent Him (John 5:19–36).

The claim-class is therefore not a detached metaphysical slogan. It is the interpretation of a public healing, in a Sabbath controversy, issuing in a discourse about shared work, life, judgment, and witness.

John 10, in the Temple at the feast of dedication, continues the class. The Jews ask how long He will make them doubt: “If thou be the Christ, tell us plainly.” He answers:

> **“I told you, and ye believed not: the works that I do in my Father’s name, they bear witness of me.”**
> — John 10:25

And:

> **“I and my Father are one.”**
> — John 10:30

They take up stones. He reasons from the works:

> **“If I do not the works of my Father, believe me not. But if I do, though ye believe not me, believe the works: that ye may know, and believe, that the Father is in me, and I in him.”**
> — John 10:37–38

The Scripture-argument that stands in the same discourse (John 10:34–36) is reserved for Movement VIII. The works-argument is this chapter’s assignment. It is explicit: the works in the Father’s name are witness; unity with the Father is claimed; the works are offered as the path to knowing that the Father is in Him and He in the Father. Claim and work are one architecture.

The sending-claims belong here as well, without repeating Chapter 44’s revelatory conclusion as if it were new. “My doctrine is not mine, but his that sent me” (John 7:16). “I proceeded forth and came from God; neither came I of myself, but he sent me” (John 8:42). “I have given unto them the words which thou gavest me” (John 17:8). These sentences present the ministry’s speech as the Father’s sending, not as independent religious entrepreneurship. They are claims about origin and authority. The works are the public counterpart: the Father’s works done by the sent Son.

Matthew 11:27 remains in force from Chapter 44 as a Synoptic claim of unique reciprocal knowledge. Its neighbouring verses (Matthew 11:20–24, 11:2–6) attach that claim to mighty works done in cities and to the catalogue offered to John’s disciples. Relation to the Father is not an interior mystic’s report. It is a public claim beside public works.

Thus:

> **The authentic testimony presents Jesus as claiming a unique relation to the Father — Son, sent, one with the Father, doer of the Father’s works — in controversies attached to public acts, and as offering those acts as witness to the relation claimed.**

# 45.12 Lordship over the Sabbath

The Sabbath is not a minor ritual preference in Second Temple Judaism. It is a creational and covenantal marker, publicly kept, publicly contested. To claim lordship over it is to claim authority at the point where Israel’s weekly life names the God who sanctified the seventh day.

Mark 2:23–28 presents the disciples plucking ears of corn on the Sabbath. The Pharisees object. Jesus answers from David’s eating of the shewbread, then states the principle:

> **“The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath: therefore the Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath.”**
> — Mark 2:27–28

The claim is not that Sabbath is nothing. It is that the Son of man is Lord of it. The title again is “Son of man.” The object is the institution that marks Israel’s week. Lordship here is authority to interpret the day’s purpose and to override a hostile application.

Mark 3:1–6 immediately attaches a work. In the synagogue is a man with a withered hand. They watch whether Jesus will heal on the Sabbath, that they might accuse Him. He asks whether it is lawful to do good or to do evil on the Sabbath, to save life or to kill. They are silent. He heals the hand. The Pharisees take counsel with the Herodians how they might destroy Him. Claim, work, and lethal opposition stand in one sequence.

John 5, already examined for the Father-claim, is also a Sabbath-claim. The healing at Bethesda and the command to carry the bed provoke the charge. Jesus’ answer, “My Father worketh hitherto, and I work,” interprets Sabbath-work as participation in the Father’s ongoing work. Luke 13:10–17 (the woman bent eighteen years) and Luke 14:1–6 (the man with dropsy) extend the class: synagogue and meal, question about lawfulness, healing, and the exposure of hypocrisy about ox and ass.

The claim-class is therefore consistent across Synoptic and Johannine settings:

> **Jesus claims authority over the Sabbath’s meaning and use, performs works of healing on the Sabbath as the exhibit of that authority, and draws opposition precisely because the authority is recognized as more than a rabbinic opinion.**

A reformer might argue for a humane casuistry. The narratives present more: the Son of man as Lord of the day itself. The works on the day are not accidents of scheduling. They are the public form of the lordship claimed.

Thus:

> **Lordship over the Sabbath is a public claim of authority over a central covenantal institution, authenticated in the narratives by healings done on the day and by the opposition those healings provoke.**

---

# 45.13 Lordship over Law and Temple

Two further institutions of Israel’s public life stand in the same field: the Law as it is taught, and the Temple as it is the house of God.

Matthew 5:17–22 locates Jesus in relation to the Law:

> **“Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil.”**
> — Matthew 5:17

Fulfilment is not abolition. It is also not mere repetition. The antitheses follow: “Ye have heard that it was said… but I say unto you.” Chapter 26 already noted that this form is distinctive over against “Thus saith the LORD.” The present point is the claim-class: He places His “I say” as the authoritative interpretation and intensification of what was said to the ancients, while claiming not to destroy the Law. Authority over the Law’s meaning, in His own name, in a public sermon, is a claim of the ministry. It is not yet Chapter 48’s doctrine of His confirmation of Scripture as Graphē. It is the career’s claim to speak as Lord of the Law’s application.

Matthew 12:6, in a Sabbath controversy, adds:

> **“But I say unto you, That in this place is one greater than the temple.”**
> — Matthew 12:6

The comparison is explicit. Temple is the highest sacred place of the nation’s worship. He claims to be greater. The claim is not a later church’s architecture. It is a sentence in a dispute about disciples and corn.

The Temple-cleansing is a claim in act. John 2:13–17 places a cleansing at a Passover near the beginning of the public ministry; Mark 11:15–18 and parallels place a cleansing in the final week. The chapter does not require a single-chronology decision to establish the class. In both presentations, Jesus drives out sellers and money-changers, names the house as a house of prayer, and charges that it has been made a den of thieves. The act is public, at the feast, in the precincts. It is a performed judgment on the Temple’s present use. Mark records that the scribes and chief priests heard it and sought how to destroy Him, because the people were astonished at His doctrine.

John 2:18–22 attaches a further saying: “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” The Jews hear stones. The narrator hears His body. The saying is therefore both a Temple-claim and a passion-claim. The passion-and-resurrection case is Chapter 46. The present section records that the ministry includes a public claim about the Temple so sharp that it can be turned into an accusation at the trial (Mark 14:58). A man who acts as judge of the Temple and speaks of its destruction and raising has claimed authority at Israel’s center. The full meaning of the raising is reserved. The claim-class is already here.

Thus:

> **The authentic testimony presents Jesus as claiming authority to fulfil and to speak in His own name concerning the Law, as claiming to be greater than the Temple, and as performing public judgment in the Temple courts. Law and Temple are not incidental scenery. They are objects of His claimed lordship.**

---

# 45.14 Eschatological Judgment

The ministry includes the claim that Jesus Himself stands as judge in the end of the matter — not merely as a preacher of someone else’s judgment.

John 5:22, 27, in the discourse already cited:

> **“For the Father judgeth no man, but hath committed all judgment unto the Son. … And hath given him authority to execute judgment also, because he is the Son of man.”**

Judgment here is not a private moral opinion. It is the authority the Father has committed. The title is again Son of man. The claim is attached, in the same discourse, to the works and to the quickening of the dead.

Matthew 7:21–23, in the public sermon, presents a future scene in which people address Him as Lord, plead works done in His name, and hear Him say, “I never knew you: depart from me.” The speaker of the sermon places Himself as the one who will refuse or receive. Matthew 13’s parables of wheat and tares, and of the net, name the Son of man as the one who sends forth angels and severs the wicked from the just. Matthew 25:31–46 presents the Son of man come in His glory, sitting on the throne of His glory, all nations gathered before Him, the king separating as a shepherd. The sheep and goats are judged in relation to what was done to “the least of these my brethren,” and the king says “me.” The judge is not an anonymous divine function. The judge is this man, speaking now of that session.

Mark 8:38 and parallels attach confession of Him now to His coming in the glory of His Father with the holy angels. The eschatological claim is therefore not a detached apocalyptic timetable. It is this Person’s claim that final judgment is judgment in relation to Him.

Luke 12:8–9: “Whosoever shall confess me before men, him shall the Son of man also confess before the angels of God.” The correlation is explicit.

This claim-class does not wait upon Chapter 46. A man may claim to be the coming judge and still have His own death and rising as a further public act. The present point is that the ministry already locates judgment in Him. Works of healing and expulsion of demons are, in Matthew 12:28, signs that the kingdom of God is come upon them — present irruption of the rule whose future session He also claims.

Thus:

> **The authentic testimony presents Jesus as claiming that the Father has committed judgment to Him, that the Son of man will sit as king over the nations, and that present confession of Him bears upon that future. Eschatological judgment is a claim of the career, not only a later church’s hope.**

---

# 45.15 Messianic Identity

“Christ” — **Χριστός (*Christos*)**, corresponding to **Μεσσίας (*Messias*)** and to the Hebrew **מָשִׁיחַ (*māšîaḥ*)**, “anointed” — is not a surname. It is an office-claim in Israel’s hope: the anointed king of David’s line, in the prophetic and Second Temple air the Gospels assume. Chapter 43 already located Jesus in that Jewish world. This section examines how the testimony presents Him as claiming, receiving, and enacting that identity.

Luke 4:16–21 is a public opening. In the synagogue at Nazareth, He reads Isaiah 61:1–2 — the Spirit of the Lord upon me, anointed to preach good tidings to the poor, sent to proclaim release — and says, “This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears.” The claim is messianic in the Isaiah register: anointed, sent, gospel to the poor. The congregation’s subsequent rage does not cancel the claim. It exhibits its offensiveness when attached to this particular townsman.

Matthew 11:2–6 / Luke 7:18–23 is the controlling test of works against messianic expectation, and will receive its own section. Here it is enough to name the question John sends: “Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another?” The question is messianic. The answer is a catalogue of works plus gospel to the poor, in Isaiah’s key.

Mark 8:27–30 / Matthew 16:13–20 / Luke 9:18–21: at Caesarea Philippi, “Whom say ye that I am?” Peter answers, “Thou art the Christ.” In Matthew, “the Christ, the Son of the living God.” Jesus receives the confession, names its source as the Father, and charges them to tell no man. The messianic claim is therefore not a late Johannine invention. It is Synoptic, attached to a named place, to a named disciple, and to a charge of silence that belongs to the ministry’s own pacing — not to later embarrassment alone, and not to later invention of the title.

John 4:25–26, at the well in Samaria: the woman names the Messiah who “will tell us all things.” Jesus says, “I that speak unto thee am he.” The claim is plain in a setting outside Judean controversy, to a Samaritan, beside a conversation about worship and living water.

The entry into Jerusalem (Mark 11:1–10 and parallels) is a claim in act: the colt, the garments, the cries of “Hosanna,” the appeal to David’s kingdom. Zechariah 9:9 stands in Matthew’s interpretation. Whether every bystander understood the same thing, the act is public and royal. The subsequent Temple-cleansing continues the enacted claim.

At the hearing, Mark 14:61–62: “Art thou the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?” Jesus answers, “I am: and ye shall see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven.” The messianic claim, the Son-claim, and the Son-of-man judgment-claim coincide at the crisis. The high priest names it blasphemy. The narrative presents it as the testimony Jesus gives of Himself when asked under oath.

Thus:

> **Messianic identity is not a slogan pasted on a sage. It is claimed in Nazareth from Isaiah, tested by works in answer to John, confessed at Caesarea Philippi and received, spoken plainly in Samaria, enacted in the entry to Jerusalem, and affirmed before the council. The works-classes that follow are the public exhibits of this identity, not a separate career.**

---

# 45.16 The Kingdom Proclaimed

The Synoptic ministry opens with a proclamation:

> **“The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand: repent ye, and believe the gospel.”**
> — Mark 1:15

Matthew 4:17: “Repent: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.” Luke 4:43: “I must preach the kingdom of God to other cities also: for therefore am I sent.” The kingdom — **βασιλεία (*basileia*, G932)**, reign, realm, royal rule — is the content of the public announcement. It is not a later ecclesiastical synonym for the church. It is God’s rule declared as near, and declared by this man as His sending.

The kingdom-claim is a claim about Jesus’ own role, not only about a general hope. He is the proclaimer. He is the one by whose expulsion of demons the kingdom has come upon them (Matthew 12:28; Luke 11:20). He is the one who gives the keys of the kingdom in Matthew 16:19. He is the king in the judgment scene of Matthew 25. He teaches the kingdom in parables as one who knows its secret (Mark 4:11). He sends the Twelve to preach it (Luke 9:2). The Beatitudes name those to whom the kingdom belongs (Matthew 5:3, 10).

Luke 17:20–21 refuses a merely spectacular mapping: the kingdom does not come with observation as they expect; “the kingdom of God is among you.” The saying does not cancel the future session. It locates the reign as present in His person and work among them.

Kingdom and works interpret each other. Healings and exorcisms are not humanitarian extras. They are the irruption of the announced reign: the blind see, the lame walk, the oppressed are freed. Table fellowship is the kingdom’s table in advance. Confrontation with the Temple’s misuse is the kingdom’s claim on the house of God. The proclamation is therefore a claim-class that gathers the others: Messiah, Son, Lord of Sabbath, forgiver, judge — these are offices and acts of the reign He announces as at hand in Himself.

Thus:

> **The kingdom of God, proclaimed as at hand and exhibited in the works, is a claim that this man’s presence is the presence of God’s reign. It is not a vague spirituality. It is the public thesis of the Synoptic career.**

---

# 45.17 Authority over Life in the Public Ministry

John 5:21: “For as the Father raiseth up the dead, and quickeneth them; even so the Son quickeneth whom he will.” John 11:25, at Bethany, before the raising of Lazarus:

> **“I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live.”**

These are claims of the ministry concerning life. They are not yet the evidential case that God raised Jesus from the dead. They are the identity-claims under which the ministry-raisings occur, and under which His own rising will later be proclaimed.

Luke 7:11–17, at Nain: He touches the bier of a widow’s only son and says, “Young man, I say unto thee, Arise.” The dead sits up and speaks. Fear takes the people; they glorify God; they say, “A great prophet is risen up among us,” and “God hath visited his people.” The crowd’s categories — prophet, visitation — are responses. The act is Jesus’ command to the dead.

Mark 5:21–43 / Luke 8:40–56 / Matthew 9:18–26: Jairus’s daughter. He says, “The damsel is not dead, but sleepeth,” puts out the mourners, takes the parents and three disciples, and says, “Talitha cumi.” She arises. He commands that something be given her to eat. The particularity is the opposite of mythic haze: a named synagogue-ruler, a household, food.

John 11 will be treated among the works. The claim here is the sentence that interprets that work in advance: He is the resurrection and the life. The work exhibits the claim. The claim interprets the work. His own resurrection remains another object.

Matthew 9:18–26 already joined a raising to the forgiveness-and-healing cluster of that chapter. Life-authority is not a late Johannine exclusive. It is Synoptic as well.

Thus:

> **In the public ministry Jesus claims authority over life — to quicken whom He will, to be the resurrection and the life — and exhibits that claim by raising others. The exhibit belongs to this chapter. The case for His own rising belongs to the next.**

---

# 45.18 Healings

The Gospels present healing as a concentrated, public, and interpreted class of works — not as an occasional legend attached to an otherwise ordinary teacher.

Mark 1:29–34 is typical of the form. From the synagogue at Capernaum He enters Simon’s house; Simon’s wife’s mother lies sick of a fever; He takes her by the hand; the fever leaves; she ministers to them. At even, when the sun set, they brought all that were diseased and them that were possessed with devils; all the city was gathered at the door; He healed many. The work is domestic, then civic. It is dated by a Sabbath’s end. It is crowded.

Further instances establish the class by diversity of malady, setting, and attachment to speech.

The leper of Mark 1:40–45 comes kneeling: “If thou wilt, thou canst make me clean.” Jesus, moved with compassion, touches him — a touch the Law would make defiling — and says, “I will; be thou clean.” The leprosy departs. He charges silence and sending to the priest, “for a testimony unto them.” The healing is therefore also a testimony toward the Temple’s own system of declaration. Publicity follows against the charge: the man publishes it; Jesus can no more openly enter the city.

The paralytic of Mark 2 has already been treated as the exhibit of authority to forgive. The same class of work here serves a specified claim. That is the point of the class: healings are not interchangeable marvels. They are attached, in the narratives, to particular claims.

The woman with the issue of blood (Mark 5:25–34) touches His garment in the crowd and is healed. He asks who touched Him, hears her whole truth, and says, “Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole; go in peace, and be whole of thy plague.” Bartimaeus at Jericho (Mark 10:46–52) cries “Son of David,” a messianic address; Jesus calls him; “Thy faith hath made thee whole”; he follows in the way. The ten lepers of Luke 17:11–19; the centurion’s servant of Matthew 8:5–13 / Luke 7:1–10; the nobleman’s son of John 4:46–54; the man born blind of John 9 — these extend the class across distance-healings, Gentile faith, Johannine sign, and Sabbath controversy.

Matthew 11:5 will summarize: the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear. Isaiah’s register is the interpretation. Healing is the kingdom’s arrival in bodies.

Several features of the class must be kept.

First, **compassion is real and is not the whole meaning.** Mark 1:41, Matthew 14:14, and related texts present mercy. Mercy does not exhaust signification. The same works are witness (John 5:36), signs written that the reader may believe (John 20:31), and answers to the messianic question (Matthew 11:2–6).

Second, **faith is often the accompanying human act, and is not the magical technique.** “Thy faith hath made thee whole” locates receptivity. It does not convert Jesus into a current that faith independently taps. He wills; He speaks; He touches; He is the agent.

Third, **hostile observation belongs to the class.** Sabbath healings produce plots. John 9 produces division and expulsion from the synagogue. Healings are public enough to generate official response. That is evidential form, not homiletic colour.

Fourth, **the class is ministry-wide, not a single anecdote.** A unique story can be a legend. A ministry characterized by healing, in multiple independent Gospel streams, in named towns, with named persons, with controversy attached, is a class of the career.

Thus:

> **Healings are public acts of the ministry, diverse in malady and setting, attached to compassion, to faith, to messianic interpretation, and to controversy. They belong to the authentication axis as works that exhibit the kingdom and the authority claimed.**

---

# 45.19 Exorcisms

The Gospels present the expulsion of demons as a distinct class, not as a subset of medical healing. The narratives distinguish fever, leprosy, paralysis, and blindness from unclean spirits. The distinction is the record’s.

Mark 1:21–28 is the first public act of power in Mark after the call of disciples. In the Capernaum synagogue, on the Sabbath, a man with an unclean spirit cries, “Let us alone; what have we to do with thee, thou Jesus of Nazareth? art thou come to destroy us? I know thee who thou art, the Holy One of God.” Jesus rebukes him: “Hold thy peace, and come out of him.” The spirit convulses the man, cries, and comes out. The observers ask, “What thing is this? what new doctrine is this? for with authority commandeth he even the unclean spirits, and they do obey him.” Authority in teaching and authority over spirits are named together. The fame spreads.

The Gerasene (or Gadarene) demoniac of Mark 5:1–20 is public in another key: tombs, chains, a legion, a herd of swine, a city that comes out to see, a man clothed and in his right mind, a plea that Jesus depart, a commission that the man publish in Decapolis what the Lord has done. The work produces civic consequence. It is not a private deliverance absorbed into silence.

Matthew 12:22–32 / Mark 3:22–30 / Luke 11:14–26 is the controlling controversy of the class. A man possessed, blind and dumb, is healed; the people ask whether this is the Son of David. The Pharisees say He casts out demons by Beelzebul, the prince of the demons. Jesus answers that a kingdom divided against itself cannot stand; that if He casts out demons by Beelzebul, by whom do their sons cast them out; and:

> **“But if I cast out devils by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God is come unto you.”**
> — Matthew 12:28

Luke 11:20: “with the finger of God.” The work is interpreted as the arrival of the kingdom. The competing claim — demonic agency — is already in the narrative. The record does not pretend that extraordinary power is self-interpreting. It requires the question of source. Jesus’ answer attaches the exorcisms to the Spirit of God and to the kingdom, and warns against calling the Spirit’s work Satan’s.

The class therefore does three things at once.

First, it exhibits **authority over the powers opposed to God’s reign**, in public, often in synagogue or in open country, with speech as command rather than as rite of technique.

Second, it forces **the question of source**, which is the authentication question. Opponents grant the power and contest the name. That is the difference between spectacle and sign.

Third, it interprets the **kingdom as present**. Exorcism is not a sideshow. It is the claim that God’s rule has arrived in this man’s command.

Thus:

> **Exorcisms are a distinct public class of works, attached to the Holy One / Son of David / kingdom claims, contested as to source, and offered by Jesus as evidence that the kingdom of God has come upon them.**

# 45.20 Nature Signs

A further class concerns wind, water, bread, and the created order’s obedience.

Mark 4:35–41: a storm on the lake; He sleeps; they wake Him; He rebukes the wind and says to the sea, “Peace, be still.” There is a great calm. He asks why they are so fearful. They fear exceedingly and say, “What manner of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?” The question is the authentication question in the mouths of disciples. The work is not explained as coincidence of weather. It is command, and the sea obeys.

Matthew 14:22–33 / Mark 6:45–52 / John 6:16–21: He walks on the sea. In Matthew, Peter comes to Him; they enter the ship; the wind ceases; they worship and say, “Of a truth thou art the Son of God.” The nature-sign is again interpreted as identity.

The feedings — five thousand (Mark 6:30–44 and parallels, including John 6) and four thousand (Mark 8:1–9 / Matthew 15:32–39) — are public in the strongest numerical sense. Counted men, seated companies, remaining baskets, and, in John, a crowd that would take Him by force to make Him king (John 6:15). John 6 then attaches the sign to the claim: “I am the bread of life.” Chapter 44 did not treat that predicate as its assignment. This chapter records it as the interpretation of a nature-sign: the feeding is not a mere welfare event. It signifies the Person as the bread that comes down from heaven. Many then walk no more with Him. Sign and claim together produce division, which is the opposite of a crowd-pleasing magic show.

John 2:1–11, the water become wine at Cana, is named “the beginning of miracles” (signs) that manifested forth His glory, and His disciples believed on Him. The first sign in John is a nature-sign at a wedding, in a named town, with servants who know the source, and with a result stated in authentication language: glory manifested, faith in Him.

The cursing of the fig tree (Mark 11:12–14, 20–21) is a sign of judgment in the week of the Temple-cleansing, not a petty display. It belongs with confrontation rather than with provision, and shows that nature-signs are not all of one mood.

The class as a whole exhibits lordship in the created order: sea, wind, bread, water, tree. Psalm 89:9 and Psalm 107:23–30 ascribe stilling of seas to the LORD. The narratives do not print that cross-reference as a footnote. They print the disciples’ question: what manner of man is this. The authentication axis includes that question as the correct question.

Thus:

> **Nature-signs are public acts in which wind, water, bread, and tree obey His command or serve His purpose, interpreted in the narratives as manifestation of glory, as Sonship, as the bread of life, and as judgment. They are not weather-anecdotes. They are works of identity.**

# 45.21 Teaching with Authority

Matthew 7:28–29 closes the Sermon on the Mount:

> **“And it came to pass, when Jesus had ended these sayings, the people were astonished at his doctrine: for he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.”**

Teaching is here a work. It is public. It produces astonishment. It is contrasted with the scribes. The contrast is not that scribes said nothing true. It is that He taught as one having **ἐξουσία**. Chapter 26 already recorded the distinctive “but I say unto you.” This chapter locates that form as a performed act of the career, belonging to the same authentication axis as healings and signs.

Mark 1:21–22 places the same astonishment in the Capernaum synagogue before the exorcism. Doctrine and command over spirits are one public morning. Luke 4:16–32 joins the Isaiah-fulfilment claim to teaching in Nazareth and to the attempt to cast Him down from the hill. John 7:14–46 presents Temple-teaching at the feast, the question of letters without having learned, the sending-claim, and the officers’ report: “Never man spake like this man.”

Authoritative teaching is therefore not a milder substitute for miracles, offered to modern readers who prefer ethics. It is a work-class of the ministry, public, contested, and attached to the claim of sending and of fulfilment. It gathers crowds. It divides synagogues. It furnishes the substance of the charges against Him. A silent wonder-worker would be a different figure. A mere lecturer without works would be a different figure. The record presents both speech as authority and acts as signs.

Parables belong here as a mode of the work. Mark 4:1–2: He taught them many things by parables. Mark 4:11: “Unto you it is given to know the mystery of the kingdom of God: but unto them that are without, all these things are done in parables.” Teaching is revelation and judgment together: disclosing and concealing according to hearing. That too is an act of authority, not a pedagogical convenience.

Thus:

> **Teaching with authority is a public work of the ministry, contrasted with scribal form, joined to synagogue and Temple, productive of astonishment and of hostility, and belonging to the same axis as the signs. Speech is not less a work than healing.**

---

# 45.22 Table Fellowship

Whom Jesus eats with is a performed claim about the kingdom’s company.

Mark 2:13–17: He calls Levi from the receipt of custom; He sits at meat in Levi’s house with many publicans and sinners; scribes and Pharisees ask the disciples why He eats with them. Jesus answers:

> **“They that are whole have no need of the physician, but they that are sick: I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.”**

The table is the work. The proverb and the mission-statement are the claim that interprets it. Forgiveness-authority and table-fellowship are one architecture: the physician is among the sick; the forgiver is at the sinner’s meal.

Luke 15 locates the same charge — “This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them” — as the occasion of the parables of lost sheep, lost coin, and lost son. The table is again the offence. The parables interpret the offence as heaven’s joy over repentance. Luke 19:1–10: Zacchaeus, a chief publican; Jesus invites Himself to the house; they murmur, “That he was gone to be guest with a man that is a sinner”; salvation comes to the house; “the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost.” Luke 7:36–50, already cited for forgiveness, is a table in a Pharisee’s house at which a sinner is received and a Pharisee is exposed.

John 2’s wedding at Cana is a table of another kind: provision of wine as the beginning of signs. John 6’s feeding is a wilderness table for thousands. The Last Supper is a table that belongs to the passion and is reserved in its sacrificial interpretation for Chapter 46. The present class is the ministry’s ordinary scandal: He eats with the excluded, and names that eating as the physician’s mission and the Son of man’s seeking.

Table fellowship is not magic. It is not a nature-sign. It is a work because it is a public act that enacts a claim and draws the same opposition the healings draw. A reduction that keeps only marvels will miss it. A reduction that keeps only social inclusion will miss the claim that interprets it: He came to call sinners; salvation comes to the house; the Son of man seeks the lost. Inclusion without that claim is a later ethic. The record’s table is the kingdom’s.

Thus:

> **Table fellowship is a public work of identity: the forgiver and physician at the sinner’s meal, interpreted by mission-speech, productive of murmuring, and belonging to the authentication axis as an enacted claim about who may sit in the kingdom.**

---

# 45.23 Confrontations

The ministry includes public collision with scribes, Pharisees, Sadducees, chief priests, and, at the last, the prefect. Confrontation is not a failure of the works. It is a work-class: performed judgment in speech and act.

Matthew 23 is a concentrated instance: woes upon scribes and Pharisees, charges of hypocrisy, of shutting the kingdom, of straining at gnats, of building the tombs of the prophets. The speech is public, in the Temple (Matthew 23:1; cf. 21:23). Luke 11:37–54 locates related woes at a Pharisee’s table. Mark 7:1–13 confronts the tradition of the elders concerning washing and Corban, placing the commandment of God over human tradition. The content of that confrontation will be developed as His treatment of Scripture in Chapter 48. The present point is that He publicly judges the governing religious practices of His setting.

The Temple-cleansing, already treated as a claim of lordship, is confrontation as act. The Sabbath controversies are confrontation as act and speech. The Beelzebul charge is confrontation as contested interpretation of works. John 8 is confrontation about origin, truth, and Abraham. John 9 is confrontation over a sign, ending in expulsion of the healed man and in Jesus’ word about judgment that the blind might see and they that see might be made blind.

Confrontation belongs to the axis because it exhibits the claims under opposition. A career of unopposed marvels would be evidentially weaker, not stronger: it would lack the test of hostile intelligence. The Gospels retain the hostility. They retain questions designed to trap Him (tribute to Caesar, the woman taken in adultery in the Johannine pericope’s traditional form, resurrection and the seven brothers). They retain His answers. They retain the plot to kill. Chapter 43 already noted that controversy is the plot of the ministry. This chapter notes that the plot is itself part of the public work: He does not only heal; He exposes and is exposed.

Thus:

> **Confrontation is a public work of the ministry: woes, Temple-act, Sabbath-collision, contested signs, and answers to traps. It exhibits the claims under opposition and belongs to the authentication axis as performed judgment, not as a regrettable social failure.**

# 45.24 Raising the Dead as Ministry Works

Three raisings in the ministry must be distinguished from Jesus’ own resurrection.

**The widow’s son at Nain** (Luke 7:11–17) is a funeral interrupted on the way to burial, before a crowd, in a named town. He speaks; the dead sits up; the crowd names prophet and visitation; the fame goes forth throughout Judaea.

**Jairus’s daughter** (Mark 5:21–43 and parallels) is a household raising, with named petitioner, delay caused by the woman with the issue of blood, professional mourners, inner witnesses (Peter, James, John, the parents), a command to eat, and a charge that no man should know it. Secrecy here is not the absence of witnesses. It is a charge to witnesses. The work remains particular and located.

**Lazarus of Bethany** (John 11) is the most developed as sign. Bethany is near Jerusalem. Lazarus is named; Mary and Martha are named; the illness is reported; Jesus delays; He says the sickness is for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified thereby. He claims, “I am the resurrection, and the life.” He weeps. He commands the stone away. He prays aloud “because of the people which stand by,” that they may believe the Father sent Him. He cries, “Lazarus, come forth.” The dead comes forth bound. Many of the Jews believe; some go to the Pharisees; the council takes counsel; Caiaphas speaks; from that day they plot to put Him to death. Chapter 36 already used this narrative as a model of event, witness, and consequence. This chapter uses it as a ministry-work attached to an identity-claim, productive of faith and of the plot that leads to the passion.

The three raisings are not His resurrection. They are works in which He acts upon others who have died. They exhibit the claim of John 5:21 and John 11:25. They remain within the public career. They will later be read, by those who proclaim that God raised Him, as consistent with that proclamation. Consistency is not yet Chapter 46’s case. It is already the right placement of the objects.

Thus:

> **Raising the dead in the ministry is a public work-class — Nain, Jairus’s house, Bethany — attached to claims of life-authority and, in John 11, productive of both belief and the death-plot. It authenticates the life-claim of the career. It does not consummate the case for His own rising.**

# 45.25 How Claims Interpret Works

A work does not name itself.

A calmed sea could be luck of weather, if the narrative were only a calm. The narrative is a command and a question: what manner of man. A healed paralytic could be a physician’s success, if the narrative were only walking. The narrative is forgiveness first, and healing “that ye may know.” A crowd fed could be sharing, if the narrative were only bread. John’s narrative is the bread of life. An exorcism could be conceded as power and assigned to Beelzebul. Jesus assigns it to the Spirit of God and to the kingdom’s arrival.

The claims are therefore not ornamental captions. They are the interpretation without which the works are mute or misassigned.

This is the record’s own order in multiple scenes. Mark 2:10 states the purpose-clause before the command to walk. John 11:25–26 states the identity-claim before the cry at the tomb. John 6:35 states the bread-claim after the feeding, as the meaning the crowd missed when they would make Him king by force. Matthew 12:28 states the kingdom-clause as the meaning of the exorcisms. John 5:17 states the Father-clause as the meaning of the Sabbath work.

To refuse the claims while keeping the works is to keep events and discard their offered meaning. That may be attempted. It is not the biblical evidential argument. The biblical argument is that the works mean what the claims say they mean: authority to forgive, relation to the Father, lordship over Sabbath and Temple, messianic identity, the kingdom at hand, authority over life.

Chapter 44’s identification remains the governing name: He is the Truth and the revealer of the Father. The claims-classes of this chapter are the public specification of that identity in the career. The works are the public exhibition. Interpretation runs from claim to work.

Thus:

> **Claims interpret works by supplying the identity, authority, and purpose without which the acts would be unassigned marvels or contested powers. The record’s argument requires the claims as the meaning of the works.**

# 45.26 How Works Interpret Claims

A claim does not exhibit itself in the public field.

“Thy sins be forgiven thee” is inaudible as an interior transaction. The walking paralytic is audible and visible. “I and my Father are one” can be heard as words. The works in the Father’s name are offered as the path to knowing that the Father is in Him (John 10:37–38). “Art thou he that should come?” is a question about identity. The catalogue of the blind, the lame, the lepers, the deaf, the dead, and the poor is the answer given (Matthew 11:4–6). “Believe me that I am in the Father, and the Father in me: or else believe me for the very works’ sake” (John 14:11) is the farewell’s own statement of the bearing of works upon the identity-claim Chapter 44 examined.

John 5:36:

> **“But I have greater witness than that of John: for the works which the Father hath given me to finish, the same works that I do, bear witness of me, that the Father hath sent me.”**

The works are witness. Witness is an evidential term. They bear upon the sending-claim. John 10:25: the works in the Father’s name bear witness of Him. Hebrews 2:3–4, already used in Movement V as warrant-form, names the Lord’s speaking as confirmed by them that heard, God also bearing witness with signs, wonders, divers miracles, and gifts of the Holy Ghost. The present use is not the apostolic-authority file. It is the same authentication logic applied to the career: speech confirmed by divine works.

Works interpret claims in a further sense: they specify the kind of Messiah, the kind of kingdom, the kind of lordship. A messianic claim attached only to force would be a different claim. Attached to the blind seeing and the poor hearing gospel, it is Isaiah’s anointed. A kingdom-claim attached only to timetable would be a different claim. Attached to exorcism “by the finger of God,” it is present irruption. A forgiveness-claim attached only to words would be assertion. Attached to the risen paralytic, it is exhibited authority.

The works therefore do not prove a different identity from the one claimed. They exhibit that identity in the created and social order. Interpretation runs from work to claim as confirmation, specification, and public witness.

Thus:

> **Works interpret claims by exhibiting in bodies, seas, tables, and contested holy places the authority asserted in speech. The record’s argument requires the works as witness to the claims.**

# 45.27 The Pattern: Claim, Challenge, Work, Interpretation

Chapter 26 already named the pattern in the paralytic narrative. This chapter now states it as the recurring authentication form of the ministry.

**Claim.** Jesus asserts authority or identity: forgiveness, Sabbath lordship, relation to the Father, messianic office, kingdom, life.

**Challenge.** Hearers name the implication, object, accuse of blasphemy, assign the power to Beelzebul, demand a sign, or plot. The challenge is not an intrusion upon an otherwise smooth hagiography. It is the recognition that something has been claimed which cannot be ordinary.

**Work.** A public act follows, or has just occurred, of a kind corresponding to the claim: the paralytic walks; the hand is restored on the Sabbath; Lazarus comes forth; the storm stills; demons depart.

**Interpretation.** The narrative, or Jesus’ own speech, states what the work is for: “that ye may know”; “the kingdom of God is come unto you”; “believe the works”; “this day is this scripture fulfilled”; “these are written, that ye might believe.”

The four moments are not always in the same temporal order. Sometimes the work precedes the discourse that interprets it (John 5; John 9). Sometimes the claim precedes the work (Mark 2). Sometimes a question from outside elicits both (Matthew 11:2–6). The form is nevertheless one form: assertion, contest, exhibit, meaning.

This form is the biblical evidential argument in miniature. It is not a modern four-step apologetic imposed on the text. It is the way the scenes are built. The investigator who reads only claims has stopped at moment one. The investigator who reads only works has stopped at moment three. The investigator who reads the four together is reading the Gospels as they argue.

Thus:

> **The recurring authentication form of the ministry is claim, challenge, work, and interpretation. That form is the interior structure of the axis this chapter names.**

# 45.28 Matthew 11:2–6 as a Controlling Test

John the Baptist, in prison, sends disciples:

> **“Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another?”**
> — Matthew 11:3

The question is the messianic question, asked by the historical marker Chapter 43 already located, at a moment of pressure rather than of triumphal certainty. Jesus answers:

> **“Go and shew John again those things which ye do hear and see: the blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have the gospel preached to them. And blessed is he, whosoever shall not be offended in me.”**
> — Matthew 11:4–6

Several features make this passage controlling for the chapter.

First, **the question is identity.** “He that should come” is the expected one. The works are not offered as a distraction from identity. They are offered as the answer to identity.

Second, **the answer is a conjunction of works and word.** Five classes of bodily restoration, then gospel to the poor. Teaching is inside the catalogue. The authentication axis is not miracles as opposed to preaching. It is works and gospel together as the messianic exhibit.

Third, **the catalogue is in Isaiah’s key** (Isaiah 35:5–6; 61:1). The interpretation of the works is scriptural without this chapter’s completing Chapter 48. The present point is that Jesus points to what is heard and seen as the fulfilment-shaped answer. The Baptist is to receive testimony of public events, not a private reassurance.

Fourth, **offence is contemplated.** “Blessed is he, whosoever shall not be offended in me.” The works do not coerce assent. They confront. John 6 and John 11 likewise produce both faith and departure or plot. An authentication axis that left no room for offence would not be this one.

Fifth, **the passage stands beside Matthew 11:20–24** (woes on cities where mighty works were done) and **Matthew 11:25–27** (the revelatory claim Chapter 44 treated). Works, judgment on unrepentant cities, and unique revelation of the Father stand in one chapter of Matthew. The Synoptic tradition therefore holds together the axis of this chapter and the identification of the last.

Thus:

> **Matthew 11:2–6 is the ministry’s own controlling test: messianic identity is to be read off what is heard and seen — healings, raising of the dead, gospel to the poor — with the possibility of offence remaining real. The axis is Jesus’ own offered grounds.**

# 45.29 Acts 2:22 as Public Summary

The first Christian sermon in Jerusalem, addressed to the men of Israel, summarizes the career in evidential terms:

> **“Ye men of Israel, hear these words; Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved of God among you by miracles and wonders and signs, which God did by him in the midst of you, as ye yourselves also know.”**
> — Acts 2:22

The sentence is a summary, not a miracle-list. Its force for this chapter is structural.

**“Jesus of Nazareth”** keeps the located man of Chapter 43.

**“A man approved of God”** names the authentication question: approval, not mere celebrity.

**“Among you” / “in the midst of you” / “as ye yourselves also know”** names publicity. The works are appealed to as known in the city where the speech is given, not as distant rumours.

**“Miracles and wonders and signs”** — δυνάμεσι καὶ τέρασι καὶ σημείοις — is the Chapter 36 triad, now applied to this man’s career as the ground of God’s approval.

**“Which God did by him”** assigns agency. The works are God’s works done through Jesus. That assignment matches John 5 and John 10: the Father’s works, the works in the Father’s name.

Acts 2:22 then continues into the determinate counsel of God, crucifixion, and resurrection. Those clauses are the next chapter’s object. The present clause is the public career as already known and as already interpreted as divine approval by works. The sermon does not begin with private experience. It begins with a man, a city, and known signs.

Acts 10:38, to the household of Cornelius, is the same summary in another register:

> **“How God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Ghost and with power: who went about doing good, and healing all that were oppressed of the devil; for God was with him.”**

Anointing, power, doing good, healing, oppression of the devil, “God was with him”: messianic anointing, the healing class, the exorcism class, and the Father-relation in a clause. The earliest preaching, as Acts presents it, already treats the claims-and-works conjunction as the public career’s meaning.

Thus:

> **Acts 2:22 and 10:38 summarize the ministry as God’s public approval of Jesus of Nazareth by known works. The apostolic proclamation, before it reaches the resurrection-clause, already treats the career as an authentication axis.**

# 45.30 John’s Signs and Their Stated Purpose

John’s Gospel names the works **signs** and states their purpose:

> **“And many other signs truly did Jesus in the presence of his disciples, which are not written in this book: but these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name.”**
> — John 20:30–31

The statement is explicit about several matters this chapter requires.

**Selectivity.** Many signs are unwritten. The written ones are a chosen file. The authentication axis is not an exhaustive marvel-catalogue. It is a sufficient public testimony toward an identity-conclusion.

**Presence of disciples.** The signs were done in their presence. Witness is attached. The dedicated eyewitness file remains Chapter 47. The present point is John’s insistence that the signs were not unobserved.

**Purpose.** The signs are written in order that the reader may believe a specified identity: that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God. Sign is for messianic and filial identity. Life through His name is the consequence of that belief, matching Chapter 44’s cluster of truth, life, and access to the Father.

**Christ and Son of God.** The purpose-clause joins the messianic office-claim and the relation-to-the-Father claim. John’s signs are not a different religion from the Synoptic kingdom. They are the same identity in sign-idiom.

The signs John does write — Cana, the nobleman’s son, Bethesda, the feeding, the sea, the man born blind, Lazarus, and, in the purpose-structure of the book, the resurrection appearances which Chapter 46 must treat — are each attached to discourse. Water to wine manifests glory. Bethesda yields the Father-discourse. The feeding yields the bread of life. The blind man yields the light of the world and the judgment of seeing. Lazarus yields “I am the resurrection and the life.” Sign and claim are never far apart in John. That is the conjunction as a literary and evidential architecture.

Thus:

> **John’s stated purpose for the signs is belief that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God. The Fourth Gospel is not a later spiritualization that abandons works. It is the most explicit statement that works are for identity.**

# 45.31 How This Relates to Movement V Without Circularity

Movement V warranted the writings as authentic written testimony of the claims under investigation: genuine, recoverable, historically embedded, internally coherent testimony of claimed divine communication and historical acts. That warrant did not yet isolate the claims-and-works conjunction of Jesus as Movement VI’s authentication axis. This chapter does that isolation.

The operation is not circular.

It would be circular if the chapter argued: the Gospels are inspired Scripture in the finished Movement VIII sense; therefore whatever they report of claims and works is true; therefore the claims and works authenticate Him; therefore the Gospels are inspired. That chain is refused. Inspiration’s guarantees, canon, and Graphē as primary written authority remain later.

It would be circular if the chapter argued: His works prove the writings; the writings prove the works; therefore both. The writings are already warranted at Movement V’s level as authentic testimony. The Person is already located. The identity as Truth is already examined. This chapter asks what that testimony presents as the public career’s own authentication structure. The conclusion is a conclusion about the content and function of the testimony, attached to a historical career, not a second warrant for the existence of the documents.

The relation to Movement V is therefore deepening, not replacement, and not theft. Chapter 36 established the category of sign and the concentration in Jesus. Chapter 38 noted Christological concentration as coherence. Chapter 42 turned the investigator toward Christ as central witness. Chapters 43–44 located and identified Him. This chapter exhibits the axis by which the testimony itself presents His ministry as approved: claims interpreted by works, works interpreted by claims. Later light on resurrection and eyewitnesses will be light on an object now in hand as a career, not the delayed arrival of the career.

Thus:

> **The claims-and-works axis is read from writings already warranted as testimony, about a Person already located and identified. The reading does not assume Graphē-gate authority, and it does not pretend to be the resurrection case. It is the next earned operation of Movement VI.**

# 45.32 Competing Claim: Assertion Alone Suffices

The reduction says:

> **If Jesus claimed these things, the claims are self-certifying. Works are optional illustration. Identity is established by speech.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **the record refuses it.** Mark 2:10 exists because saying “forgiven” is the easier sentence if sentences sufficed. The work is given “that ye may know.” John 10:37 exists because He Himself says: if I do not the works of my Father, believe me not. John 14:11 offers the works as grounds of belief alongside the words. The speaker of the claims does not treat the claims as self-certifying.

Second, **challenge in the narratives is the recognition that claims of this height require more than utterance.** Blasphemy-charges, Beelzebul-charges, demands for signs, and plots are the cost of the claims. A self-certifying slogan would not generate that structure.

Third, **Movement I already distinguished assertion from truth.** A claim is a claim. Truth is correspondence to reality. The authentication axis exists because the record itself will not collapse those two.

Fourth, **the reduction cannot explain why the Gospels are so full of works.** If speech sufficed, the nature-signs, the raisings, the Sabbath healings, and the stated purposes of signs would be excess. They are not treated as excess. They are treated as witness.

The chapter therefore states with full force that claims are essential — they supply meaning — and states with equal force that claims are not, in the biblical argument, proof by assertion alone.

The reduction fails.

# 45.33 Competing Claim: Work as Magic Spectacle

The reduction says:

> **The works are displays of power, impressive or legendary, detached from identity. They are magic, thaumaturgy, or wonder for wonder’s sake. The claims are later captions.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **Chapter 36 already distinguished miracle from magic.** Magic as technique, control, and spectacle detached from the God of the record is not the Gospel form. Jesus refuses signs on demand (Matthew 12:38–39; Mark 8:11–12). He silences demons who speak His identity. He withdraws from crowds that would make Him king because they ate bread (John 6:15, 26). A magician’s career is not structured by refusal of spectacle.

Second, **the works are interpreted before, during, or immediately after by claims of forgiveness, Father, kingdom, Messiah, and life.** Detachment is an operation the reader performs against the narratives, not an operation the narratives perform.

Third, **the Beelzebul controversy shows that power without right source-assignment is already a live option in the story, and is refused.** The record is not naïve about wonder. It contests wonder’s meaning.

Fourth, **table fellowship and confrontation are works in this chapter’s definition.** They are not thaumaturgy. A magic-reduction that can be made, with strain, against a stilling of the sea cannot be made against eating with Levi. The class of works is broader than marvel. The reduction explains at most a subset and cancels the rest.

Fifth, **John 20:30–31 states the purpose of the written signs: identity, not entertainment.** Matthew 11:4–6 states the purpose of the heard-and-seen things: the coming One, not a show.

The works are real as claimed public acts of the testimony. They are not magic spectacle detached from identity. The reduction fails.

# 45.34 Competing Claim: Mere Social or Temple Reformer

The reduction says:

> **Jesus was a critic of table-exclusion and of Temple-corruption, a reformer of Israel’s piety. Forgiveness-speech, Father-speech, Sabbath-lordship, and signs are later inflation of a prophet of inclusion and purity.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **table and Temple are real strands and are not denied.** Levi’s house and the cleansing belong to the career. The reduction takes them as the ceiling. The record does not.

Second, **the table is interpreted by forgiveness, physician, and Son-of-man seeking the lost**, not by a general ethic of hospitality. The Temple-act is interpreted by house-of-prayer, by “greater than the temple,” and by a saying about destroying and raising the temple. Reform does not reach those clauses.

Third, **the forgiveness-claim in Mark 2 is not a reform of sacrificial bureaucracy.** It is authority on earth to forgive, named as God’s prerogative by the scribes, exhibited by a healing. A reformer might urge repentance and send a man to the priest. This man speaks forgiveness and commands walking.

Fourth, **Matthew 11:27 and John 10:30 and John 5:18** are not the idiom of a purity-reformer. Unique revelation of the Father, oneness with the Father, and the opposition’s charge of making Himself equal with God exceed reform.

Fifth, **the date of high claims is not late enough for the reduction’s convenience.** Paul’s churches in the 50s already live inside the identity Chapter 44 established. Acts 2:22 already summarizes the career by signs as divine approval. A reformer inflated into Son and sign-worker within two or three decades, among those who knew James and the pillars, is the same pace-problem Chapter 44 named against projection.

Jesus reformed nothing if reform means only improved casuistry. He judged table and Temple. He is not merely a reformer. The reduction fails.

# 45.35 Competing Claim: Legendary Inflation of Claims and Works

The reduction says:

> **A historical teacher existed. The claims of forgiveness, Father, Sabbath-lordship, and judgment, and the works of healing, exorcism, nature, and raising, accrued as legend. The conjunction is a literary product of decades.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **Chapter 43 already destroyed the vacuum and the coreless legend.** A located man, a short public career, crucifixion under Pilate, a brother named James, a movement in the 50s — these remain. Inflation upon a core must then explain the content, the date, and the form.

Second, **the form is not the ordinary form of legend.** Legend typically smooths, removes friction, and accumulates wonders without the Beelzebul debate, without the charge to silence, without the Baptist’s doubt, without disciples’ fear and misunderstanding, without offence contemplated in the beatitude of Matthew 11:6. The Gospels retain challenge as a structural moment. Inflation that produces Mark 2 would ordinarily omit the scribes’ interior reasoning or the “easier to say” question. The authentication pattern is too argumentative to be the ordinary legendary drift.

Third, **multiple streams carry the conjunction.** Synoptic healings, exorcisms, nature-signs, table, Temple, and authority-to-forgive; Johannine signs with discourses; Acts-summaries; Pauline identity already high in the 50s. Independent idiom with the same architecture is the same evidential fact Chapter 44 used. Legendary inflation must then be multiple, early, and coincident.

Fourth, **hostility as a source of preservation works against free inflation.** Works and claims that generate plots, blasphemy-charges, and execution are not the easiest legendary gifts. They are the career’s crisis. Chapter 43 already used the shame of crucifixion as historical weight. The claims that lead to that shame are of a piece with it.

Fifth, **Movement V already warranted the writings as authentic testimony, not as late folklore.** Local questions of how a particular scene was remembered remain legitimate. They are not a global solvent of the conjunction.

The community remembered, preached, and wrote. Memory is not the same act as the creation of a wonder-worker and a forgiver of sins out of a mild reformer within a generation. The reduction fails as an explanation of the first-century conjunction.

# 45.36 Competing Claim: Natural or Psychological Reduction of the Conjunction

The reduction says:

> **Healings were psychosomatic; exorcisms were the calming of hysteria; nature-signs were misremembered weather and sharing of lunches; raisings were recoveries from coma; claims were the teacher’s sincerity or the disciples’ later faith. No authentication axis remains.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **it must be applied uniformly across a diverse class, and the class resists uniformity.** A fever in Simon’s house is one kind of object. A man born blind in John 9 is another. A storm commanded is another. Lazarus four days in the tomb is another. Five thousand fed with twelve baskets remaining is another. The reduction changes instrument with each instance and then claims to have explained the conjunction. A family of ad hoc explanations is not one explanation.

Second, **the narratives include features that the reduction must delete.** The paralytic’s bed carried through the streets; the leper sent to the priest; the swine in Mark 5; the man born blind whose neighbours debate identity; Lazarus bound in graveclothes; remaining fragments of bread counted. Particularity is not proof by itself. Systematic deletion of particularity is the reduction’s method, and it is circular: whatever would resist naturalization is declared legendary in advance.

Third, **psychological sincerity does not reach the claim-classes.** “Thy sins be forgiven thee,” “I and my Father are one,” “the Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath,” “the Father hath committed all judgment unto the Son” — these are not sincerity. Chapter 44 already refused sincerity as a definition of truth. This chapter refuses it as a definition of the claims.

Fourth, **hostile observers and official consequence remain.** A psychosomatic fashion does not by itself explain a plot involving chief priests and a Roman prefect, or a Sabbath coalition of Pharisees and Herodians. The career’s end is public execution. The reduction of the works must still explain the crisis of the claims.

Fifth, **the reduction offers no account of the authentication pattern as argument.** Claim, challenge, work, interpretation is a reasoned form. Naturalization explains (at most) isolated events by dissolving them. It does not explain why the testimony is built as an evidential argument.

Some healings in the world’s ordinary course are recoveries. That fact does not become a solvent of this ministry’s reported conjunction. The live question is whether the testimony’s conjunction is better explained as authentic public career of the identified Person, or as a heap of misrememberings later captioned with divine titles. The heap fails the diversity, the dates, the hostility, and the argumentative form.

The reduction fails.

# 45.37 Reason Through the Evidence

The reasoning may now be stated so that it can be inspected.

**Step 1.** The biblical writings are warranted as authentic written testimony of the claims under investigation (Movement V). The Person exists in first-century history (Chapter 43). He is identified as the Truth and the decisive revealer of the Father (Chapter 44). Those are earned premises.

**Step 2.** A claim is a public assertion of identity or authority. A work is a public act of the ministry. Neither is, by itself, the biblical authentication argument. The argument is their conjunction.

**Step 3.** The testimony presents specified claim-classes in context: authority to forgive sins; unique relation to the Father; lordship over Sabbath, Law, and Temple; eschatological judgment; messianic identity; the kingdom at hand; authority over life in the ministry.

**Step 4.** The testimony presents specified work-classes belonging to the same career: healings; exorcisms; nature-signs; teaching with authority; table fellowship; confrontations; raising the dead in the ministry.

**Step 5.** The testimony itself interprets each by the other. Claims supply meaning to works. Works supply public witness to claims. The recurring form is claim, challenge, work, interpretation.

**Step 6.** Matthew 11:2–6 offers what is heard and seen as the answer to the messianic question. John 10:25, 37–38 and John 14:11 offer the works as witness to the Father-relation. John 20:30–31 states that signs are written that the reader may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God. Acts 2:22 and 10:38 summarize the career as God’s public approval by known works.

**Step 7.** The live reductions — assertion as self-certifying; works as magic spectacle; mere reformer; legendary inflation; natural or psychological dissolution — fail as accounts of this conjunction. They explain a strand and cancel the structure.

**Step 8.** Therefore the investigator is warranted, with full force, in concluding that the authentic testimony presents the claims and works of Jesus Christ as a central authentication axis of the public career, and that this presentation is the next established operation of Movement VI.

The form of reasoning is the same form the book has used: abductive historical and textual warrant. The writings say this. They are authentic testimony. The Person exists and has been identified. The reductions fail. The axis follows. It is not Euclidean demonstration. It is not impression. It is the exhibited bearing of the record upon the proposition.

# 45.38 Explicit Versus Inferred

The distinction is mandatory.

**Explicit in the biblical record:**

- “Son, thy sins be forgiven thee.” “That ye may know that the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins” (Mark 2:5, 10).
- “Who can forgive sins but God only?” as the opponents’ named implication (Mark 2:7).
- “My Father worketh hitherto, and I work.” The narrator’s statement that He said God was His Father, making Himself equal with God (John 5:17–18).
- The works given by the Father bear witness that the Father sent Him (John 5:36).
- “The works that I do in my Father’s name, they bear witness of me.” “I and my Father are one.” “Believe the works: that ye may know … that the Father is in me, and I in him” (John 10:25, 30, 37–38).
- “The Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath” (Mark 2:28).
- “I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil.” “But I say unto you.” “In this place is one greater than the temple” (Matthew 5:17, 22; 12:6).
- The Father has committed all judgment unto the Son (John 5:22, 27). The Son of man on the throne of glory judging the nations (Matthew 25:31–46).
- Isaiah 61 read and “This day is this scripture fulfilled” (Luke 4:16–21). “Thou art the Christ” received (Mark 8:29). “I that speak unto thee am he” (John 4:26). “Art thou the Christ …? I am” (Mark 14:61–62).
- “The kingdom of God is at hand” (Mark 1:15). “If I cast out devils by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God is come unto you” (Matthew 12:28).
- “I am the resurrection, and the life” (John 11:25). Ministry raisings at Nain, at Jairus’s house, at Bethany.
- Healings, exorcisms, stilling of the sea, feedings, Cana, teaching with authority not as the scribes, tables with publicans, Temple-cleansing, woes and controversies — as narrative events of the ministry.
- “Go and shew John again those things which ye do hear and see…” (Matthew 11:4–6).
- “A man approved of God among you by miracles and wonders and signs, which God did by him in the midst of you, as ye yourselves also know” (Acts 2:22).
- “These are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God” (John 20:31).
- “Believe me for the very works’ sake” (John 14:11).

**Inferred, and marked as inferred:**

- That “authentication axis” is an accurate conceptual name for the conjunction of these explicit claims and works in the record’s own purpose-clauses and summaries. The phrase is the chapter’s. The conjunction is the record’s.
- That the claim-classes and work-classes named above are the major classes of the ministry rather than an arbitrary modern list. The grouping is an inference from recurrence and from the summaries (Matthew 11; Acts 2; Acts 10; John 20).
- That claim, challenge, work, and interpretation is one recurring form. The four-moment name is the chapter’s. The scenes exhibit it.
- That, given Movement V’s warrant and Chapters 43–44, the investigator is entitled to treat this conjunction as the authentic testimony concerning the historical Person’s public career, not as late folklore. That entitlement is an inference from prior chapters plus the contents now examined.
- That the reductions fail as global explanations. Failure of a rival is an inferential conclusion from mismatch with the specified claims, works, dates, and argumentative form.

**Not inferred here, because reserved:**

- That God raised Jesus from the dead as a completed evidential demonstration (Chapter 46).
- That the eyewitness file is closed (Chapter 47).
- That He confirms Scripture as Graphē-doctrine (Chapter 48).
- That apostolic testimony carries His authority as a finished argument (Chapters 49–50).
- That the Graphē is primary written authority; that Scripture cannot be broken; that “thy word is truth” is the finished Graphē-gate (Movement VIII).

The reader can therefore see which sentences are the Bible’s and which are the chapter’s reasoning from the Bible. The warranted conclusion in the next section is drawn from the explicit claims and works plus the marked inferences, not from the reserved files.

# 45.39 The Warranted Conclusion Stated with Full Force

The investigator is rationally warranted, with full force, in holding that:

> **Jesus Christ, the historical Person already established and already identified as the Truth and the revealer of the Father, is presented by the authentic written testimony as one whose public claims and public works constitute a central authentication axis of the ministry. He claimed authority to forgive sins, a unique relation to the Father, lordship over Sabbath, Law, and Temple, eschatological judgment, messianic identity, the kingdom of God as at hand in His presence, and authority over life. He healed, expelled demons, commanded nature, taught with authority, kept table with sinners, confronted the governing pieties of His setting, and raised the dead in the public career. Claims interpret works. Works interpret claims. The recurring form is claim, challenge, work, and interpretation. Matthew 11:2–6, John 10:25–38, John 14:11, John 20:30–31, and Acts 2:22 are the record’s own statements of this bearing. The live reductions fail. This is the authentic testimony’s positive case concerning what He claimed and did, and how those claims and works function in the biblical evidential argument.**

The conclusion is not a list of slogans. It is not a marvel-catalogue. It is not a later council’s vocabulary imposed backward. It is the career as the Gospels and the Jerusalem sermon present it, now examined in its classes and its form, attached to a man who occupied the reign of Tiberius, and warranted as the content of authentic testimony.

The conclusion is not hostage to Chapter 46. Resurrection will bear upon this axis with decisive force. The axis is already the ministry’s own offered grounds. Later light is light on an object in hand. It is not the delayed arrival of the object.

A mere asserter has not been established as the ceiling of the file. A mere wonder-worker has not been established as the ceiling of the file. The ceiling the record names is the conjunction: the One who is the Truth, claiming and acting in public so that the works bear witness of Him, and the claims name what the works mean.

That is the verdict of this chapter. It is full force for this stage.

# 45.40 The Scope of This Stage

This is the scope statement for the authentication axis. It is sequence and limitation of level, not a withdrawal of force.

By sequence, later chapters examine:

- the death and resurrection as the central historical claims of the passion and the apostolic proclamation (Chapter 46);
- the eyewitness testimony, including 2 Peter 1:16 and related apostolic testimony (Chapter 47);

- John 10:34–36, “the scripture cannot be broken,” “Thy word is truth” as Graphē-doctrine, and why the Graphē is the primary written authority (Movement VIII);
- mishandling, tergiversation, and ἄπειρος (*apeiros*, G552) (Movement IX);
- the scholarly labour of the Graphē, including σπουδάζω (*spoudazō*, G4704), and readiness for the Six First Principles (Movement X).

Those questions remain later because they are different questions. The present conclusion is already in force for its level.

This chapter has not proved the resurrection. It has not treated the eyewitness file as complete. It has not established His confirmation of Scripture as a doctrine of the Graphē. It has not established apostolic authority as a finished argument. It has not claimed that the Graphē is primary written authority in the Movement VIII sense. It has not treated John 10:34–36 as finished. It has not entered the First Principles. It has used John 10’s works-argument and left John 10’s Scripture-argument in its place.

It has established that the authentic testimony presents the claims and works of Jesus Christ as a public conjunction that functions as a central authentication axis of Movement VI, and that this presentation is warranted for the historical Person already located and already identified as the Truth.

One scope statement is enough. The force of the axis is not diminished by the existence of later chapters.

# 45.41 Apply the Standard

The reader should now be able to take any proposed sentence about “what Jesus claimed” or “what Jesus did” and ask:

> **Is this sentence a claim of historical existence, a claim of identity with the Truth and decisive revelation, a claims-and-works authentication, a passion-and-resurrection case, or a reserved file of eyewitness, Scripture-confirmation, or apostolic authority?**

Then:

> **If it is a claims-and-works sentence, what class of claim or work is it, in what context, attached to what interpretation, and does the record join it to the other pole of the conjunction?**

Then:

> **Does the proposed reduction explain Mark 2, Matthew 11:2–6, John 10:25–38, John 20:30–31, and Acts 2:22 together, or only a preferred strand?**

Then:

> **Is the conclusion being postponed because later chapters remain, or is it being drawn at the level the grounds support?**

If the grounds are the authentic testimony’s explicit claims and works, mutually interpreting, attached to the historical Person already identified as the Truth, with the reductions failing, the authentication axis is to be granted with full force.

If a later conclusion is desired — that God raised Him, that eyewitnesses of His majesty are decisive, that He confirms Scripture, that apostolic testimony carries His authority — it is to be sought in its chapter, not smuggled into this axis and not used to postpone this axis.

If a catalogue of “the miracles of Jesus” mixes nature-signs with table-fellowship without interpretation, or a catalogue of “the titles of Jesus” mixes Messiah with later ecclesiastical slogans without context, the catalogue is to be classified before it is allowed to speak of authentication. Classification is the discipline of the whole book.

The governing maxim remains:

> **Do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture. Teach the reader to reason through Scripture.**

Reasoning through the writings at this stage, concerning the Person already located and identified, yields the claims-and-works conjunction as a central authentication axis of Movement VI. That is not the abandonment of historical method. It is the refusal to separate what the record joins, and the refusal to call that joining a postponement.

# 45.42 From Claims and Works to Death and Resurrection

A public career of this height does not end in a quiet retirement. The same testimony that presents the claims and works presents a death under Pontius Pilate and a proclamation that God raised Him from the dead.

The plot that follows Lazarus, the council’s counsel, the Temple-act, the hearing before the high priest, the delivery to Pilate, and the crucifixion already named as historical baseline are not a separate biography accidentally attached to a wonder-worker. They are the crisis of the claims. A man who forgives sins, names God His Father, judges the Temple, and receives messianic confession is the kind of man who can be tried for blasphemy and executed as a king. Chapter 43 located the execution. This chapter has shown why the career runs toward that execution as its interior logic, not as a misfortune.

The other pole remains. The ministry includes His own predictions of suffering, death, and rising (Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34 and parallels). Those predictions are claims of the career. The empty tomb, the appearances, and the apostolic proclamation that God raised Him are the next necessary object. Acts 2:22 already stood on the edge of that object: the man approved by works is the man taken, crucified, and raised. John 2:19 already stood on the edge of it: destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up. John 11:25 already named Him the resurrection and the life before He Himself entered death.

Those edges are not the case. The case is the next chapter.

The question is therefore not:

> **Did the authentic testimony present His claims and works as an authentication axis?**

That question has been answered.

The question is:

> **What are the central historical claims concerning His death and resurrection, and how do those claims bear upon apostolic testimony and upon the identity and axis now in hand?**

That question is not a retreat from the ministry. It is the crisis and the claimed reversal of the ministry. A Messiah crucified is a scandal. A crucified Messiah raised is a different public fact. Movement VI cannot stop at Capernaum and Bethany. It must go to Golgotha and to the first day of the week.

The investigation therefore moves from:

> **He claimed and He worked, and the conjunction authenticates the career as the testimony presents it.**

to:

> **He was crucified, and God — the testimony claims — raised Him from the dead.**

That is the proper open of the next chapter of Movement VI. The axis was never meant to be the last word. It was meant to be a word sufficient to justify taking the next one, which is the word the apostles themselves placed at the center.
