# Chapter 84 — The Discipline of Biblical Interpretation

## Movement X — The Scholarly Labour of the Graphē

### Central Objective

Bring together perception, language, context, comparison, inference, reasoning, and disciplined handling of Scripture.

---

## 84.1 The Question After the Workman

Chapter 83 established the worker.

The workman is responsible.

He is accountable to God.

He works with the Word of Truth.

He is not the source of the Word.

He is not the authority over the Word.

He is not infallible merely because he is skilled.

He is called to diligent and faithful workmanship.

But one question remains.

The workman has been identified.

The object of his work has been identified.

The responsibility has been identified.

What remains is the discipline by which the work is actually performed.

This is the subject of the present chapter.

The question is not merely:

> **Who interprets?**

It is:

> **How does the interpreter discipline the act of interpretation so that the Word remains the authority and the conclusion does not outrun the evidence?**

That question brings together the labour of Movement X.

---

## 84.2 Interpretation Is Not a Single Mental Act

The word **interpretation** can make the task sound simpler than it is.

A reader sees words.

He recognizes language.

He identifies propositions.

He considers context.

He asks what the words mean.

He follows the argument.

He compares related statements.

He distinguishes explicit statements from inference.

He evaluates alternative explanations.

He reaches a conclusion.

He may then apply the conclusion.

These are related operations, but they are not identical.

Therefore a disciplined interpreter should not collapse them into one undefined act called:

> “What I think the verse means.”

That sentence can conceal many steps.

The workman must make the steps visible.

---

## 84.3 The Master Interpretive Sequence

The preceding chapters provide the material for a governing sequence:

> **Perceive → Learn → Understand → Reason → Compare → Distinguish → Infer → Test → Conclude → Apply.**

This sequence should not be treated as a mechanical formula.

It is a discipline of intellectual order.

Perception asks what is there.

Learning receives it.

Understanding asks what it means.

Reasoning examines relationships.

Comparison places statements beside related evidence.

Distinction prevents categories from being collapsed.

Inference identifies what follows.

Testing examines the conclusion.

Conclusion states what is warranted.

Application asks what the established meaning requires in a particular circumstance.

The sequence protects the source from the interpreter's haste.

---

## 84.4 Interpretation Begins With What Is Written

The first discipline is simple:

> **Begin with the text.**

Not with a doctrine.

Not with a favorite commentary.

Not with an inherited interpretation.

Not with a theological system.

Not with an experience.

Not with a conclusion already chosen.

Not with a controversy.

Not with the question:

> “How can I make this verse support my position?”

The workman begins with:

> **What is written?**

This is not an elementary question beneath scholarship.

It is the foundation of scholarship.

---

## 84.5 The Text Before the Theory

A theory can become a lens through which every passage is forced to speak.

The interpreter then sees his system everywhere.

A passage that appears to support the system is treated as confirmation.

A passage that creates difficulty is reinterpreted.

A passage that contradicts the system is explained away.

Eventually the theory determines the reading.

The order has been reversed.

The disciplined workman therefore permits the text to challenge the theory.

He asks:

> **If my interpretation is wrong, what would this passage expose?**

That question creates intellectual openness.

---

## 84.6 Interpretation Is Not the Manufacture of Meaning

The interpreter does not manufacture meaning.

The text communicates.

The interpreter seeks to understand.

This distinction is fundamental.

A person can misunderstand.

A person can overinterpret.

A person can underinterpret.

A person can misapply.

But these failures do not mean the interpreter creates the original meaning.

Meaning belongs to the communication being interpreted.

The task is to recover, understand, and articulate what the communication establishes.

---

## 84.7 The Interpreter Is Not a Co-Author

The workman is not completing Scripture.

He is not adding missing revelation.

He is not improving the author's argument.

He is not correcting the text according to modern preference.

He is not rewriting the proposition into a form he finds more acceptable.

Interpretation is therefore not co-authorship.

The interpreter remains downstream from the source.

The source speaks.

The worker listens.

The worker analyzes.

The worker explains.

That order must remain intact.

---

## 84.8 Perception Comes Before Interpretation

The first practical difficulty is often perception.

A reader cannot interpret what he has failed to notice.

He may overlook:

- a negative;
- a condition;
- a contrast;
- a repeated term;
- a connective;
- a question;
- an answer;
- a change of speaker;
- a change of audience;
- a temporal marker;
- a qualification;
- or the conclusion of an argument.

The resulting interpretation may therefore be wrong before sophisticated reasoning even begins.

The workman must learn to see.

---

## 84.9 Perception Is Not Yet Understanding

Seeing the words is not identical to understanding them.

A reader can accurately quote:

> “For…”

without knowing what the “for” explains.

He can see:

> “therefore…”

without identifying the conclusion it introduces.

He can recognize:

> “if…”

without noticing the condition that follows.

Perception supplies the material.

Understanding begins to grasp the relationships.

The workman must therefore resist the illusion that reading words is the same as interpreting them.

---

## 84.10 Reading Slowly Enough to See

The scholarly reader must sometimes slow down.

A familiar passage encourages rapid recognition.

The reader sees the words and immediately remembers the traditional interpretation.

That familiarity can conceal what is actually written.

Slowing down allows the worker to ask:

> What exactly is the sentence saying?

> What is the subject?

> What is the predicate?

> What modifies what?

> What is being contrasted?

> What conclusion is being drawn?

The purpose of slow reading is not slowness for its own sake.

It is accurate perception.

---

## 84.11 Familiarity Is Not Understanding

A person may have heard a verse hundreds of times.

He may know where it appears.

He may remember the wording.

He may know the popular sermon attached to it.

He may know the traditional application.

Yet he may never have examined its immediate argument.

Familiarity can therefore create a false sense of understanding.

The workman must periodically approach familiar passages as though they deserve fresh examination.

Not because the text changes.

Because the reader may discover that his understanding was incomplete.

---

## 84.12 Language Is the Medium of Communication

Biblical interpretation requires attention to language because Scripture communicates through language.

Words matter.

Grammar matters.

Syntax matters.

Relations among words matter.

Sentence structure matters.

Paragraph structure matters.

Argument matters.

A doctrine cannot legitimately be detached from the linguistic form through which it is communicated.

This does not mean that every interpretive question requires advanced grammatical analysis.

It means that the workman must respect language.

---

## 84.13 Words Have Meaning in Use

A word does not carry every possible meaning every time it occurs.

Lexical range is not contextual meaning.

The fact that a Greek word can mean several things does not authorize the interpreter to choose whichever sense supports his conclusion.

The workman asks:

> **Which sense is warranted here?**

He considers:

- immediate context;
- grammatical form;
- subject matter;
- authorial usage;
- parallel usage;
- argument;
- and relevant lexical evidence.

The dictionary supplies possibilities.

Context determines which possibility belongs to the passage.

---

## 84.14 The Dictionary Does Not Interpret the Verse

A lexical definition is evidence.

It is not a complete interpretation.

A lexicon may provide a semantic range.

The workman must still determine contextual force.

Therefore:

> **Lexical information serves interpretation; it does not replace interpretation.**

The interpreter who simply finds a preferred definition and inserts it into every occurrence has not completed the work.

He has only begun.

---

## 84.15 The Danger of Word Studies Without Context

One of the most common forms of overinterpretation is the isolated word study.

The worker discovers an interesting meaning in a lexicon.

He then builds doctrine around that meaning.

But the passage may not use the word in that sense.

The result is a lexical fallacy disguised as scholarship.

The disciplined workman therefore asks:

> Does this meaning fit the sentence?

> Does it fit the paragraph?

> Does it fit the argument?

> Does it fit the author's usage?

> Does it fit the relevant grammatical form?

Only then can the lexical information contribute responsibly.

---

## 84.16 Grammar Serves Meaning

Grammar is not a decorative academic layer.

It communicates relationships.

Subject and object matter.

Tense and aspect can matter.

Number can matter.

Case relationships can matter.

Mood can matter.

Participles and infinitives can matter.

Conditional structures can matter.

But grammar must not be used as a magical mechanism for manufacturing doctrine.

The question is always:

> **What does this grammatical feature establish in this context?**

If it establishes little, the worker should say little.

If it establishes something important, he should show why.

---

## 84.17 Translation and Interpretation

Most readers encounter Scripture through translation.

A translation therefore deserves careful use.

The interpreter should distinguish:

> **What the translation says**

from:

> **What the underlying-language evidence contributes.**

Neither should be casually dismissed.

The worker should not use Greek or Hebrew simply to overturn an English translation.

Nor should he assume that every English rendering exhausts the underlying semantic possibilities.

The responsible approach is examination.

---

## 84.18 Translation Is Not a Blank Check

An interpreter cannot say:

> “The Greek can mean X”

and therefore conclude:

> “The verse means X.”

The underlying language must still be interpreted in context.

Similarly, the existence of an alternative translation does not automatically invalidate the received rendering.

The workman must ask:

> **Which rendering best accounts for the linguistic and contextual evidence?**

Translation questions therefore remain evidence questions.

---

## 84.19 Immediate Context

The first context is the immediate context.

Read the sentence.

Read the paragraph.

Read what comes before.

Read what comes after.

Ask why the statement occurs where it occurs.

The immediate context often supplies qualifications that an isolated quotation hides.

The workman should therefore resist building doctrine from fragments before reading the surrounding argument.

---

## 84.20 The Larger Context

Immediate context is not always sufficient.

The interpreter may need to examine the surrounding section, chapter, book, or related argument.

But expansion should be purposeful.

More context is not automatically better context.

The worker should not collect dozens of passages merely because they contain similar words.

He should identify what contextual expansion is required to answer the question.

The governing principle is:

> **Expand context as far as necessary to understand the proposition, but do not replace argument with accumulation.**

---

## 84.21 Speaker

Who is speaking?

This question can change the interpretation.

The Bible records the words of many persons.

Some statements are divine declarations.

Some are prophetic speech.

Some are apostolic instruction.

Some are questions.

Some are accusations.

Some are lies spoken by wicked persons.

Some are statements reported in narrative.

The interpreter must therefore identify the speaker before assigning doctrinal force.

---

## 84.22 Audience

To whom is the statement addressed?

A statement addressed to Israel is not automatically addressed in exactly the same manner to the church.

A statement addressed to a particular individual is not automatically a universal command.

A statement to believers in a specific circumstance may contain a principle without being a direct repetition of the same circumstance for every reader.

The workman therefore distinguishes:

> **original audience**

from:

> **present application.**

---

## 84.23 Subject

What is the passage actually discussing?

A reader may begin with a theological category that the passage itself is not addressing.

For example, a statement about a particular historical event may be recruited into an unrelated doctrinal controversy.

The workman asks:

> **What question is the author answering?**

That question often prevents interpretive drift.

---

## 84.24 Occasion

Why was the statement made?

Letters have circumstances.

Prophecies have circumstances.

Warnings have circumstances.

Questions arise from situations.

Commands respond to needs.

The occasion does not automatically limit the truth of the statement.

But understanding the occasion can clarify what problem the statement addresses.

The workman therefore seeks historical and literary setting where it materially affects meaning.

---

## 84.25 Time

When was the statement made?

Temporal setting can matter.

A command can belong to a particular event.

A prophecy can concern a future period.

A historical description can record what happened at a particular time.

A promise can have a specified temporal context.

The interpreter must not erase time merely because a statement is spiritually attractive.

---

## 84.26 Scope

How far does the proposition extend?

This is one of the most important questions in interpretation.

A statement may concern:

- one person;
- one group;
- one generation;
- one circumstance;
- one covenantal setting;
- one event;
- or a broader principle.

The workman must not universalize without warrant.

Nor should he unnecessarily restrict what the text clearly generalizes.

The discipline is proportionality.

---

## 84.27 Conditions

Does the statement contain a condition?

Words such as:

> if;

> unless;

> except;

> when;

> until;

> provided;

can matter greatly.

Removing the condition changes the proposition.

The workman must preserve it.

A conditional promise should not automatically be preached as unconditional.

A conditional warning should not be treated as though the condition were irrelevant.

The interpreter must carry the condition through the conclusion.

---

## 84.28 Qualifications

A text may qualify a statement.

It may say:

> some;

> many;

> all;

> only;

> chiefly;

> nevertheless;

> but;

> except.

These qualifications constrain meaning.

The interpreter must not silently remove them because they make the conclusion less dramatic.

Faithful interpretation preserves qualifiers even when qualifiers complicate doctrine.

---

## 84.29 Negation

Negation must be respected.

“Not” matters.

“Neither” matters.

“Never” matters.

“No man” matters.

“Nothing” matters.

A reader who drops the negative can reverse the proposition.

This is one of the simplest and most serious interpretive errors.

The workman therefore watches for what the text denies as carefully as for what it affirms.

---

## 84.30 Contrast

Biblical arguments frequently use contrast.

But.

Yet.

Nevertheless.

On the other hand.

Instead.

Rather.

The contrast may establish the author's intended distinction.

If the interpreter merges the contrasted categories, he may destroy the argument.

The workman therefore asks:

> **What two things is the text distinguishing?**

---

## 84.31 Repetition

Repetition can signal emphasis.

It can also establish thematic continuity.

But repeated wording does not automatically establish identical contextual meaning.

The workman must distinguish:

> repeated word

from:

> repeated proposition.

A word can recur while its function changes.

Likewise, a concept can recur through different vocabulary.

Interpretation therefore requires more than word counting.

---

## 84.32 Connectives

Connective words often reveal argumentative structure.

“For” may provide reason.

“Therefore” may introduce consequence.

“Because” may identify cause.

“But” may contrast.

“So that” may indicate purpose or result.

“If” may introduce condition.

The workman should therefore follow the argument through its connectors.

A passage is not merely a pile of sentences.

It is a structured communication.

---

## 84.33 Questions Can Carry Arguments

A question can communicate more than uncertainty.

It can expose an assumption.

It can challenge an opponent.

It can introduce an argument.

It can prepare a conclusion.

It can contain a rhetorical assertion.

The workman must therefore ask:

> **What function does this question perform?**

He should not automatically interpret every question as though it were a doctrinal proposition.

---

## 84.34 Answers Must Be Read With Their Questions

An answer can be misunderstood if the question is ignored.

This is particularly important in dialogue.

A response may correct an assumption rather than answer the question exactly as asked.

The workman therefore reads:

> **question → answer → explanation → consequence.**

This is part of contextual discipline.

---

## 84.35 Narrative Is Not Command

A narrative tells what happened.

A command tells what ought to be done.

The difference is fundamental.

The fact that a biblical character performed an action does not automatically make the action a command.

The fact that something happened does not automatically mean God approves it.

The workman must distinguish:

> **description**

from:

> **prescription.**

---

## 84.36 Narrative Is Not Endorsement

The Bible can truthfully report sinful actions.

It can report lies.

It can report violence.

It can report deception.

It can report bad decisions.

The inclusion of an event in Scripture does not automatically commend the event.

The workman therefore distinguishes:

> **What Scripture records**

from:

> **What Scripture commands or approves.**

---

## 84.37 Quoted Speech Is Not Automatically Doctrine

A biblical writer may quote another person's statement.

The fact that Scripture records the statement guarantees that the record is truthful as a record.

It does not necessarily mean the speaker's proposition is true.

The workman therefore asks:

> **Who said this?**

> **Why is it being reported?**

> **Does the narrative affirm or refute it?**

This is one of the most important protections against misuse of quotation.

---

## 84.38 Genre Matters

Different forms of writing communicate differently.

Narrative.

Poetry.

Wisdom.

Prophecy.

Law.

Gospel.

Epistle.

Apocalyptic material.

The presence of figurative language does not mean the text is meaningless.

Nor does a genre label automatically settle every interpretive question.

Genre is a contextual feature that helps the worker understand how the communication operates.

The workman therefore asks:

> **What kind of communication is this?**

---

## 84.39 Literal Language and Figurative Language

The workman should begin with the ordinary sense of language where that sense is coherent with the passage.

But biblical writing also uses figures.

The presence of figurative language does not authorize arbitrary interpretation.

A figure has a communicative purpose.

The interpreter must therefore ask:

> **What does the figure communicate?**

The goal is not to choose between “literal” and “figurative” as rival ideologies.

The goal is to understand what the language communicates in context.

---

## 84.40 Figures Must Be Interpreted, Not Invented

A figure does not become a license for unlimited symbolism.

The worker should identify textual signals and contextual evidence.

If a passage itself explains a symbol, that explanation carries special weight.

If a figure has a conventional function within its context, that matters.

If the interpreter invents an elaborate symbolic system unsupported by the passage, he has moved beyond disciplined interpretation.

The workman must distinguish:

> **figure supplied by the text**

from:

> **symbolism supplied by the interpreter.**

---

## 84.41 Comparison Is Necessary

Scripture should be compared with Scripture.

But comparison must be disciplined.

A related passage may:

- clarify vocabulary;
- illuminate a concept;
- supply parallel testimony;
- reveal an argument;
- provide historical context;
- or establish a complementary proposition.

The workman must ask what kind of relationship exists.

Similarity alone is insufficient.

---

## 84.42 Similarity Is Not Identity

Two passages can use the same word without discussing the same thing.

Two passages can discuss similar subjects without making the same claim.

Two passages can use different words to communicate the same principle.

Therefore:

> **Word similarity is not proof of doctrinal identity.**

The interpreter must compare propositions, context, and argument.

---

## 84.43 Cross-Reference Is Not Cross-Proof

A cross-reference can be useful.

But merely placing two verses side by side does not prove that one explains the other.

The workman should be able to state:

> **Why are these passages related?**

If the answer is only:

> “They both contain the same word,”

the comparison may be weak.

If the answer is:

> “They address the same proposition in comparable contexts and illuminate one another,”

the relationship is stronger.

The quality of comparison depends upon the relationship established.

---

## 84.44 Comparison Must Not Become Concordance Theology

A concordance can identify occurrences.

It cannot by itself establish doctrine.

The worker who searches a word, collects every occurrence, and assumes the same meaning everywhere has confused retrieval with interpretation.

The concordance is a tool.

Context remains necessary.

The workman therefore uses word occurrence as a beginning point rather than an automatic conclusion.

---

## 84.45 Immediate Context Before Remote Parallel

A distant passage should not be allowed to override an immediate context merely because the distant passage uses similar language.

The interpreter begins with the passage under investigation.

Then he expands outward.

This preserves contextual priority.

The remote parallel may clarify.

It should not automatically control.

---

## 84.46 The Whole Canon and Local Meaning

The broader scriptural witness matters.

But the broader witness does not erase local meaning.

A passage means what it means in its own communication.

Then the interpreter asks how that meaning relates to other Scripture.

This avoids two opposite errors:

> **isolating the verse**

and:

> **dissolving the verse into a system.**

The workman preserves both local meaning and canonical relationship.

---

## 84.47 Explicit Statement

An explicit statement is what the text directly asserts.

This is the strongest starting point for interpretation.

If the text says:

> X,

the worker can establish:

> The text says X.

He should not immediately replace X with a larger doctrine.

First preserve the explicit proposition.

---

## 84.48 Necessary Inference

A conclusion can be genuinely established even when it is not expressed in exactly the same words.

A necessary inference follows from established propositions under valid reasoning.

The workman should therefore not restrict biblical knowledge only to sentences that appear word-for-word.

But he must demonstrate necessity.

The phrase:

> “It obviously follows”

is not enough.

Show the premises.

Show the relationship.

Show why the conclusion follows.

---

## 84.49 Possible Inference

A possible inference is different.

Several conclusions may be compatible with the same text.

Compatibility is not necessity.

The workman must therefore distinguish:

> **possible**

from:

> **necessary.**

This distinction protects against dogmatism.

A possibility can be worth considering.

It should not be presented as though the text requires it.

---

## 84.50 Probable Inference

Some conclusions may be more strongly supported than alternatives without being logically necessary.

The workman should be able to represent that difference.

He can say:

> “This appears most likely because…”

without pretending:

> “The Scripture explicitly says…”

The strength of language should correspond to the strength of warrant.

---

## 84.51 Interpretation and Inference

Interpretation asks:

> **What does this communication mean?**

Inference asks:

> **What follows from what has been established?**

They interact.

But they are not identical.

A worker can interpret a passage correctly and then make an invalid inference from it.

Likewise, a worker can reason correctly from a proposition he has misunderstood.

The discipline therefore requires both sound interpretation and sound reasoning.

---

## 84.52 Interpretation and Application

Application asks:

> **What should this mean for this person or circumstance?**

It may involve additional premises.

Suppose the text establishes principle X.

A particular circumstance presents facts Y.

The worker concludes that X applies to Y.

That conclusion may be sound.

But Y is not automatically part of the original text.

The workman should therefore identify the additional reasoning involved.

Application must remain downstream from meaning.

---

## 84.53 Application Must Not Rewrite Meaning

An application can be valid without being the original meaning.

The worker must resist saying:

> “This verse means…”

when he really means:

> “This principle can be applied…”

The distinction protects the text.

It also strengthens application because the reader can see the reasoning.

---

## 84.54 Doctrinal Synthesis

Doctrine often requires synthesis.

One passage may establish one proposition.

Another may establish another.

The workman brings them together.

But synthesis should remain identifiable.

The interpreter should not hide the premises.

A responsible doctrinal argument therefore shows:

> **Passage A → proposition A**

> **Passage B → proposition B**

> **relationship between A and B**

> **conclusion**

This is stronger than merely saying:

> “The Bible teaches…”

without demonstrating how.

---

## 84.55 Synthesis Is Not New Revelation

A conclusion synthesized from Scripture is not thereby a new revelation.

If the premises are genuinely biblical and the inference is warranted, the conclusion can be a legitimate doctrinal conclusion.

But it remains a conclusion drawn from what has been given.

The interpreter must not speak as though God has newly spoken merely because he has reasoned successfully.

This preserves the distinction between:

> **revelation**

and:

> **inference from revelation.**

---

## 84.56 The Interpreter Must Track His Sources

The workman should know where each claim comes from.

Is it:

- direct biblical assertion?
- necessary inference?
- historical evidence?
- lexical evidence?
- theological formulation?
- philosophical argument?
- traditional interpretation?
- application?

These categories should not be silently collapsed.

Source tracking is part of intellectual honesty.

---

## 84.57 The Source Hierarchy

The source hierarchy established by this book must remain operative.

The Graphē and Holy Writ occupy the primary position within the biblical investigation.

Biblical textual witnesses serve the investigation of the written record.

Historical and testimonial evidence can illuminate historical claims.

Lexical evidence can clarify language.

Historical sources can provide external information.

Commentaries and reference works can assist interpretation.

Philosophical and theological arguments can help articulate or test reasoning.

But none becomes primary merely because it is sophisticated.

The workman keeps the hierarchy visible.

---

## 84.58 Secondary Sources Serve the Work

A commentary can save time.

A historical work can supply information.

A lexicon can reveal semantic possibilities.

A grammar can clarify a construction.

A textual study can identify manuscript evidence.

These are useful.

But the workman asks:

> **What does this source actually establish?**

He does not simply copy its conclusion.

He examines its premises.

He examines its evidence.

He examines whether the source has crossed from evidence into interpretation.

---

## 84.59 The Commentary Audit

Before adopting a commentary's interpretation, the workman can ask:

1. What passage is being interpreted?
2. What does the commentator claim?
3. What evidence is offered?
4. What assumptions are introduced?
5. What alternative interpretations are considered?
6. Does the argument preserve context?
7. Does it distinguish text from inference?
8. Is a tradition being treated as though it were Scripture?
9. Does the conclusion exceed the evidence?
10. What remains uncertain?

This keeps commentary subordinate to the Word.

---

## 84.60 Tradition Audit

The same discipline applies to tradition.

Ask:

> Who taught this?

> How old is the teaching?

> What evidence supports it?

> Is it explicitly biblical?

> Is it a synthesis?

> Is it a historical interpretation?

> What assumptions support it?

Age can be evidence of historical continuity.

It is not proof of doctrinal truth.

The workman respects history without surrendering judgment.

---

## 84.61 Institutional Audit

Institutional statements also require classification.

An institution may have:

- authority over its own procedures;
- historical significance;
- educational expertise;
- recognized credentials.

But none of these automatically makes its interpretation Scripture.

The workman asks:

> **What kind of authority is being exercised?**

Institutional authority should not silently become biblical authority.

---

## 84.62 Experience Audit

Experience can be real.

It can be important.

But experience is not automatically interpretation.

A person may experience something and then construct an explanation.

The worker must distinguish:

> **what happened**

from:

> **what he concludes it means.**

Experience may supply testimony.

It does not automatically supply doctrinal authority.

---

## 84.63 Philosophy Audit

Philosophical reasoning can clarify concepts.

But philosophy must not silently become the master of Scripture.

The workman asks:

> What philosophical premise is being introduced?

> Is it established?

> Is it compatible with the biblical evidence?

> Is it being used to clarify or to override?

This is especially important when philosophical categories are imported into theological interpretation.

---

## 84.64 Theological System Audit

A theological system can organize doctrine.

But systems are human constructions.

The workman should therefore distinguish:

> **the text**

from:

> **the system used to organize conclusions from the text.**

A system may be useful.

It may also contain assumptions that need testing.

The workman must not read every passage through the system before examining the passage itself.

---

## 84.65 The Interpretation Audit

A complete interpretive audit can therefore ask:

### Text
What is written?

### Language
What do the words and grammatical relationships communicate?

### Context
What is the immediate and broader setting?

### Speaker
Who speaks?

### Audience
To whom?

### Occasion
Why?

### Time
When?

### Subject
About what?

### Scope
How far?

### Form
What kind of communication?

### Argument
How do the statements relate?

### Comparison
What other passages genuinely illuminate the question?

### Inference
What follows?

### Alternatives
What competing explanations exist?

### Evidence
What supports each?

### Limits
What does the passage not establish?

### Application
How should the established meaning be applied?

This is disciplined interpretation.

---

## 84.66 The Interpreter Must Test Alternatives

A worker should not ask only:

> “Can I make my interpretation work?”

He should ask:

> **“Can the competing interpretation also explain the evidence?”**

Then:

> **“Which explanation accounts for the evidence with fewer unsupported assumptions?”**

This is comparative reasoning.

It does not mean that the simplest explanation is always true.

It means that alternative explanations should be taken seriously.

---

## 84.67 The Principle of Alternative Explanation

If a conclusion depends upon a particular interpretation, the worker should identify alternatives.

For example:

> Interpretation A explains the passage this way.

> Interpretation B explains it another way.

The worker should then compare:

- textual fit;
- contextual fit;
- grammatical fit;
- canonical fit;
- inferential strength;
- assumptions;
- explanatory power.

This produces a stronger conclusion than merely asserting preference.

---

## 84.68 Competing Interpretations Are Not Equal by Definition

The existence of multiple interpretations does not prove that truth is unknowable.

There may be:

- one correct interpretation;
- several compatible applications;
- one strong inference and several weak ones;
- or a genuinely unresolved question.

The workman therefore refuses the lazy conclusion:

> “Everyone has an interpretation.”

That statement does not answer which interpretation is warranted.

The task remains examination.

---

## 84.69 The Interpreter Must Identify Hidden Assumptions

Many disagreements occur below the visible conclusion.

Two workers quote the same passage.

They disagree.

The difference may not be the quotation.

It may be an unstated premise.

For example:

> “If X is true, then Y.”

One worker accepts the condition.

The other rejects it.

The disagreement therefore exists at the premise level.

The workman must expose such assumptions.

---

## 84.70 The Hidden-Premise Test

Whenever a conclusion seems to appear suddenly, ask:

> **What premise connects the evidence to this conclusion?**

If the premise is explicit in Scripture, identify it.

If it is a necessary inference, demonstrate it.

If it is an external fact, support it.

If it is an application assumption, disclose it.

If it is merely a preference, do not disguise it as doctrine.

This single question can expose large amounts of interpretive confusion.

---

## 84.71 The Interpreter Must Distinguish Evidence From Explanation

Evidence supports a conclusion.

Explanation describes how the evidence relates to the conclusion.

These should not be confused.

A commentary may offer an explanation.

A historical document may offer evidence.

A lexical source may provide linguistic evidence.

A theological system may offer a synthesis.

The worker must classify the material correctly.

This is part of the evidence protocol.

---

## 84.72 Evidence Does Not Interpret Itself

Even evidence requires interpretation.

A historical inscription may establish that a place existed.

It does not automatically establish every theological conclusion attached to that place.

A lexical occurrence establishes usage.

It does not automatically establish doctrinal meaning.

A manuscript variant establishes a textual difference.

It does not automatically establish which interpretation is correct.

The workman therefore asks:

> **Evidence of what?**

---

## 84.73 The Evidence Question

For every significant piece of evidence:

1. What proposition does it support?
2. Is the evidence direct or indirect?
3. Is it independent?
4. Is it reliable?
5. Are there alternatives?
6. What additional premise is required?
7. How strong is the resulting conclusion?

This prevents evidence from becoming a decorative list of quotations.

---

## 84.74 The Argument Map

A serious interpretation should be reconstructible as an argument.

For example:

> **Claim → Definition → Evidence → Analysis → Inference → Conclusion → Consequence.**

The workman should be able to identify where each step occurs.

If the conclusion appears before the evidence has been established, he should stop.

If the definition changes halfway through the argument, he should notice.

If the evidence does not support the conclusion, he should revise.

This is disciplined reasoning inside interpretation.

---

## 84.75 The Why Audit

The workman should repeatedly ask:

> **Why?**

Why does this passage mean that?

Why does this word carry that sense?

Why does this context apply?

Why does this inference follow?

Why is this comparison legitimate?

Why does this conclusion extend to this audience?

Why should this application be made?

Why should this alternative be rejected?

The answer to “why?” should lead backward to evidence and premises.

If the answer eventually becomes:

> “Because this is how we have always understood it,”

the worker has discovered a tradition, not necessarily a proof.

---

## 84.76 The Circularity Audit

The workman must also ask:

> **Am I using my interpretation to prove my interpretation?**

For example:

> “This passage means X because our doctrine says X.”

Then:

> “We know our doctrine is true because this passage means X.”

That is circular.

The worker must find independent support where independent support is required.

The Graphē may establish a proposition.

The interpreter cannot make his interpretation authoritative merely by repeating it.

---

## 84.77 The Authority Audit

When a conclusion is asserted, ask:

> **What gives this conclusion authority?**

If the answer is:

> Scripture explicitly says it,

identify the passage.

If:

> It follows necessarily from Scripture,

show the premises.

If:

> This is a historical interpretation,

label it accordingly.

If:

> This is a theological synthesis,

say so.

If:

> This is an application,

do not call it direct revelation.

The category of the claim should remain visible.

---

## 84.78 The Certainty Audit

Every conclusion should be expressed with proportionate certainty.

Ask:

> Is it explicit?

> Is it necessary inference?

> Is it strongly supported?

> Is it probable?

> Is it possible?

> Is it uncertain?

This does not weaken biblical conviction.

It strengthens intellectual honesty.

A worker who treats every possibility as certainty will eventually lose the ability to distinguish strong doctrine from speculation.

---

## 84.79 The Silence Audit

Silence must also be handled carefully.

The absence of a statement does not automatically prove its opposite.

An argument from silence requires justification.

The workman should ask:

> **Was the author expected to mention this if it were true?**

> **Does the structure of the argument make the silence significant?**

> **Is there another reason for the omission?**

Silence can sometimes be informative.

It is not automatically proof.

---

## 84.80 The Exception Audit

Universal statements require careful testing.

Words such as:

> all;

> every;

> always;

> never;

> only;

require attention to scope and exceptions.

The worker should not make universal claims from local statements without warrant.

Likewise, he should not create exceptions merely because a universal statement is difficult.

The text determines the scope.

---

## 84.81 The Category Audit

Category errors can create false disagreements.

For example:

> historical evidence is asked to perform the work of doctrinal authority;

or:

> a theological inference is treated as though it were a quotation;

or:

> a personal application is treated as though it were original meaning.

The workman asks:

> **What kind of claim is this?**

Correct classification often resolves confusion.

---

## 84.82 The Definition Audit

Many theological disagreements are actually definition disagreements.

Two people may use:

> faith;

> revelation;

> inspiration;

> authority;

> interpretation;

> prophecy;

> church;

> kingdom;

in different senses.

The workman therefore defines important terms before arguing from them.

A word cannot carry an argument if its meaning changes during the argument.

---

## 84.83 The Context Audit

A complete context audit should ask:

- What comes immediately before?
- What comes immediately after?
- Who speaks?
- Who listens?
- What occasion produced the statement?
- What problem is being addressed?
- What is the main subject?
- What qualifications appear?
- What contrasts appear?
- What conclusion follows?
- What larger section contains the argument?

The more significant the doctrinal conclusion, the more seriously this audit should be performed.

---

## 84.84 The Comparison Audit

When comparing Scripture with Scripture:

1. Identify the proposition in passage A.
2. Identify the proposition in passage B.
3. Explain why they are related.
4. Distinguish similarity from identity.
5. Identify what each passage independently establishes.
6. Identify what the combination establishes.
7. Avoid importing conclusions that belong to neither passage.

This prevents proof-text chains.

---

## 84.85 The Proof-Text Audit

A proof text is not necessarily a bad thing.

A text can genuinely prove a proposition.

The problem arises when the text is detached from the proposition it actually establishes.

The worker therefore asks:

> **What exactly does this verse prove?**

Not:

> “How can this verse be made relevant?”

Relevance is broader than proof.

The distinction matters.

---

## 84.86 The One-Verse Audit

A single verse can establish a great deal.

It can also establish very little.

The workman should not assume that the number of verses determines the strength of the argument.

One explicit statement may be sufficient.

Ten weakly related quotations may not be.

The governing question remains:

> **What does the evidence establish?**

---

## 84.87 The Accumulation Audit

Likewise, multiple passages can form a cumulative case.

But accumulation is not magic.

Ten passages that all support the same premise may strengthen confidence.

Ten passages that merely resemble one another may add little.

The workman therefore distinguishes:

> **quantity of quotations**

from:

> **quality of evidential relationship.**

---

## 84.88 The Harmony Audit

When passages appear to conflict, the worker should investigate before declaring contradiction.

But he should also avoid forced harmonization.

A harmonization is not successful merely because it makes every passage fit a preferred system.

The workman asks:

> Does the proposed reconciliation respect each passage?

> Does it require unsupported assumptions?

> Does it alter the wording?

> Does it preserve context?

> Is the reconciliation necessary or merely possible?

This produces disciplined harmony rather than manufactured harmony.

---

## 84.89 The Contradiction Audit

A genuine contradiction requires incompatible propositions under the same relevant conditions.

The worker must therefore establish:

- what proposition A says;
- what proposition B says;
- whether they actually conflict;
- whether they concern the same subject;
- same time;
- same respect;
- same scope;
- same conditions.

Many apparent contradictions disappear when these categories are examined.

But the workman must not simply assume that every difficulty has an easy reconciliation.

He should follow the evidence.

---

## 84.90 The Interpretation of Difficult Passages

A difficult passage should be treated proportionately.

Do not abandon the text because it is difficult.

Do not invent certainty because difficulty is uncomfortable.

Do not allow a commentator to settle the question merely because his explanation sounds sophisticated.

Do the work.

Read.

Compare.

Define.

Reason.

Test.

Then state what can actually be established.

---

## 84.91 The Discipline of Uncertainty

A mature interpreter can locate uncertainty precisely.

He can say:

> “The wording is clear, but the historical background is uncertain.”

Or:

> “The historical event is clear, but its theological significance is debated.”

Or:

> “The principle is clear, but its application here requires further judgment.”

This is superior to global uncertainty.

The workman should not say:

> “We cannot know anything.”

when something is clear.

Nor:

> “We know everything.”

when something is disputed.

---

## 84.92 The Discipline of Stopping

Stopping is one of the highest forms of interpretive discipline.

When the evidence ends, stop.

When the conclusion is established, stop.

When the question has been answered, stop.

When further speculation becomes unnecessary, stop.

The workman must not feel compelled to produce a conclusion for every conceivable question.

The Word has not promised to answer every question humans can invent.

Interpretive maturity includes restraint.

---

## 84.93 The Discipline of Returning

Stopping does not mean permanent closure.

A conclusion can later be revisited when new evidence or better reasoning appears.

The workman should be able to return to a question without treating revision as failure.

This creates a cycle:

> **Interpret → Test → Conclude → Revisit if necessary → Correct if warranted.**

The source remains stable.

The worker remains teachable.

---

## 84.94 The Discipline of Correction

Correction should operate at multiple levels.

The worker may correct:

- perception;
- definition;
- lexical assumption;
- contextual reading;
- inference;
- conclusion;
- application.

He should correct the earliest defective step he can identify.

If the premise is wrong, merely changing the conclusion may not be enough.

If the context was misunderstood, later reasoning must be rebuilt.

The workman corrects foundations, not merely symptoms.

---

## 84.95 The Discipline of Intellectual Honesty

Intellectual honesty requires saying exactly what the evidence permits.

It means:

> **Do not say more because you want the conclusion to be stronger.**

It also means:

> **Do not say less because the conclusion is uncomfortable.**

The workman neither exaggerates nor suppresses.

He represents the evidence proportionately.

---

## 84.96 The Discipline of Intellectual Courage

Honesty sometimes requires courage.

A conclusion may contradict one's tradition.

It may challenge one's previous teaching.

It may disappoint one's audience.

It may require revision of a published work.

It may weaken a cherished argument.

The workman must still follow the evidence.

The objective is not preservation of identity.

It is faithfulness.

---

## 84.97 The Discipline of Intellectual Humility

Humility means knowing the difference between:

> **“I know.”**

and:

> **“I think.”**

between:

> **“The text says.”**

and:

> **“I infer.”**

between:

> **“This follows necessarily.”**

and:

> **“This appears possible.”**

between:

> **“This is established.”**

and:

> **“This remains uncertain.”**

Such distinctions are not signs of weakness.

They are signs of disciplined scholarship.

---

## 84.98 The Discipline of Teaching

The interpreter who teaches must make his reasoning accessible.

He should not use technical vocabulary to hide weak evidence.

He should define terms.

He should show the text.

He should explain the context.

He should identify the inference.

He should distinguish application.

He should welcome examination.

Teaching therefore becomes an extension of interpretive discipline.

---

## 84.99 The Discipline of Listening

A teacher must also listen.

An objection can reveal:

- a hidden premise;
- a missed context;
- a lexical problem;
- an alternative interpretation;
- or merely a misunderstanding.

The workman should distinguish these.

He should not dismiss an objection simply because it is inconvenient.

Listening is part of testing.

---

## 84.100 The Discipline of Fair Representation

If presenting another interpretation, represent it fairly.

Do not weaken the opponent's argument into a caricature.

State the strongest form you can reasonably identify.

Then test it.

This is intellectual justice.

It also strengthens the worker's own argument.

If the preferred conclusion survives the strongest serious alternative, it becomes more credible.

---

## 84.101 The Discipline of Not Striving About Words

The context of 2 Timothy 2:14–26 remains important.

Precision about words is necessary.

But endless verbal combat is not the goal.

The worker should distinguish:

> **productive lexical analysis**

from:

> **contentious word-battling.**

The question is whether the examination produces truth and understanding.

If a dispute about terminology contributes nothing to the proposition under investigation, the workman should know when to move on.

---

## 84.102 The Discipline of Profitable Speech

Interpretation should produce profitable communication.

The goal is not merely to display information.

The work should help the learner understand:

- what is written;
- what it means;
- what follows;
- and how it should be handled.

A technically impressive argument that leaves the central proposition obscure has failed its communicative purpose.

The workman serves understanding.

---

## 84.103 The Discipline of Patience

Interpretive discipline requires patience.

Some questions cannot be answered responsibly in five minutes.

Some require repeated reading.

Some require comparison.

Some require historical investigation.

Some require lexical study.

Some require waiting until the evidence is clearer.

Patience prevents premature conclusions.

---

## 84.104 The Discipline of Proportion

Not every question deserves the same level of analysis.

The worker should distinguish:

- central claims;
- supporting claims;
- minor questions;
- speculative questions.

A major doctrinal conclusion deserves substantial warrant.

A minor observation may require less.

The workman therefore applies effort proportionately.

This is another expression of σπουδάζω.

---

## 84.105 The Discipline of Relevance

Evidence should be relevant to the proposition.

A source can be true and still irrelevant.

A historical fact may be interesting but not establish the conclusion.

A lexical fact may be accurate but not settle the doctrine.

A theological quotation may be respected but not function as proof.

The workman asks:

> **How does this evidence bear upon the claim?**

---

## 84.106 The Discipline of Independence

Independent evidence is generally stronger than multiple copies of the same source.

If five commentaries repeat one commentator, the number five does not create five independent witnesses.

If ten websites repeat one tradition, repetition does not become independent confirmation.

The workman therefore distinguishes:

> **many sources**

from:

> **independent lines of evidence.**

---

## 84.107 The Discipline of Source Reliability

Not every source is equally reliable for every question.

A lexicon is suited to lexical questions.

A manuscript is relevant to textual questions.

A historical record may illuminate historical questions.

A commentary may reveal an interpretation.

A theological system may organize doctrine.

The worker must therefore match source type to question.

A source should not be asked to prove what it was never capable of proving.

---

## 84.108 The Discipline of Evidence Strength

Evidence can be:

- direct;
- indirect;
- strong;
- weak;
- independent;
- dependent;
- corroborative;
- ambiguous.

The workman should not flatten these distinctions.

A single explicit statement can be stronger for one question than twenty indirect references.

The quantity of evidence is not the only variable.

Its relationship to the proposition matters.

---

## 84.109 The Discipline of Argumentative Honesty

The worker should state the strongest objection to his own conclusion.

Ask:

> **What would someone who disagrees with me point out?**

Then answer it fairly.

If the objection succeeds, revise.

If it fails, explain why.

This creates an argument that can withstand examination.

The workman should not hide weaknesses from himself.

---

## 84.110 The Discipline of Consequence

Interpretation should be tested for consequence.

If a conclusion is true, what else follows?

Does it contradict another established proposition?

Does it require an impossible premise?

Does it produce a category error?

Does it depend upon a definition that changes?

Consequential reasoning can expose hidden problems.

But consequences should not replace textual evidence.

They test the coherence of the conclusion.

---

## 84.111 The Discipline of Coherence

A good interpretation should fit the passage and the broader established evidence.

But “coherence” does not mean forcing every passage into a preconceived system.

The worker should distinguish:

> **coherence discovered from the evidence**

from:

> **coherence imposed upon the evidence.**

The first is explanatory.

The second is potentially circular.

---

## 84.112 The Discipline of Simplicity

When two interpretations explain the evidence comparably well, unnecessary assumptions should be treated cautiously.

But simplicity is not an absolute rule.

A difficult truth does not become false because a simpler alternative is emotionally attractive.

The principle is:

> **Do not add complexity without warrant.**

The workman should neither complicate the simple nor simplify the genuinely complex.

---

## 84.113 The Discipline of Restraint

Interpretive restraint means refusing to say what the evidence does not support.

It does not mean refusing to say what the evidence does support.

Therefore restraint has two sides:

> **Do not overstate.**

> **Do not understate.**

Both distort the source.

---

## 84.114 The Discipline of Repetition and Re-Reading

Re-reading can reveal:

- repeated themes;
- contrasts;
- structural markers;
- argument progression;
- qualifications;
- and relationships.

The workman should therefore return to the passage after forming a preliminary interpretation.

Ask:

> **Does the text still read naturally under my conclusion?**

If not, investigate.

---

## 84.115 The Discipline of Writing

Writing exposes reasoning.

A thought that seems clear in the mind may become confused on paper.

Writing therefore serves interpretation.

The workman should be able to write:

1. the proposition;
2. the evidence;
3. the reasoning;
4. the conclusion;
5. the limitation.

If he cannot state the argument clearly, he may not yet understand it clearly.

---

## 84.116 The Discipline of Revision

Revision is part of interpretation.

A sentence may be too strong.

A conclusion may exceed the evidence.

A definition may be ambiguous.

A quotation may need context.

A transition may conceal an inference.

The workman revises.

This is not merely editorial polish.

It is intellectual correction.

---

## 84.117 The Discipline of Documentation

Where external evidence is used, the worker should preserve its source.

Where lexical claims are made, the lexical source should be identifiable.

Where historical claims are made, the historical evidence should be distinguishable.

Where quotations are used, the wording should be accurate.

Documentation protects against accidental invention and deliberate misrepresentation.

---

## 84.118 The Discipline of Verification

Before presenting a significant conclusion, verify:

- quotation;
- reference;
- lexical form;
- grammatical claim;
- historical claim;
- contextual statement;
- inference.

The workman should not rely on memory when verification is reasonably available.

Memory is useful.

Verification is stronger.

---

## 84.119 The Discipline of Correction After Publication

The work does not become infallible because it has been published.

If an error is discovered, correct it.

A published mistake is still a mistake.

A respected author is still capable of error.

A mature workman therefore values correction more than the appearance of perfection.

---

## 84.120 The Discipline of Digital Research

Digital tools make biblical research faster.

They also make careless research easier.

Search results can detach phrases from context.

Automated summaries can merge distinct sources.

Copied quotations can reproduce errors.

Search rankings can create an illusion of consensus.

The workman therefore verifies digital information against primary sources wherever possible.

Speed is useful.

Accuracy remains necessary.

---

## 84.121 The Discipline of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence can assist the workman.

It can help:

- locate passages;
- compare wording;
- organize material;
- generate questions;
- identify possible objections;
- summarize sources;
- and expose alternative explanations.

But generated output is not automatically true.

The worker must ask:

> **What is the source?**

> **What evidence supports this claim?**

> **Is this quotation accurate?**

> **Is this interpretation or direct Scripture?**

> **What premise is hidden?**

AI can accelerate the labour.

It cannot assume the responsibility of the workman.

---

## 84.122 The Discipline of Tool Verification

The more powerful the tool, the more important verification becomes.

A concordance can retrieve.

A lexicon can define.

A database can search.

An AI can synthesize.

A commentary can explain.

None can relieve the worker of judgment.

The tool should therefore be treated as:

> **instrument**

not:

> **authority.**

---

## 84.123 The Discipline of Human Judgment

Interpretation ultimately requires judgment.

The worker must decide:

- whether a lexical sense fits;
- whether a context is relevant;
- whether an inference is necessary;
- whether a comparison is legitimate;
- whether an alternative is stronger;
- whether a conclusion is sufficiently supported.

This is why the earlier chapters on reasonability, exercise, maturity, and discernment matter.

The method does not eliminate judgment.

It disciplines judgment.

---

## 84.124 The Discipline of Prayer and Intellectual Labour

Prayer and intellectual labour should not be placed in opposition.

The workman can seek God's help while diligently studying.

He can ask for wisdom while examining evidence.

He can pray while comparing passages.

But prayer should not become an excuse for refusing intellectual responsibility.

Nor should intellectual ability become an excuse for refusing dependence upon God.

The posture is:

> **pray and labour; seek and examine; trust and reason.**

---

## 84.125 Divine Assistance Does Not Abolish Discipline

A reader may sincerely believe that God helps him understand Scripture.

That belief does not make every impression infallible.

The workman remains responsible to test what he thinks he understands.

Otherwise subjective certainty could become a rival authority.

The disciplined posture is:

> **I seek divine help, but I remain accountable to the written Word.**

---

## 84.126 The Discipline of Teachability

The workman must remain teachable.

Teachability does not mean accepting every opinion.

It means being willing to examine.

A person can say:

> “Show me the evidence.”

and then genuinely examine it.

He can reject a conclusion after testing it.

He can accept correction when warranted.

Teachability therefore means openness to evidence, not surrender of judgment.

---

## 84.127 The Discipline of Independence Without Isolation

The workman should think independently.

But independent thinking does not mean refusing all external help.

Isolation can create blind spots.

The worker should therefore consult:

- other readers;
- language resources;
- historical sources;
- commentaries;
- textual evidence;
- and serious objections.

But he must return to the primary authority.

The proper posture is:

> **consult broadly, judge carefully, remain under the Word.**

---

## 84.128 The Discipline of Community

Interpretation can benefit from community.

Another reader may notice something missed.

A discussion may reveal an assumption.

A historical specialist may supply relevant information.

A language specialist may correct a lexical mistake.

But community does not make a conclusion true by majority vote.

The community assists examination.

The Graphē remains the governing source.

---

## 84.129 The Discipline of Majority Agreement

Consensus can be evidence of historical continuity.

It can also reveal widespread error.

Therefore majority agreement should be classified rather than worshipped.

The workman asks:

> **Why do they agree?**

If the agreement rests upon strong evidence, that matters.

If it rests upon inherited tradition, the category is different.

The number of people holding a conclusion cannot substitute for warrant.

---

## 84.130 The Discipline of Minority Views

A minority interpretation is not automatically wrong.

Nor is it automatically courageous or prophetic.

It must be examined.

The workman asks:

> What does it explain?

> What evidence supports it?

> What assumptions does it require?

> What difficulties does it create?

This protects the worker from both conformity and contrarianism.

---

## 84.131 The Discipline of Novelty

Novelty is neither proof nor disproof.

An old interpretation may be correct.

A new interpretation may be correct.

An old interpretation may be wrong.

A new interpretation may be wrong.

The workman therefore asks:

> **What does the evidence warrant?**

not:

> **Is this original?**

---

## 84.132 The Discipline of Historical Awareness

Historical interpretation matters when the historical setting affects meaning.

The workman should therefore investigate relevant:

- persons;
- places;
- customs;
- political circumstances;
- religious practices;
- literary conventions;
- and historical events.

But historical reconstruction must remain evidence-based.

The worker should distinguish:

> **known historical fact**

from:

> **plausible reconstruction**

and:

> **speculation.**

---

## 84.133 The Discipline of Historical Limits

Historical information can clarify Scripture.

It cannot automatically determine doctrine.

An external historical source may tell us how a culture behaved.

It does not thereby tell us what Scripture teaches.

The workman integrates historical evidence carefully.

---

## 84.134 The Discipline of Manuscript Awareness

Where textual questions arise, the workman must distinguish:

> **what the textual evidence establishes**

from:

> **what interpretation is built upon the text.**

A manuscript question is not automatically a theological question.

A textual variant should be identified accurately.

Its significance should be assessed proportionately.

The workman should not exaggerate a minor textual issue into a collapse of the entire biblical record.

Nor should he suppress a genuine textual question merely because it is inconvenient.

---

## 84.135 The Discipline of Canonical Awareness

The interpreter also works within a recognized scriptural corpus.

But the existence of a canonical corpus does not eliminate the need to interpret each writing according to its own context.

The whole informs the part.

The part contributes to the whole.

Neither should erase the other.

---

## 84.136 The Discipline of Christ's Use of Scripture

Earlier chapters established Christ's treatment of the written Word.

The interpreter should learn from the seriousness with which Christ treated Scripture.

But Christ's use of Scripture should not become a shortcut around interpretation.

The worker must examine:

- what Christ quoted;
- why He quoted it;
- how the quotation functions in His argument;
- what the surrounding passage establishes.

The model is not merely:

> “Jesus quoted it.”

The model includes:

> **How did He reason from what was written?**

---

## 84.137 The Discipline of Apostolic Use

The apostles likewise reasoned from Scripture.

Their use demonstrates that written texts can function as:

- testimony;
- premise;
- prophecy;
- argument;
- correction;
- and explanation.

The workman should therefore study their reasoning rather than merely collect their quotations.

This continues the discipline of Chapter 76.

---

## 84.138 The Discipline of Following the Argument

The mature reader follows an argument from beginning to end.

He does not stop at the sentence that supports his conclusion.

He continues.

He asks:

> What happens next?

> Does the author qualify the statement?

> Does he answer an objection?

> Does he introduce an exception?

> Does the conclusion differ from what I expected?

Following the argument protects against selective reading.

---

## 84.139 The Discipline of Reading Against Yourself

One of the strongest interpretive disciplines is to read the passage against one's preferred conclusion.

Ask:

> **If my doctrine were false, what would I expect this passage to say?**

Then examine whether the passage actually behaves that way.

This can expose confirmation bias.

The workman should not merely search for support.

He should actively search for disconfirmation.

---

## 84.140 The Discipline of Confirmation

Confirmation is still valuable.

Once a proposition appears established, the workman can look for independent corroboration within Scripture and relevant evidence.

But confirmation should follow investigation, not replace it.

Otherwise the worker searches only for what he already believes.

---

## 84.141 The Discipline of Disconfirmation

Disconfirmation asks:

> **What evidence would challenge my conclusion?**

If nothing could possibly challenge the interpretation, the worker may have turned it into an unfalsifiable belief.

Not every theological claim can be tested in exactly the same manner.

But the principle of openness remains.

A disciplined interpretation should be able to identify its vulnerabilities.

---

## 84.142 The Discipline of Error Detection

The workman should learn recurring error patterns:

- proof texting;
- lexical fallacy;
- context neglect;
- equivocation;
- false universalization;
- category error;
- circular reasoning;
- argument from silence;
- appeal to popularity;
- appeal to antiquity;
- appeal to authority;
- emotional reasoning;
- hidden premise;
- false analogy;
- conflation of application and meaning.

The purpose is not to create a catalogue for attacking others.

It is to detect error in one's own reasoning.

---

## 84.143 The Discipline of Self-Audit

Before finalizing an interpretation, the workman should ask:

> Have I read the whole passage?

> Have I defined the important terms?

> Have I identified the speaker and audience?

> Have I preserved conditions and qualifications?

> Have I distinguished quotation from interpretation?

> Have I distinguished interpretation from inference?

> Have I identified hidden premises?

> Have I considered alternatives?

> Have I overstated certainty?

> Have I confused application with meaning?

> Have I allowed a secondary source to become primary?

> Have I checked my conclusion against the actual text?

This is the self-audit of the workman.

---

## 84.144 The Discipline of the “What Does This Establish?” Question

The most useful recurring question may be:

> **What does this establish?**

A quotation establishes something.

A lexical fact establishes something.

A historical fact establishes something.

An inference establishes something.

An application establishes something.

But none necessarily establishes everything.

The worker therefore asks this question repeatedly.

It forces proportionality.

---

## 84.145 The Companion Question

The second question is:

> **What does this not establish?**

This protects against overreach.

If a passage establishes:

> X,

what does it not establish?

If a lexical source establishes:

> possible meanings A, B, C,

does it establish that the context means B?

If a historical fact establishes:

> event Y,

does it establish the theological conclusion being proposed?

The negative boundary is often as important as the positive conclusion.

---

## 84.146 The Discipline of Boundaries

Every interpretation needs boundaries.

The worker should know:

> where the text begins;

> where the inference begins;

> where the application begins;

> where external evidence begins;

> where uncertainty begins.

These boundaries prevent the argument from becoming an undifferentiated mass of “biblical truth.”

---

## 84.147 The Discipline of Proportionate Language

The worker's language should reveal the strength of his conclusion.

Use:

> **“The text says…”**

for explicit statements.

Use:

> **“This necessarily implies…”**

when necessity can be demonstrated.

Use:

> **“This strongly suggests…”**

when evidence is strong but inferential.

Use:

> **“This may indicate…”**

when evidence is weaker.

Use:

> **“One possible explanation is…”**

when alternatives remain.

Use:

> **“The evidence does not presently settle…”**

when uncertainty remains.

This vocabulary is not mere style.

It communicates epistemic status.

---

## 84.148 The Discipline of Refusing False Precision

The opposite problem also exists.

A worker may invent exactness where the evidence does not provide it.

He may specify:

- exact chronology;
- exact motives;
- exact historical circumstances;
- exact symbolic referents;

without sufficient evidence.

False precision is still error.

The workman should be precise about uncertainty.

---

## 84.149 The Discipline of Not Making the Text Say More Than It Says

This is the simplest summary of interpretive restraint.

Do not add.

Do not subtract.

Do not distort.

Do not isolate.

Do not universalize without warrant.

Do not restrict without warrant.

Do not turn inference into quotation.

Do not turn application into meaning.

Do not turn tradition into Scripture.

Do not turn scholarship into authority.

The workman handles.

He does not manufacture.

---

## 84.150 The Discipline of Not Making the Text Say Less

Restraint has an opposite danger.

A worker may become so cautious that he refuses conclusions the text clearly establishes.

He may say:

> “We cannot know.”

when the passage explicitly states the matter.

He may reduce doctrine to possibility because he fears dogmatism.

That is not humility.

It is under-interpretation.

Faithful handling requires both:

> **restraint**

and:

> **courage to affirm what is established.**

---

## 84.151 The Discipline of Clear Conclusions

After the analysis, the workman should state the conclusion clearly.

A reader should know:

> What has been established?

If the worker cannot summarize the conclusion, the argument may still be incomplete.

The final conclusion should not be stronger than the evidence.

But it should not be weaker either.

---

## 84.152 The Discipline of Application

Once meaning is established, application may follow.

The workman should ask:

> What does this require?

> Who does it concern?

> Under what conditions?

> What additional facts are present?

> What principle is being applied?

The application should be traceable.

It should not be presented as though the original author directly addressed the modern circumstance unless that is actually the case.

---

## 84.153 The Discipline of Obedience

Interpretation that never reaches obedience can become sterile.

The purpose of understanding is not merely to accumulate explanations.

Where Scripture commands, the learner must obey.

Where Scripture corrects, the learner must be willing to change.

Where Scripture warns, the learner must take the warning seriously.

Where Scripture teaches, the learner must learn.

The workman therefore remains a servant.

---

## 84.154 The Discipline of Teaching Others

A mature interpreter should be able to teach without making the learner dependent upon his personality.

He should show the evidence.

He should explain the reasoning.

He should invite examination.

He should teach the learner to return to Scripture.

This is how the scholarly labour becomes generational.

---

## 84.155 The Discipline of Forming Workmen

The highest educational outcome is not:

> “I know what my teacher believes.”

It is:

> **“I can responsibly examine the Word myself.”**

This does not produce isolated individualism.

It produces capable participants in the community of learning.

The teacher remains valuable.

But the student becomes capable.

The workman produces other workmen.

---

## 84.156 The Discipline of Correcting Other Workmen

Correction should be:

- evidence-based;
- text-based;
- proportionate;
- clear;
- patient;
- and aimed at truth.

The purpose is not humiliation.

The workman should be able to say:

> “This conclusion does not follow because…”

rather than:

> “You are ignorant.”

The strength of the argument should carry the correction.

---

## 84.157 The Discipline of Receiving Correction

The same worker who corrects must receive correction.

A one-way correction system creates hierarchy rather than scholarship.

The mature workman asks:

> **Can you show me where my reasoning fails?**

If the objection is valid, he changes.

This is not surrender.

It is disciplined accountability.

---

## 84.158 The Discipline of Intellectual Fellowship

Serious interpretation benefits from dialogue.

A good question can reveal a hidden assumption.

A good objection can strengthen an argument.

A careful alternative can expose weakness.

The workman should therefore cultivate environments where examination is possible.

The goal is not consensus at any cost.

It is better understanding under the Word.

---

## 84.159 The Discipline of Silence

Sometimes the appropriate response is silence.

Not every speculation requires a doctrinal answer.

Not every controversial claim deserves immediate engagement.

Not every accusation requires a response.

The workman should know when speaking clarifies and when speaking multiplies confusion.

Silence can be restraint.

---

## 84.160 The Discipline of Returning to the Source

When interpretation becomes confused, return.

Return to the passage.

Return to the words.

Return to the context.

Return to the question.

Return to the evidence.

Return to the premises.

Return to the source.

The answer is not always found by adding more commentary.

Sometimes the worker has simply moved too far away from the text.

---

## 84.161 The Discipline of Simplicity After Complexity

Serious investigation can become complicated.

The final explanation should often become simpler.

After examining the language, context, history, arguments, and alternatives, the worker should be able to say:

> **Here is what the passage means.**

The complexity belonged to the investigation.

It should not become unnecessary complexity in the conclusion.

Clarity is the fruit of disciplined analysis.

---

## 84.162 The Discipline of Keeping the Text Visible

A long interpretation can eventually hide the text.

The workman should therefore keep returning to the actual passage.

Quote relevant wording.

Explain relevant context.

Show the connection.

Then state the conclusion.

The reader should never have to trust the interpreter's memory of Scripture when the Scripture itself can be examined.

---

## 84.163 The Discipline of Distinguishing Source and Commentary

The interpreter should make it clear when he is quoting Scripture and when he is explaining Scripture.

This is especially important in writing.

A paragraph may contain:

- biblical quotation;
- historical information;
- lexical explanation;
- interpretation;
- inference;
- application.

The reader should be able to tell which is which.

This is source transparency.

---

## 84.164 The Discipline of Intellectual Traceability

A conclusion should have a traceable path back to its grounds.

The reader should be able to ask:

> Where did this come from?

Then follow:

> conclusion → inference → premises → evidence → text.

If the chain disappears into:

> “This is what scholars say,”

the worker should continue investigating.

Traceability is one of the strongest protections against hidden authority.

---

## 84.165 The Discipline of Evidence Proportionality

The larger the claim, the stronger the support required.

A minor observation may need little.

A foundational doctrinal claim requires substantial warrant.

A universal claim requires broader evidence than a local observation.

A claim that overturns established interpretation requires especially careful argument.

The workman should therefore scale his evidence to his claim.

---

## 84.166 The Discipline of Intellectual Economy

More evidence is not always better.

Irrelevant evidence can obscure the argument.

Repeated evidence can create false weight.

The workman should therefore use enough evidence to establish the proposition and no more than necessary to clarify the argument.

This is not minimalism.

It is economy.

---

## 84.167 The Discipline of Completeness

Economy must not become omission.

A worker should not remove a difficult piece of evidence simply because it complicates the conclusion.

Completeness means including relevant contrary evidence.

The workman should present the case honestly enough that the reader can see the difficulty.

---

## 84.168 The Discipline of Fair Qualification

If a conclusion has a limitation, state it.

If a historical source is disputed, say so.

If a lexical claim depends upon a particular semantic judgment, identify it.

If a doctrine rests upon synthesis rather than one explicit verse, explain the synthesis.

Qualification increases credibility because it prevents false certainty.

---

## 84.169 The Discipline of the Whole Argument

The workman must eventually step back and ask:

> **Does the whole interpretation make sense?**

Not merely each sentence.

Not merely each word.

The entire argument.

Do the premises support the conclusion?

Do the conclusions remain consistent?

Have definitions shifted?

Have exceptions been ignored?

Has the worker introduced an assumption late in the argument?

Whole-argument review is essential.

---

## 84.170 The Discipline of the Reader's Question

The interpretation should answer the actual question.

If the question is:

> “Does Scripture explicitly say X?”

the first task is quotation and context.

If the question is:

> “What does X mean?”

the task is interpretation.

If:

> “What follows from X?”

the task is reasoning.

If:

> “How should I respond?”

the task is application.

The workman must not answer one question while pretending to answer another.

---

## 84.171 The Discipline of Not Answering a Different Question

A common error is substitution.

The reader asks:

> “Is this written?”

The teacher answers:

> “This is a beautiful doctrine.”

The reader asks:

> “What does the passage mean?”

The teacher answers:

> “This doctrine has been taught for centuries.”

The reader asks:

> “Does this follow?”

The teacher answers:

> “Many scholars believe it.”

None of these answers addresses the actual epistemic question.

The workman stays with the question.

---

## 84.172 The Discipline of Question Classification

Questions themselves should be classified.

Is it:

- textual?
- lexical?
- historical?
- theological?
- logical?
- evidential?
- interpretive?
- practical?

Different questions require different evidence.

A historical question cannot be answered merely by a theological assertion.

A lexical question cannot be settled by majority vote.

A practical question may require facts beyond the biblical proposition.

Classification improves precision.

---

## 84.173 The Discipline of Method Fit

The method should fit the question.

Do not perform a massive word study when the question is simply whether a verse explicitly says something.

Do not answer a complex historical question with a dictionary definition.

Do not settle a doctrinal synthesis with one isolated quotation.

Do not settle a textual question through theological preference.

Methodological fit is part of scholarly discipline.

---

## 84.174 The Discipline of Scale

Interpretive work operates at multiple scales:

> word → phrase → sentence → paragraph → section → book → canon.

The workman moves between scales carefully.

A word can clarify a sentence.

A sentence belongs to a paragraph.

A paragraph belongs to an argument.

An argument belongs to a book.

The book belongs to the broader scriptural witness.

No scale should erase another.

---

## 84.175 The Discipline of Structural Reading

The worker should notice literary structure where it materially affects meaning.

Repetition.

Parallelism.

Progression.

Contrast.

Chiasm where demonstrable.

Question-answer sequences.

Argument transitions.

Narrative development.

Structural observations can illuminate meaning.

But speculative structures should not become hidden authorities.

---

## 84.176 The Discipline of Demonstrable Structure

If a proposed structure is important to the interpretation, demonstrate it.

Do not simply announce:

> “This is a chiasm.”

Show the corresponding elements.

Do not simply say:

> “The passage has seven stages.”

Identify them.

The workman makes structural claims inspectable.

---

## 84.177 The Discipline of Theological Vocabulary

The interpreter must be careful when importing theological terms into a passage.

A theological term may summarize a biblical concept.

But the worker should not assume that the technical system associated with the term is automatically present in every passage where related words occur.

Define the term.

Show the evidence.

Then explain the relationship.

---

## 84.178 The Discipline of Doctrinal Vocabulary

Likewise, words such as:

> justification;

> sanctification;

> election;

> covenant;

> kingdom;

> church;

> salvation;

may carry extensive theological systems.

The workman must distinguish:

> the biblical usage in the passage

from:

> the later systematic definition.

The systematic definition may be useful.

It must not be silently imposed.

---

## 84.179 The Discipline of Systematic Synthesis

Systematic theology can organize conclusions.

But the workman should be able to trace the system back to its biblical premises.

A system becomes dangerous when it becomes the authority used to interpret the texts that originally supplied its premises.

The worker therefore moves:

> **text → interpretation → inference → synthesis**

rather than:

> **system → text forced into system.**

---

## 84.180 The Discipline of Returning From System to Text

When a doctrinal system encounters a difficult passage, the worker should not immediately alter the passage to preserve the system.

Return to the text.

Ask:

> What does this passage actually establish?

Then examine how it relates to the system.

If the system needs revision, revise it.

The system serves the Word.

---

## 84.181 The Discipline of Interpretation Under Authority

The entire chapter can now be summarized:

> **Interpretation is disciplined understanding under the authority of an authoritative text.**

The interpreter is active.

The text remains primary.

Reason is active.

Reason remains subordinate to the source.

Tools are active.

Tools remain subordinate.

Tradition can assist.

Tradition remains secondary.

The result is not passive reading.

It is accountable interpretation.

---

## 84.182 The Discipline of the Graphē

The Graphē therefore functions as the governing boundary.

The worker may explore widely.

But he must return.

He may consult many sources.

But he must classify them.

He may develop sophisticated arguments.

But he must show their premises.

He may form conclusions.

But he must distinguish them from the text.

He may apply truth.

But he must preserve meaning.

This is what it means to interpret under the Graphē.

---

## 84.183 The Discipline of Right Handling Revisited

Chapter 82 said:

> **Right handling preserves communicated meaning.**

Chapter 83 said:

> **The workman is accountable for that handling.**

Chapter 84 now adds:

> **The workman disciplines the interpretive process so that perception, language, context, comparison, reasoning, inference, and application remain ordered under the authority of the Word.**

The three chapters therefore form one unit.

> **Right handling → responsible worker → disciplined interpretation.**

---

## 84.184 The Discipline of the Approved Workman Revisited

The approved workman is not simply someone who reaches correct answers.

He is someone who works responsibly.

The process matters.

If he reaches a correct conclusion by accident, that does not demonstrate reliable workmanship.

A disciplined method allows the work to be examined.

It also makes correction possible.

The workman therefore values process because faithful conclusions require faithful handling.

---

## 84.185 The Discipline of Knowledge

Knowledge in this architecture is not mere possession of information.

It is the result of engagement with what has been given.

Perception.

Learning.

Reasoning.

Exercise.

The interpreter therefore asks not merely:

> “Do I know this verse?”

but:

> **“Do I know what this verse establishes?”**

That question moves the learner beyond citation.

---

## 84.186 The Discipline of Skill

Skill is demonstrated in use.

The worker shows skill when he can:

- identify context;
- preserve distinctions;
- expose assumptions;
- reason from premises;
- compare responsibly;
- recognize overreach;
- correct himself;
- and state conclusions proportionately.

Skill is therefore functional.

---

## 84.187 The Discipline of Maturity

Maturity becomes visible when the workman can handle complexity without losing the source.

He can engage difficult questions.

He can distinguish strong from weak arguments.

He can tolerate uncertainty.

He can correct himself.

He can teach others.

He can remain humble.

Maturity is not the elimination of difficulty.

It is increased capacity to work responsibly within difficulty.

---

## 84.188 The Discipline of Discernment

Discernment is the capacity to distinguish.

The workman distinguishes:

- source from interpretation;
- quotation from inference;
- meaning from application;
- evidence from assertion;
- possibility from necessity;
- tradition from Scripture;
- tool from authority;
- confidence from warrant;
- correction from humiliation.

This is not suspicion.

It is discrimination in service of truth.

---

## 84.189 The Discipline of Diligence

The workman cannot perform this discipline casually.

He must give attention.

He must return.

He must examine.

He must verify.

He must compare.

He must correct.

This is the practical force of the scholarly labour developed earlier.

Diligence is what keeps interpretation from becoming impulsive assertion.

---

## 84.190 The Discipline of Exercise

Repeated use develops capacity.

The worker becomes better at noticing:

- qualifiers;
- assumptions;
- context;
- argument;
- and inferential gaps.

But training must remain under the Word.

A worker can become highly skilled at defending a bad method.

Therefore exercise must be disciplined.

Practice must be corrected.

---

## 84.191 The Discipline of Correction

Every serious method needs correction.

If a worker repeatedly makes the same contextual error, the error should be identified.

If he repeatedly overextends lexical claims, the habit should be corrected.

If he repeatedly confuses application with meaning, the distinction should be reinforced.

Skill grows through corrected practice.

---

## 84.192 The Discipline of Responsibility

The culmination is responsibility.

The worker is not merely learning for himself.

His interpretation may become someone else's knowledge.

His teaching may become someone else's doctrine.

His application may influence someone's practice.

Therefore the workman must take the work seriously.

---

## 84.193 The Discipline of Love for Truth

The deepest intellectual discipline is love for truth.

If the worker loves his conclusion more than truth, he will defend the conclusion.

If he loves truth more than his conclusion, he will examine the conclusion.

If he loves his reputation more than truth, he will hide errors.

If he loves truth more than reputation, he will correct them.

The scholarly labour of the Graphē therefore has a moral direction.

---

## 84.194 The Discipline of Freedom From Ego

Interpretation becomes dangerous when the worker's ego becomes invested in being right.

The worker should therefore cultivate the ability to say:

> “I was wrong.”

This is not a minor virtue.

It protects the source from the interpreter.

The workman must never need his interpretation to be true merely because it is his.

---

## 84.195 The Discipline of Submission

Submission does not mean surrendering thought.

It means placing thought under the appropriate authority.

The worker reasons.

He questions.

He investigates.

He compares.

But at the end he asks:

> **What does the Word establish?**

If the Word contradicts his preferred conclusion, he must yield.

That is intellectual submission.

---

## 84.196 The Discipline of Obedient Reason

Reason is therefore neither sovereign nor suppressed.

It is obedient.

It serves the investigation.

It identifies relationships.

It exposes contradictions.

It tests premises.

It follows conclusions.

But it does not create revelation.

This is disciplined rationality under Scripture.

---

## 84.197 The Discipline of the Reader's Responsibility

The reader is responsible for his conclusions.

He cannot permanently outsource interpretation.

Teachers help.

Commentaries help.

Institutions help.

Tools help.

But the reader must eventually stand before the Word and ask:

> **What do I understand, and why?**

This is the formation of the workman.

---

## 84.198 The Discipline of the Teacher's Responsibility

The teacher carries additional responsibility because others may receive his conclusions.

He should therefore avoid:

- unsupported certainty;
- hidden assumptions;
- selective quotation;
- manipulation;
- and unnecessary complexity.

He should make the reasoning inspectable.

Teaching is stewardship.

---

## 84.199 The Discipline of the Scholar's Responsibility

The scholar has another responsibility.

He must resist the temptation to turn technical knowledge into authority.

His expertise should increase precision.

It should not increase sovereignty.

The scholar remains a workman.

---

## 84.200 The Discipline of the Institution's Responsibility

Institutions also benefit from this discipline.

A curriculum should distinguish Scripture from commentary.

A credential should not imply infallibility.

A doctrinal statement should identify its basis.

An institutional tradition should remain open to examination.

Institutional continuity is valuable.

It is not self-authenticating.

---

## 84.201 The Discipline of the Generation

A generation inherits interpretations.

It should also inherit methods of examination.

Otherwise error becomes easier to transmit than truth.

The disciplined workman therefore forms readers who can examine.

This is why the scholarly labour of the Graphē is generational rather than merely individual.

---

## 84.202 The Discipline of the Future Reader

The future reader should be able to see:

> What did the author claim?

> What evidence did he use?

> What was Scripture?

> What was interpretation?

> What was inference?

> What remained uncertain?

That transparency allows future correction.

A workman should write so that another workman can inspect the work.

---

## 84.203 The Discipline of Legacy

The workman's legacy should not be:

> “Everyone accepted my interpretation.”

It should be:

> **“The work was done faithfully, and others were taught to examine the Word responsibly.”**

This is a better scholarly legacy.

It keeps the source above the worker.

## 84.206 The Discipline in One Sentence

The whole chapter can be condensed to:

> **Biblical interpretation is the disciplined process by which the workman perceives, understands, examines, compares, reasons through, and applies the written Word while preserving the distinction between what the Graphē says and what the interpreter concludes.**

This is the central definition of the chapter.

## 84.207 The Interpretive Sequence

The practical sequence is:

> **Perceive → Identify → Define → Contextualize → Understand → Compare → Distinguish → Reason → Infer → Test → Conclude → Limit → Apply.**

The worker should not:

> infer before understanding;

or:

> apply before interpreting;

or:

> compare before identifying the proposition;

or:

> define a doctrine before defining the terms;

or:

> appeal to commentary before examining the text.

Order matters.

> **What alternatives exist?**

This is the working discipline of the approved workman.

## 84.210 The Core Interpretive Distinctions

The discipline can be summarized in the following distinctions:

| Distinction | Governing principle |
|---|---|
| Text / interpretation | The text is the source; interpretation explains it. |
| Perception / understanding | Seeing words is not yet grasping their meaning. |
| Lexical range / contextual meaning | Possible senses do not determine the sense in context. |
| Translation / underlying language | A translation communicates the source; linguistic analysis may clarify where necessary. |
| Context / isolation | Meaning is established within communicative relationships. |
| Narrative / command | What happened is not automatically what is commanded. |
| Report / endorsement | Reported speech is not automatically affirmed doctrine. |
| Explicit statement / inference | Direct assertion and derived conclusion are distinct. |
| Necessary / possible | Necessity requires demonstration; possibility does not establish doctrine. |
| Interpretation / application | Meaning must precede contextual application. |
| Evidence / conclusion | Evidence supports a conclusion but does not replace reasoning. |
| Scripture / commentary | Commentary serves Scripture; it does not become Scripture. |
| Tradition / authority | Historical continuity does not automatically establish truth. |
| Institution / Graphē | Institutional authority does not become biblical authority merely by recognition. |
| Experience / interpretation | Experience is not automatically its own explanation. |
| System / text | The theological system must remain accountable to the texts from which it is constructed. |
| Consensus / warrant | Agreement does not substitute for evidence. |
| Novelty / truth | Newness is neither proof nor disproof. |
| Confidence / certainty | Confidence should correspond to warrant. |
| Method / source | A method assists interpretation; it is not the authoritative source. |
| Tool / authority | Tools facilitate research; they do not assume the worker's responsibility. |
| Application / revelation | Applying established truth does not create new revelation. |
| Skill / infallibility | Skill increases capacity, not divine authority. |
| Correction / failure | Correction can be evidence of responsible workmanship. |

## 84.211 The Master Interpretive Discipline

The entire discipline can now be compressed into a single governing movement:

> **Begin with what is written.**

> **Perceive it accurately.**

> **Understand the language.**

> **Read the context.**

> **Identify the proposition.**

> **Distinguish speaker, audience, subject, time, scope, genre, conditions, and qualifications.**

> **Compare only where the comparison is warranted.**

> **Expose the premises.**

> **Reason from what has been established.**

> **Distinguish necessary inference from possibility.**

> **Test alternative interpretations.**

> **State what the evidence establishes.**

> **State what it does not establish.**

> **Apply only after meaning has been established.**

> **Remain correctable.**

> **Keep the Graphē above the interpreter.**

That is the discipline.

## 84.214 The Method Cannot Save the Interpreter From Error

A person can misuse a good method.

He can misunderstand evidence.

He can overlook a premise.

He can misclassify a passage.

He can make a grammatical mistake.

He can misunderstand history.

He can draw an invalid inference.

Therefore no method makes the human interpreter infallible.

The value of discipline is not that it eliminates human fallibility.

It reduces careless error and makes reasoning inspectable and correctable.

## 84.215 The Method Creates Accountability

A disciplined method makes it possible to ask:

> Where did the error enter?

Was perception wrong?

Was the definition wrong?

Was context ignored?

Was the lexical sense misapplied?

Was an inference invalid?

Was an application mistaken for meaning?

Was a secondary source promoted to primary authority?

This is one of the greatest advantages of disciplined interpretation.

It turns vague disagreement into examinable reasoning.

---

## 84.216 The Method Creates Correctability

If the work is visible, it can be corrected.

If the premises are visible, they can be tested.

If the inference is visible, it can be challenged.

If the source is visible, the conclusion can be returned to it.

This is why transparent scholarship is superior to unexplained assertion.

## 84.217 The Method Creates Reproducibility

The workman therefore does not merely announce:

> **“Here is the text, here is the reasoning, here is the conclusion, and here are its limits.”**

# Chapter 84 Conclusion

Biblical interpretation is not the freedom to make Scripture say whatever the reader prefers.

Neither is it the mechanical application of a formula that eliminates human judgment.

It is disciplined engagement with an authoritative written communication.

The interpreter must perceive.

He must learn.

He must understand.

He must reason.

He must compare.

He must distinguish.

He must infer.

He must test.

He must conclude.

He must know where to stop.

He must apply only after meaning has been established.

He must remain correctable.

He must preserve the boundary between:

> **what the Graphē says**

and:

> **what the interpreter concludes.**

He must know the difference between:

> **quotation and interpretation;**

> **interpretation and inference;**

> **inference and application;**

> **evidence and conclusion;**

> **possibility and necessity;**

> **tradition and Scripture;**

> **commentary and authority;**

> **method and source;**

> **skill and infallibility.**

He must understand that language matters.

Context matters.

Speaker matters.

Audience matters.

Subject matters.

Occasion matters.

Time matters.

Scope matters.

Conditions matter.

Qualifications matter.

Genre matters.

Comparison matters.

Reasoning matters.

Evidence matters.

But all these remain subordinate to the central question:

> **What does the Word of Truth establish?**

The disciplined workman does not approach the Graphē as an owner.

He approaches as a servant.

He does not approach as an editor.

He approaches as a reader.

He does not approach as a second source of revelation.

He approaches as one accountable for understanding what has been written.

He does not use scholarship to elevate himself above Scripture.

He uses scholarship to serve Scripture faithfully.

He does not use method to replace the Word.

He uses method to discipline his handling of the Word.

He does not use certainty to conceal weakness.

He uses proportionate language to represent what the evidence warrants.

He does not fear correction.

He welcomes correction when correction brings his interpretation closer to the source.

He does not seek victory over readers.

He seeks fidelity to truth.

He does not make the reader dependent upon his authority.

He forms the reader into a capable workman.

The whole discipline can therefore be stated in one governing sentence:

> **Biblical interpretation is the disciplined process by which the workman perceives, understands, examines, compares, reasons through, and applies the written Word while preserving the distinction between what the Graphē says and what the interpreter concludes.**

And the practical sequence is:

> **Perceive → Identify → Define → Contextualize → Understand → Compare → Distinguish → Reason → Infer → Test → Conclude → Limit → Apply.**

The authority remains with the Graphē.

The responsibility remains with the workman.

The method remains the servant.

The interpreter remains fallible.

The truth remains the objective.

And the accountability remains before God.

He has been made more responsible.

He has been taught to stand beneath it with greater skill.

He has not been given a seventh principle.

He has been brought to the threshold at which the Six First Principles can be approached without confusing the foundation with the things founded upon it.

The final question is now unavoidable:

> **From the Graphē, how does the prepared workman enter the Six First Principles without making the Doctrine of the Graphē itself a seventh principle?**

That is **Chapter 85 — From Graphē to the Six First Principles**.
