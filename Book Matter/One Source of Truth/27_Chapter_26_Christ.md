# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 26 — Jesus Christ

> **Movement IV — How the Written Record Was Given**

---

## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 25 established the prophetic mode of divine communication. The biblical record presents God as speaking through prophets whom He commissioned to receive and proclaim His word within history.

The investigation now reaches the decisive transition stated at the opening of Hebrews:

> **“God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son.”**

The movement is therefore not merely from one prophet to another:

> **God → prophets → Son**

Chapter 26 examines what the biblical record means by this decisive revelation.

## Central Objective

> **To establish from the biblical record that Jesus Christ occupies a unique and climactic position in divine revelation: He is not merely another prophet or messenger, but the Son through whom God has spoken, the Word made flesh, the historical person witnessed by those who encountered Him, and the central person around whom the later apostolic written testimony is formed.**

The chapter will therefore examine Christ's place in the transition from prophets to the Son; the biblical language of Son and Word; the incarnation; Christ's revelation of the Father; His historical manifestation; His teaching and works; His relationship to Scripture and prophecy; the witnesses who encountered Him; and the transition from Christ to apostolic testimony.

---

## 26.1 The Decisive Transition — Hebrews 1:1–2

Hebrews deliberately places two periods of divine communication side by side. God spoke in former times through the prophets; in these last days He has spoken through His Son.

This establishes both continuity and climax.

The Speaker is the same. The earlier prophets are not discarded. But the later revelation is presented as qualitatively different because the mediator is **the Son**.

Hebrews 1:2–3 immediately identifies the Son as heir of all things, the One through whom God made the worlds, the radiance of God's glory, the exact representation of His being, and the One who sustains all things.

The text therefore does not permit Christ to be treated simply as another entry in the prophetic succession.

---

## 26.2 “By the Prophets” and “By His Son”

Hebrews' parallel language is significant. God spoke through the prophets, but has spoken through the Son.

The distinction becomes clearer from the surrounding context. The prophets are commissioned human spokesmen. The Son is described in relation to creation itself and to God's own glory and being.

The biblical architecture is therefore:

> **Prophets — commissioned mediators of divine speech**
>
> **Son — the climactic revelation through whom God has spoken**

The distinction must remain intact throughout the argument.

---

## 26.3 The Son Is Not Merely Another Prophet

Christ can be called a prophet in certain Gospel contexts, and He certainly exercises a prophetic ministry. But the New Testament's highest claims concerning Him exceed the prophetic office.

John does not begin his Gospel by presenting Jesus simply as another prophet. He begins with the **Word**:

> **“In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.”**

Then:

> **“The Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us.”**

The movement is therefore from eternal identity to historical incarnation, not merely from one human prophet to another. John 1:1–14 presents the Word as divine, active in creation, and subsequently manifested in flesh.

---

## 26.4 The Word — λόγος (*logos*)

John uses the Greek noun **λόγος (*logos*)**, conventionally translated “Word.” Its meaning must be controlled primarily by John's own context rather than by importing a later philosophical system into the passage.

John's immediate claims are decisive. The Word exists “in the beginning,” is with God, is God, is associated with creation, and later becomes flesh.

The Word is therefore not merely an impersonal sound, written statement, or abstract proposition. John presents the Word as personal and divine.

---

## 26.5 The Word and Creation

John 1:3 states that all things were made through the Word.

This places Christ's identity before His earthly birth. The incarnation is not presented as the beginning of His existence. It is His entrance into human history in flesh.

The sequence is therefore:

> **Word → creation → incarnation → historical presence → human witness**

That sequence is foundational for understanding why Christ occupies a unique place in the Bible's doctrine of revelation.

---

## 26.6 “The Word Was Made Flesh”

John 1:14 states that the Word became flesh and dwelt among humanity. John immediately connects this incarnation with witness:

> **“We beheld his glory.”**

The revelation is therefore no longer described merely as information delivered by a messenger. The Word is presented as entering human existence.

This creates an extraordinary evidential consequence: the revelation is attached to a historical person who could be seen, heard, followed, questioned, opposed, touched, and remembered.

John's Gospel explicitly joins incarnation and witness: the Word became flesh, and the witnesses say that they saw His glory.

---

## 26.7 The Incarnation Makes Revelation Historical

The incarnation places the claim about Christ within identifiable human history.

Jesus has a mother, family, ancestry, geographic setting, contemporaries, disciples, opponents, public ministry, teaching, execution, and—according to the apostolic testimony—resurrection.

The claim is consequently not merely a proposition about an invisible spiritual experience.

It is a claim concerning a person who lived, taught, acted, suffered, died, and was proclaimed as risen.

Historical claims are therefore appropriate subjects of historical investigation.

---

## 26.8 John the Baptist — Witness to the Light

John 1 introduces John the Baptist as a man sent from God whose purpose was to bear witness to the Light.

This creates a fundamental distinction:

> **Christ — the Light**
>
> **John — the witness to the Light**

The witness is not the object of the witness.

John's role therefore anticipates the later apostolic structure: Christ is the historical and revelatory subject; human beings bear testimony concerning Him.

---

## 26.9 “We Beheld His Glory”

The phrase “we beheld his glory” is important because it is experiential testimony.

The Word became flesh.

Witnesses encountered Him.

Witnesses testify concerning Him.

This is the beginning of the evidential chain that later becomes explicit in apostolic writings.

The Christian proclamation is therefore presented as testimony concerning an encountered person, not merely as detached speculation.

---

## 26.10 Christ as the Revelation of the Father

Christ repeatedly describes His relationship to the Father. John 14:9 records:

> **“He that hath seen me hath seen the Father.”**

Hebrews 1:3 similarly presents the Son as the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of His being.

These passages establish a common point: Christ is presented as uniquely revealing God.

The prophet says what God has given him to say.

Christ is presented as the Son who uniquely manifests the Father.

---

## 26.11 Christ and Truth

John 1:14 describes the incarnate Word as full of grace and truth. John 14:6 records Christ's declaration:

> **“I am ... the truth.”**

The claim is therefore not merely that Jesus taught a collection of true propositions. The New Testament places Christ Himself in a unique relationship to truth.

This becomes important to the larger argument because the question is no longer merely, “What message did God send?” but also, “Who is the person through whom the decisive message is presented?”

---

## 26.12 Christ Speaks With Authority

The Gospels repeatedly portray Jesus teaching with distinctive authority. Matthew 7:28–29 contrasts His teaching with the scribes and emphasizes the authority with which He taught.

The distinction is also visible in His recurring expression:

> **“But I say unto you.”**

The prophet characteristically identifies the divine source with formulations such as “Thus saith the LORD.” Christ repeatedly speaks directly in His own authority.

This does not mean every statement attributed to Christ must be interpreted in isolation. It means the Gospel writers intentionally present His authority as distinctive.

---

## 26.13 Christ's Teaching Is Public

Jesus teaches in synagogues, towns, homes, the Temple, by the sea, on mountains, before disciples, crowds, and opponents.

His ministry therefore occurs publicly.

People hear Him. Some accept Him; others reject Him. Some follow Him; others oppose Him.

The Gospel presentation is consequently not a story of a private teacher communicating secret doctrines to one isolated individual.

---

## 26.14 Christ's Works Are Part of the Revelation

The Gospel writers do not separate Christ's words from His works. His actions function within the testimony concerning His identity.

John repeatedly describes miraculous acts as **signs**. A sign points beyond itself. John 20:30–31 explicitly states that the recorded signs are presented so that readers may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God.

The biblical structure is therefore:

> **Christ's identity → His words and works → witness → written testimony**

The later authentication chapters will examine what these signs establish and what additional historical reasoning is required.

---

## 26.15 Christ and the Scriptures

Jesus enters a story already shaped by the Hebrew Scriptures.

He appeals repeatedly to Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms. He reasons from Scripture, interprets Scripture, and identifies Scripture as bearing witness concerning Him.

This is essential to the continuity of the biblical record.

Christ does not appear as an unrelated religious figure who abandons the earlier written testimony. He presents His mission within the history and expectations already established by that testimony.

---

## 26.16 Christ's Use of Scripture

Christ appeals to Scripture in temptation, controversy, teaching, and the interpretation of His mission.

After His resurrection, Luke 24 presents Him explaining the things concerning Himself in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms.

The structure is:

> **Earlier written revelation → Christ → Christ's interpretation of that revelation → apostolic witness**

This establishes continuity between the earlier written record and the Christ-event.

---

## 26.17 Christ and Moses

John 5:46 records Christ saying that Moses wrote concerning Him.

Whatever detailed questions later arise concerning individual prophetic fulfillments, the immediate point is clear: Jesus presents Himself as belonging to the trajectory of the earlier Scriptures.

The biblical movement is:

> **Moses → prophetic witness → Christ**

The later revelation does not erase the earlier revelation. It claims to fulfill and clarify it.

---

## 26.18 Christ and the Prophets

The same pattern occurs throughout the Gospel accounts. Jesus repeatedly explains His mission through the Prophets.

The Old Testament prophetic corpus therefore forms part of the background against which His life is interpreted.

This matters to the doctrine of the Graphē because the Bible presents a developing written testimony rather than disconnected religious documents.

---

## 26.19 Fulfillment and Continuity

The biblical language of **fulfillment** is central to this relationship.

A prior Scripture is interpreted as pointing toward a later event or person; the Gospel presents Christ in relation to that earlier testimony.

The detailed evidential treatment of predictive prophecy belongs to Chapter 37. Here the narrower conclusion is sufficient:

> **The biblical record presents Christ as standing in continuity with, and as the fulfillment of, the earlier written revelation.**

---

## 26.20 Christ's Genealogy and Historical Location

Matthew begins with a genealogy. Luke also provides genealogical material. Matthew places Jesus within the history associated with Abraham and David.

These genealogies are important for the historical architecture because they locate Jesus within a people, family history, and covenantal narrative.

The incarnation is therefore not presented as an appearance without ancestry.

---

## 26.21 Bethlehem, Nazareth, Galilee, and Jerusalem

The Gospel narratives repeatedly locate Jesus in identifiable places: Bethlehem, Nazareth, Galilee, Capernaum, Judea, Jerusalem, the Mount of Olives, and other locations.

These geographical references anchor the narrative in a recognizable physical environment.

The claim is therefore geographically situated as well as chronologically situated.

---

## 26.22 Historical Rulers and the Christ Event

The Gospel narratives also identify political authorities associated with the period of Jesus' life, including Herod, Pontius Pilate, Philip, and Herod Antipas.

Luke 3:1–2 is particularly notable for anchoring the beginning of John the Baptist's ministry within the reigns and offices of named rulers and religious authorities.

The Christ-event is thus presented within public political history rather than in an undefined mythical past.

---

## 26.23 Christ Is Not Presented as a Mythological Abstraction

The Gospel presentation contains genealogies, named rulers, geographical places, public disputes, identifiable disciples, religious institutions, Roman authority, execution, and a sequence of events.

These features do not by themselves prove every theological claim made about Jesus. They do, however, establish the proper category of inquiry: the claims concerning Jesus include historical claims and are therefore capable of historical examination.

---

## 26.24 Christ's Human Experience

The Gospel record presents Jesus as genuinely human. He is born, grows, becomes hungry and tired, sleeps, travels, speaks, weeps, suffers, bleeds, and dies.

These details are integral to the incarnation claim.

The Word did not merely appear to be human in the narrative. The New Testament presents Him as having entered genuine human existence.

---

## 26.25 Christ's Divine Identity and Human History

At the same time, the New Testament does not present Jesus as merely an extraordinary human teacher.

John 1 identifies Him with the eternal Word. Hebrews 1 relates the Son to creation and divine glory. Philippians 2 and Colossians 1 likewise place Christ within categories far exceeding an ordinary human prophet.

The biblical presentation therefore joins:

> **divine identity + genuine human existence.**

This is the distinctive Christological center of the revelation claim.

---

## 26.26 Christ's Self-Claims

The Gospels report extraordinary claims concerning Christ's identity and authority, including claims concerning His relationship to the Father, His pre-existence, His authority over sin and judgment, and His relationship to life and resurrection.

The first historical question is not whether every claim is true. It is:

> **What does the Gospel testimony actually report Jesus as saying and doing?**

The answer is substantial and forms a central part of the later historical investigation.

---

## 26.27 “Before Abraham Was, I Am”

John 8:58 records Jesus saying:

> **“Before Abraham was, I am.”**

The statement occurs within a dispute concerning Abraham and forms part of John's wider presentation of Jesus' identity.

The point here is not to use one sentence as a complete proof of Christology. It is to establish that the Gospel reports Jesus making claims that reach beyond ordinary human prophetic authority.

---

## 26.28 Christ Claims Authority Over Life

John 11:25 records:

> **“I am the resurrection, and the life.”**

Again, this is a personal claim concerning Christ's relationship to resurrection and life, not merely a lesson about a doctrine.

The significance of the claim belongs to the cumulative Christological argument.

---

## 26.29 Christ Claims Authority to Forgive Sins

Mark 2:1–12 records Jesus telling a paralytic that his sins are forgiven.

The scribes recognize the significance of the claim and ask who can forgive sins but God.

Jesus then heals the man and connects the visible act with the authority He has claimed.

The narrative therefore contains a distinctive pattern:

> **claim → challenge → visible action → interpretation.**

The historical authenticity of the episode belongs to the later evidential investigation. Its importance here is the kind of authority the Gospel attributes to Jesus.

---

## 26.30 Christ's Opponents Are Part of the Historical Setting

The Gospel narratives include opponents who challenge Jesus, dispute His authority, accuse Him, and seek grounds against Him.

The narrative therefore does not depend upon universal agreement among Jesus' contemporaries.

The historical setting contains supporters, observers, opponents, authorities, and competing interpretations.

This is part of the context in which the testimony arose.

---

## 26.31 Christ Was Publicly Executed

The crucifixion is presented as a public event under Roman authority, with Pontius Pilate occupying the governing role in the Gospel narratives.

This places the death of Jesus firmly within the historical claim.

The crucifixion will receive much fuller treatment in the later historical investigation of the death and resurrection. Here it establishes the seriousness of the incarnation: the Word who became flesh entered the full reality of human suffering and death.

---

## 26.32 The Resurrection Claim

The New Testament then makes the central claim that Jesus was raised from the dead.

Paul summarizes the tradition in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 by identifying Christ's death, burial, resurrection, appearances, and witnesses.

The resurrection therefore becomes a historical claim with an identifiable testimony structure.

Its complete evidential examination belongs to the later dedicated chapters. At this stage, the important point is that the biblical revelation culminates in a historical claim about Christ's resurrection rather than ending at His death.

---

## 26.33 The Witnesses of the Resurrection

The New Testament identifies or names resurrection witnesses including Cephas/Peter, the Twelve, more than five hundred, James, the apostles, and Paul.

The claim is consequently not presented merely as a private spiritual feeling.

It is presented as an event witnessed and proclaimed by identifiable people.

This makes the next chapter indispensable.

---

---

## 26.35 Christ and the Apostles

Christ does not leave behind anonymous followers. The Gospel accounts identify particular disciples, and Acts 1 gives a striking definition of apostolic witness: the replacement for Judas must be someone who had accompanied Jesus and could become a witness to His resurrection.

This connects apostolic office with historical proximity.

The apostles are therefore not merely later interpreters appearing centuries afterward. They belong to the historical generation surrounding Christ.

---

## 26.36 Christ's Promise Concerning the Spirit

John 14–16 contains Christ's teaching concerning the Holy Spirit and the disciples. John 14:26 speaks of the Spirit teaching them and bringing Christ's words to their remembrance; John 16:13 speaks of the Spirit of truth guiding them.

These statements belong to the biblical account of how Christ's teaching would continue into apostolic proclamation.

The complete doctrine of inspiration belongs to Chapters 30–32. The present conclusion is narrower:

> **Christ Himself establishes a connection between His earthly teaching, His disciples, the Spirit, and the future proclamation of His message.**

---

## 26.37 Revelation 1 — “The Revelation of Jesus Christ”

Revelation 1:1 begins with:

> **“The Revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave unto him.”**

The verse then describes the communication as being sent through an angel to John.

The chain is explicit:

> **God → Jesus Christ → angel → John → servants**

This is important because it shows that the New Testament itself contains multiple levels of mediation.

It does not reduce Christ to an ordinary angelic messenger. Christ stands at the center of the revelatory chain.

---

## 26.38 Christ and the Earlier Mediators

The progression established by the preceding chapters can now be stated:

> **God → angels → people**
>
> **God → prophets → people**
>
> **God → Son**
>
> **God → Christ → angel → John → servants**

The biblical record contains multiple communicative pathways, but Christ occupies a unique position among the mediators.

---

## 26.39 Christ as the Center, Not Merely a Messenger

The cumulative biblical portrait does not permit Christ to be reduced to prophet, teacher, moral example, miracle worker, political figure, or messenger alone.

He is presented as the Son, the Word, the One through whom the worlds were made, the Word made flesh, the revelation of the Father, the Christ, and the Lord.

The complete theological implications will be developed throughout the later architecture. The present chapter establishes the unique category itself.

---

## 26.40 The Historical and Evidential Importance of the Incarnation

If the Word became flesh, divine revelation entered a form accessible to human historical observation.

People could encounter Jesus in ordinary human circumstances. They could hear His speech, observe His actions, dispute with Him, follow Him, reject Him, and testify about Him.

This is why the incarnation is central to the movement from revelation to historical testimony.

---

## 26.41 Multiple Witnesses

The New Testament does not present one solitary observer. It presents disciples and other witnesses who encountered Jesus.

First John 1:1–3 describes what was heard, seen, observed, and touched and connects those experiences with proclamation.

The structure is:

> **encounter → testimony → proclamation**

The message is therefore presented as testimony concerning an encountered person.

---

## 26.42 Different Witnesses, One Christ

Multiple witnesses do not need to produce identical literary accounts in order to testify concerning the same person.

Different witnesses may select different events, emphasize different sayings, arrange material differently, and write for different audiences while still reporting the same historical subject.

The proper question is therefore not whether all Gospel accounts are mechanically identical, but whether they provide testimony concerning the same Christ and the same central historical events.

Detailed questions of Gospel composition, harmony, and historical corroboration belong to later chapters.

---

## 26.43 Christ and the Written Testimony

The Gospels are written testimony about Christ.

This distinction is essential:

> **Christ is the historical subject.**
>
> **The witnesses are historical observers and proclaimers.**
>
> **The evangelists are writers of testimony.**

The written Gospel is therefore not Jesus personally holding the pen. It is preserved literary testimony concerning Him.

This distinction prepares the transition to apostolic testimony.

---

## 26.44 The Subject and the Witness

The architecture can therefore be stated:

> **Christ — historical subject**
>
> ↓
>
> **witnesses — historical observers**
>
> ↓
>
> **apostles — commissioned witnesses**
>
> ↓
>
> **proclamation**
>
> ↓
>
> **written testimony**

The written New Testament is fundamentally connected to what God has done and revealed in Christ.

---

## 26.45 Christ Does Not Depend on the Written Record for His Historical Existence

A necessary distinction must be maintained.

The Gospel writings are our principal surviving literary sources for the detailed narrative of Jesus' ministry, but the historical person preceded the written accounts.

The events occurred before they were written down. Witnesses encountered Christ before their testimony was committed to written form.

Thus:

> **event → witness → proclamation → writing**

is a historically intelligible sequence.

---

## 26.46 The Written Record Points Back to the Historical Person

The written testimony points backward:

> **text → testimony → events → Christ**

The text is therefore not the historical subject in isolation. The historical subject is Jesus Christ and the events associated with Him.

The written record preserves testimony concerning that subject.

This distinction becomes essential in Chapter 27.

---

## 26.47 Christ as the Fulfillment of the Prophetic Movement

The movement of Chapters 23–26 can now be summarized:

### Chapter 23

> **God speaks.**

### Chapter 24

> **God can communicate through mediators, including angels.**

### Chapter 25

> **God communicates through prophets.**

### Chapter 26

> **God has spoken by His Son.**

The sequence reaches its center.

The Son is not merely another messenger added to a chain. He is presented as the One toward whom the preceding revelation points and through whom the biblical record presents God's climactic self-disclosure.

---

## 26.48 Christ and the Unity of the Biblical Record

Christ provides a major point of continuity between the Old and New Testaments.

The earlier writings establish creation, humanity, covenant, Israel, kingship, prophecy, and expectation. The Gospel writings present Christ within that history. The apostles subsequently interpret His death and resurrection in relation to that earlier revelation.

The biblical record therefore possesses a historical center rather than a collection of unrelated religious documents.

---

## 26.49 The Divine-Human Pattern Reaches Its Center

Earlier chapters established that God communicates through human agents.

Christ presents a unique manifestation of that principle.

He is not merely another human messenger carrying a divine message. The New Testament presents the Word becoming flesh.

Thus the divine-human pattern reaches its center:

> **God communicates through human messengers.**
>
> **The Word becomes flesh.**

The latter is categorically more than the former.

---

## 26.50 Why This Matters for Scripture

The question of Scripture is ultimately inseparable from the question of what happened in Christ.

If Christ is merely an ordinary teacher, the New Testament's claims about Him require rejection or radical reinterpretation.

If Christ is the Son and Word presented in the New Testament, then His treatment of Scripture, His commissioning of witnesses, His promises concerning the Spirit, and the testimony of those witnesses become central to understanding the origin and authority of the Christian written record.

The investigation must therefore proceed from:

> **What does the Bible say about Christ?**

into:

> **What did Christ claim, what did He do, whom did He commission, and what testimony did those witnesses provide?**

---

# 26.51 Necessary Conclusion

The biblical record presents Jesus Christ as fundamentally different from the ordinary prophetic mediators examined in Chapter 25.

The prophets were commissioned human spokesmen.

Christ is presented as:

> **the Son**
>
> **the Word**
>
> **the One through whom God made the worlds**
>
> **the Word made flesh**
>
> **the revelation of the Father**
>
> **the historical person witnessed by His followers**

The necessary conclusion is therefore:

> **Christ is the decisive center of the biblical revelation claim because the Bible presents God's communication not merely as another message delivered through a human messenger, but as the Son and Word entering human history in the flesh.**

His historical life then becomes the foundation for a new evidential chain:

> **Christ → witnesses → apostolic testimony → proclamation → written testimony.**

# 26.53 What This Chapter Does Not Yet Establish

This chapter does not yet attempt to provide the complete historical case for Jesus' existence, the complete historical case for the resurrection, exhaustive authentication of every Gospel event, the complete doctrine of Christ's deity, the complete doctrine of incarnation, the complete doctrine of inspiration, the full question of Gospel composition, or the New Testament canon.

Those questions have their designated places later in the architecture.

The present chapter establishes Christ's biblical position within the movement from divine communication to historical witness.

---

# 26.54 Transition to Chapter 27

The prophets were God's commissioned spokesmen.

Christ is presented as the Son through whom God has spoken.

But Christ's earthly ministry did not end with His resurrection. He commissioned witnesses.

Those witnesses were not merely later admirers. They were appointed to testify concerning what they had seen and heard and, critically, concerning His resurrection.

The next question is therefore:

> **Who were the apostles, what were they commissioned to do, and what kind of testimony did they provide concerning Christ?**

# Methodological Guardrails

### 1. Do not reduce Christ to a prophet

The biblical record presents prophets and Christ within different categories.

### 2. Establish the textual claim before developing later theological conclusions

First establish what the texts say about Christ; then follow the implications.

### 3. Do not separate Christ from history

The incarnation places Christ within identifiable human history.

### 4. Do not confuse Christ with the Gospel writers

Christ is the subject of the Gospel testimony; the evangelists are the writers of that testimony.

### 5. Do not confuse event, witness, proclamation, and writing

These are distinct stages within the historical chain.

### 6. Do not treat the resurrection as established merely by assertion

The resurrection is a central historical claim whose detailed evidential examination belongs to the later dedicated treatment.

### 7. Preserve the cumulative textual witness

Major Christological conclusions should rest on the whole relevant textual corpus rather than one isolated passage.

### 8. Preserve Old Testament–Christ continuity

Christ is presented within the history and expectations established by the earlier Scriptures.

### 9. Preserve the Christ–apostle distinction

Christ is the historical subject and revelatory center. The apostles are commissioned witnesses to Him.

### 10. Preserve the distinction between revelation and written testimony

Christ is the incarnate Word. The Gospel writings are written testimony concerning Him.

# Final Doctrinal Statement

> **The biblical record presents Jesus Christ as the decisive center of divine revelation. Whereas God formerly spoke through prophets, Hebrews declares that He has spoken in these last days through His Son. John identifies this Son with the eternal Word who was with God and was God and who became flesh and dwelt among humanity. Christ therefore enters history not merely as another messenger but as the person in whom the biblical record presents God's revelation as uniquely manifested. His life, teaching, works, death, resurrection, and relationship to the earlier Scriptures become the historical foundation for the testimony of those whom He commissioned. The movement therefore proceeds from Christ to witnesses, from witnesses to apostolic proclamation, and from proclamation toward written testimony.**
