# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT I — TRUTH AND THE POSSIBILITY OF KNOWLEDGE

# Chapter 4 — Knowledge, Evidence, and Rational Warrant

> **Central question:** What is knowledge, what constitutes evidence, and what warrants a conclusion as rationally established?

Chapter 1 established truth as conformity to reality. Chapter 2 examined truth in relation to what actually is. Chapter 3 distinguished truth from error and showed why belief, sincerity, popularity, tradition, institutional status, and repetition cannot themselves determine truth.

The investigation now has to answer the epistemological and evidential questions that necessarily follow:

> **How can truth be known?**

and:

> **What warrants treating a conclusion as established?**

This chapter therefore moves from the **truth of a proposition** to the **grounds upon which a person may rationally know or accept that proposition**.

The distinction is fundamental:

> **Truth concerns whether a proposition corresponds to reality. Knowledge concerns whether the knower has reached and apprehended that truth in an adequately grounded way. Evidence concerns what bears upon the truth of a proposition. Rational warrant concerns whether the available grounds adequately support the conclusion.**

---

# 1. What Is Knowledge?

Knowledge is not merely possession of information.

A person may have heard a statement without knowing whether it is true.

A person may memorize a proposition without understanding it.

A person may repeat a correct sentence while being unable to explain what it means or why it is true.

Conversely, a person may know something without possessing a written quotation of it.

Knowledge therefore involves more than the existence of a statement in the mind.

At minimum, the investigation requires a distinction among:

- **information** — something communicated or available to the mind;
- **belief** — acceptance of a proposition;
- **understanding** — apprehension of what a proposition means and how its parts relate;
- **knowledge** — cognition that has a sufficiently grounded relationship to what is true.

The exact philosophical definition of knowledge has been debated extensively, but the present investigation does not need to settle every dispute in epistemology before making a practical distinction.

The crucial point is:

> **Knowledge must be connected to truth.**

A person cannot know a proposition merely because he believes it.

If the proposition is false, the person's belief may be mistaken, sincere, confident, traditional, or institutionally endorsed—but it is not knowledge of that proposition's truth.

Thus the previous chapters' distinction between truth and belief remains essential.

---

# 2. Knowledge Requires More Than Possessing Words

This distinction is particularly important for the study of Scripture.

A learner may possess the text of a biblical passage and still fail to understand it.

He may know the words but not:

- their grammatical relation;
- their historical setting;
- their immediate context;
- their connection with other statements;
- the proposition being asserted;
- the inference being made;
- or the conclusion that legitimately follows.

This does not make the words unimportant.

It demonstrates the opposite.

Because the written Word is meaningful, the learner must actually engage what is written.

The movement is:

> **Written words → apprehension → understanding → reasoning → judgment → application**

This is why later biblical instruction concerning learning, exercise, wisdom, understanding, study, and rightly dividing cannot be reduced to memorization.

The goal is not merely to possess Scripture physically or verbally.

The goal is to **know what is written and what it means**.

---

# 3. What Is Evidence?

Evidence is that which **bears upon a proposition by providing rational support for or against its truth**.

This definition is important because it prevents almost anything from being called evidence merely because someone regards it as persuasive.

Evidence has a relationship to a claim.

If the proposition is:

> “An event occurred,”

evidence must bear upon whether that event actually occurred.

If the proposition is:

> “A document is authentic,”

evidence must bear upon whether the document is genuinely what it purports to be.

If the proposition is:

> “A witness was present,”

evidence must bear upon the witness's presence.

If the proposition is:

> “This writing constitutes divine revelation,”

the evidence must bear upon the origin, reliability, authenticity, and divine character claimed for that writing.

Therefore:

> **Evidence is not simply information. It is information or factually relevant material considered in relation to a proposition.**

A fact becomes evidential when its relationship to the proposition is understood.

---

# 4. Not Everything Called Evidence Has Equal Force

Once evidence is defined, another question follows:

> **What makes evidence good evidence?**

Several characteristics are especially important.

## Relevance

Evidence must actually bear upon the proposition.

Evidence about one question cannot automatically establish another.

## Reliability

The evidential source or process must be sufficiently dependable.

A measurement from a reliable instrument and a random guess do not have equal evidential weight.

## Independence

Independent evidence can have greater force than repeated versions of the same dependent claim.

Ten people repeating one person's statement do not automatically provide ten independent witnesses.

## Specificity

Evidence that bears specifically upon the proposition can be stronger than evidence merely compatible with it.

## Sufficiency

Evidence must have enough force to support the conclusion being drawn.

One piece of evidence may establish a limited proposition without establishing a much larger one.

## Corroboration

Independent lines of evidence that converge on the same conclusion can strengthen the case.

## Coherence

Evidence should fit coherently with established facts rather than requiring unexplained contradictions.

## Explanatory power

A conclusion is strengthened when it explains the relevant evidence more adequately than competing explanations.

## Non-circularity

The evidence cannot simply assume the conclusion it is supposed to establish.

These criteria do not constitute a mechanical numerical formula.

They constitute disciplines for judgment.

---

# 5. Evidence Must Be Connected to the Proposition

This is where many arguments fail.

Suppose someone presents an ancient manuscript as evidence that a particular text existed in antiquity.

That may be relevant.

But it does not automatically establish:

- who originally wrote the text;
- whether every statement in it is true;
- whether the text is divinely inspired;
- or whether every interpretation of it is correct.

The manuscript may establish one proposition while leaving another proposition still requiring examination.

This gives us a critical rule:

> **Always ask: What exactly does this evidence establish?**

And immediately afterward:

> **What does it not establish?**

This discipline will become indispensable when the book later examines:

- manuscripts;
- preservation;
- prophecy;
- miracles;
- eyewitness testimony;
- apostolic testimony;
- Christ's testimony;
- historical correspondence;
- canonical recognition;
- and inspiration.

No evidential category should be asked to prove more than it actually bears upon.

---

# 6. Testimony Is Evidence

Testimony is an important form of evidence because human beings acquire knowledge through the reports of others.

We do not personally observe every historical event.

We do not personally witness every geographical fact.

We do not personally perform every scientific observation.

We therefore depend upon testimony.

The existence of testimony does not make a proposition true.

Rather, testimony can provide evidence concerning whether a proposition is true.

The investigator consequently asks:

- Who is testifying?
- What did the witness actually observe?
- Was the witness in a position to know?
- Is the witness reporting direct observation or inference?
- Is the account contemporaneous or remote?
- Is the witness reliable?
- Is there corroboration?
- Are there independent witnesses?
- Is there evidence against the testimony?
- Does the testimony cohere with established facts?

This is not skepticism toward testimony.

It is the rational evaluation of testimony.

---

# 7. Eyewitness Testimony Has a Distinct Evidential Character

An eyewitness account is different from a philosophical conjecture.

If a person says:

> “I witnessed this event,”

the proposition can be examined historically.

The witness may be mistaken.

The witness may be deceptive.

The witness may be accurate.

Those possibilities must be investigated.

This distinction becomes especially significant when examining the apostolic record.

Peter writes:

> **“For we have not followed cunningly devised fables, when we made known unto you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but were eyewitnesses of his majesty.”**  
> — 2 Peter 1:16

The statement is not merely a theological proposition.

It is also a claim about **how the knowledge being communicated was acquired**.

Peter distinguishes what he is communicating from “cunningly devised fables” and appeals to eyewitness experience.

The proper evidential question is therefore not merely:

> “Does Peter claim eyewitness status?”

It is:

> **What historical evidence bears upon Peter's testimony, the apostolic witness, the events reported, and the reliability of the record preserving that testimony?**

That larger question belongs to later chapters.

But the epistemological significance is already clear:

> **The biblical record contains testimony claims that are themselves evidential propositions capable of historical examination.**

---

# 8. Direct Observation and Inference Must Be Distinguished

A witness may say:

> “I saw the man enter the building.”

That is a report of observation.

If the witness says:

> “Therefore he intended to steal something,”

the second statement is an inference.

The witness may be justified in making that inference, but the inference is not identical to the observation.

This distinction matters enormously in historical and biblical investigation.

A writer may record:

- what happened;
- what someone said happened;
- what a witness understood;
- or what the writer inferred from an event.

These must not be collapsed.

The investigator should therefore ask:

> **What was observed?**

then:

> **What was reported?**

then:

> **What was inferred?**

then:

> **Does the inference follow?**

This distinction protects both evidence and reasoning.

---

# 9. Rational Warrant Is Not the Same as Certainty

A rationally warranted conclusion is a conclusion supported by adequate grounds.

That does not mean every rational conclusion must have the same logical form or the same degree of certainty.

Some conclusions follow deductively.

If:

1. All men are mortal.
2. Socrates is a man.

then:

3. Socrates is mortal.

The conclusion follows necessarily if the premises are true.

Other conclusions are historical or abductive.

For example, a convergence of eyewitness testimony, physical evidence, documentary evidence, and independent corroboration may rationally establish that an historical event occurred without functioning as a mathematical deduction.

The important distinction is:

> **Different kinds of claims require appropriate kinds of evidence and reasoning.**

It would be irrational to demand mathematical demonstration for every historical proposition.

It would be equally irrational to treat weak historical suggestion as if it were mathematical certainty.

Rational warrant therefore requires **proportionality between the evidence, the inference, and the conclusion**.

---

# 10. What Makes a Conclusion Warranted?

A conclusion is rationally warranted when the grounds offered for it are sufficiently connected to the proposition and strong enough, taken together, to justify accepting it.

A useful analytical structure is:

> **Claim → Grounds → Connection → Inference → Conclusion**

For example:

### Claim

The document is historically authentic.

### Grounds

There are relevant manuscript, historical, linguistic, transmission, and contextual indicators.

### Connection

Those indicators bear upon whether the document is genuinely ancient and whether the text has been transmitted from the claimed period.

### Inference

If the evidence is sufficiently reliable, independent, and convergent, it increases the rational warrant for historical authenticity.

### Conclusion

The document may therefore be regarded as historically authentic to the degree established by the evidence.

Notice what has not happened.

We have not simply said:

> “There are manuscripts, therefore everything in the document is true.”

The conclusion is limited to what the evidence supports.

This discipline will prevent the book from making exaggerated evidential claims later.

---

# 11. Evidence Can Be Cumulative

Some propositions cannot be adequately assessed through one isolated piece of evidence.

Historical authenticity is often cumulative.

Consider several independent categories:

- manuscript evidence;
- historical references;
- linguistic evidence;
- archaeological correspondence;
- eyewitness testimony;
- independent testimony;
- fulfilled predictions;
- internal coherence;
- external corroboration.

One category may establish one part of the case.

Another may establish another.

When multiple independent lines converge, the cumulative case may become substantially stronger.

This is not the fallacy of simply adding numbers.

The question is whether the lines of evidence are genuinely independent and whether they converge upon the same explanation.

Thus:

> **Cumulative evidence is strongest when distinct evidential lines independently bear upon the same conclusion.**

This principle will later govern the investigation of the Bible's authenticity.

---

# 12. Evidence and the Burden of Proof

The person making a positive claim carries a responsibility to provide grounds for the claim.

If someone asserts:

> “This manuscript is authentic,”

he should be prepared to explain why.

If someone asserts:

> “This manuscript is fraudulent,”

he likewise bears the responsibility of explaining why.

The proper intellectual method is not:

> “Prove me wrong.”

It is:

> **“What evidence supports the proposition?”**

The burden can shift as evidence accumulates.

For example, once strong evidence establishes a fact, a person proposing an alternative explanation must provide grounds for that alternative.

This prevents an investigator from dismissing established evidence merely by imagining an unsupported possibility.

The existence of a logically conceivable alternative does not automatically make it a rationally competitive alternative.

---

# 13. Possibility Is Not Probability

Another essential distinction is between:

> **possible**

and:

> **probable**.

Something can be logically possible without being a reasonable explanation of the evidence.

Suppose someone says:

> “It is possible that every witness independently fabricated the same event.”

The mere logical possibility does not establish that this explanation is plausible.

The question is:

> **What evidence supports that explanation?**

Likewise, an explanation that is theoretically possible but requires numerous unsupported assumptions may have less explanatory adequacy than an explanation that naturally accounts for the evidence.

This distinction is especially important when alternative explanations are proposed against cumulative evidence.

The investigator must compare explanations, not merely enumerate possibilities.

---

# 14. Rational Warrant Must Not Be Circular

Circular reasoning occurs when the conclusion is effectively assumed in the grounds offered to establish it.

For example:

> “This proposition is true because the proposition itself says that it is true.”

That does not independently establish the proposition.

Likewise:

> “This book is authoritative because the book says it is authoritative.”

may identify the book's **claim to authority**, but it does not by itself provide the complete authentication of that claim.

This distinction is fundamental to the architecture of *One Source of Truth*.

The book will eventually examine the Bible's own claims about:

- divine communication;
- inspiration;
- Scripture;
- truth;
- authority;
- and revelation.

But those claims will be examined **together with the evidential and historical grounds relevant to their authenticity**.

The Bible's claim is evidence of what the Bible claims.

It is not, by itself, the entire external authentication of the claim.

This is why the investigation requires a cumulative argument.

---

# 15. But Non-Circularity Does Not Mean “Ignore the Source's Own Testimony”

Avoiding circularity does not mean refusing to consider what a document says about itself.

That would create another error.

A witness's statement about his own experience is relevant evidence concerning what he claims to have experienced.

A document's internal claims are relevant evidence concerning its authorship, purpose, claims, and conceptual coherence.

A prophetic text's predictions are relevant evidence when later historical events can be independently examined against them.

A biblical writer's claim to have witnessed an event is relevant evidence concerning the writer's asserted basis of knowledge.

The proper question is:

> **What evidential weight should the source's own testimony receive, and what independent or convergent evidence bears upon it?**

Thus:

> **Internal testimony is evidence; it should neither be treated as worthless nor automatically treated as conclusive.**

This is a much stronger method than either blind acceptance or automatic dismissal.

---

# 16. The Bible's Evidential Case Must Eventually Be Examined in This Way

The earlier source material identifies numerous evidential categories relevant to the Bible's authenticity and inspiration, including:

- extraordinary unity;
- doctrinal and moral superiority;
- historical correspondence;
- preservation;
- fulfilled prophecy;
- miracles;
- internal claims of divine communication;
- Christ's confirmation of the Old Testament;
- apostolic testimony;
- eyewitness testimony;
- and extensive New Testament use of the Old Testament.

These categories should not be presented as a decorative list.

Each must be assigned a precise evidential question.

For example:

### Unity

Does the remarkable coherence of the writings constitute evidence relevant to the nature and origin of the corpus?

### Prophecy

Can claimed predictions be identified sufficiently in advance and compared with independently established subsequent events?

### Miracles

What evidence establishes that the reported event occurred, and what inference follows concerning its significance?

### Eyewitness testimony

What evidence bears upon the presence, credibility, and testimony of the witnesses?

### Christ

What historical evidence establishes His existence, claims, works, death, resurrection, and treatment of Scripture?

### Preservation

What does manuscript and transmission evidence establish about the preservation of the written record?

### Canon

What historical evidence explains the recognition of the writings constituting the authoritative corpus?

### Internal claims

What do the writings claim concerning their own origin, and how do those claims interact with the external evidence?

This is how evidence becomes argument.

---

# 17. Knowledge Is Not Reduced to Quotation

The distinction developed here also clarifies an issue that will recur throughout the book.

A quotation can be **knowledge-bearing**.

If I accurately quote a true proposition from a reliable record, the quotation can communicate genuine knowledge.

Therefore it would be wrong to say:

> “Quotation is not knowledge.”

The correct distinction is:

> **Quotation can transmit knowledge; quotation alone does not demonstrate that the person quoting it understands everything that follows from it.**

For example, a student may correctly quote:

> **“Thy word is truth.”**

That quotation is not inferior because it is a quotation.

But if the student cannot explain:

- what “thy word” refers to in context;
- what “truth” means;
- what proposition Christ is making;
- why the statement matters;
- how it relates to the surrounding argument;
- and what conclusions legitimately follow,

then the student possesses the words without necessarily possessing the corresponding understanding.

Thus:

> **Quotation can preserve and communicate truth. Understanding requires apprehending the meaning and relations of what is quoted.**

This is why later intellectual labour does not compete with Scripture.

It serves Scripture.

---

# 18. Knowledge Requires Apprehension, Learning, and Reasoning

The investigation therefore reaches a more complete conception of learning.

The learner must:

### Perceive

Receive the information accurately.

### Learn

Acquire and retain what has been communicated.

### Understand

Comprehend what the information means.

### Compare

Relate one proposition to another.

### Distinguish

Separate things that are different and identify relevant categories.

### Connect

Recognize relationships among propositions.

### Reason

Move from premises toward conclusions.

### Judge

Determine whether the conclusion is warranted.

### Exercise

Put understanding into practice.

This is not a rejection of memorization or quotation.

It is the development of what quotation makes possible.

The written Word gives the learner something definite to know.

Reason enables the learner to apprehend and work through what has been written.

---

# 19. The Difference Between Evidence and Authority

Another distinction must be preserved.

**Evidence** supports a proposition.

**Authority** concerns the right or basis by which a source, person, or record governs judgment.

A witness may provide evidence.

A document may provide evidence.

A historical event may provide evidence.

None of those categories should automatically be confused with ultimate authority.

This distinction will become critical later.

The question:

> “What evidence authenticates Scripture?”

is not identical to:

> “What makes Scripture authoritative?”

The first concerns the grounds for recognizing the record as genuine and trustworthy.

The second concerns the nature and source of its authority.

The two questions are related but not identical.

A genuine document can be authoritative because of what it is.

Authentication establishes whether the document genuinely possesses the characteristics claimed for it.

Authority concerns the significance of those characteristics.

This distinction prevents a major category error.

---

# 20. Why Rational Warrant Matters Before Ultimate Authority

The next major question in the book is:

> **What can finally govern judgment when competing claims exist?**

That question cannot be answered responsibly until we understand warrant.

Suppose two documents make incompatible claims.

Suppose two institutions teach incompatible doctrines.

Suppose two interpreters offer incompatible readings of the same passage.

Which one should govern?

The investigator must have a method for determining:

- which claim is true;
- which evidence is stronger;
- which testimony is more reliable;
- which inference is valid;
- which interpretation actually follows;
- and which authority has legitimate grounds for governing the question.

Therefore rational warrant is not an optional philosophical exercise.

It is necessary for determining whether a claimed authority deserves recognition.

---

# 21. The Epistemological Chain

The argument can now be expressed as a chain:

> **Reality**
>
> ↓
>
> **Truth**
>
> ↓
>
> **Evidence**
>
> ↓
>
> **Perception / testimony / information**
>
> ↓
>
> **Analysis**
>
> ↓
>
> **Inference**
>
> ↓
>
> **Rational warrant**
>
> ↓
>
> **Knowledge / justified judgment**

Each stage has a distinct function.

Reality is what actually is.

Truth is conformity to reality.

Evidence bears upon the truth of a proposition.

Perception and testimony provide ways information reaches the investigator.

Analysis examines the information.

Inference connects premises to conclusions.

Rational warrant evaluates whether those grounds adequately support the conclusion.

Knowledge concerns the resulting cognitive grasp of what is true.

This chain is not intended as a simplistic universal formula. It is an organizing model for disciplined investigation.

---

# 22. What This Means for the Coming Investigation of God and Scripture

The next movement will ask what grounds truth and whether truth has an ultimate foundation.

That investigation cannot simply begin:

> “God is the ground of truth because the Bible says so.”

The proposition may ultimately be established through the biblical record, but the reasoning must show how the conclusion is reached.

The book must distinguish:

> **the existence of truth**

from:

> **our knowledge of truth**

and:

> **the ultimate ground of truth**

and:

> **the evidence by which we know that ground.**

Likewise, when the investigation reaches the Bible, it must distinguish:

> **what the Bible claims**

from:

> **why the Bible's claim is credible**

from:

> **what evidence authenticates the record**

from:

> **what follows concerning divine revelation**

from:

> **what follows concerning inspiration and infallibility**

from:

> **what follows concerning the authority of the Graphē.**

That is the cumulative architecture of the book.

---

# 23. Chapter Conclusion

The question of truth cannot be separated from the question of knowledge.

If truth concerns reality, then human beings require reliable means of apprehending reality.

Evidence provides rational support for propositions.

Testimony can transmit knowledge of events beyond one's personal observation.

Reason connects evidence and premises to conclusions.

Rational warrant concerns whether those grounds adequately support the conclusion.

Knowledge therefore cannot be reduced to mere possession of words, belief, quotation, institutional agreement, or personal confidence.

At the same time, quotation must not be disparaged. A true quotation can transmit genuine knowledge. The intellectual task is to **understand what has been quoted, determine what it establishes, reason through its implications, and distinguish what follows from what does not follow**.

This gives the investigation its next necessary step.

If evidence can warrant conclusions, then the investigator must now ask:

> **How does the mind move from evidence and established premises to a conclusion—and what makes that movement rationally valid?**

That is the subject of Chapter 5:

# Reason, Inference, and Ratiocination

## Evidential disciplines

For major evidential claims, ask:

1. **What proposition is being established?**
2. **What evidence bears upon it?**
3. **Is the evidence relevant?**
4. **Is it reliable?**
5. **Is it independent?**
6. **Is it sufficient?**
7. **Does it corroborate other evidence?**
8. **What inference connects the evidence to the conclusion?**
9. **Is the inference valid or adequately probable?**
10. **What does the evidence establish?**
11. **What does it not establish?**
12. **What competing explanation must be considered?**

## Biblical and historical categories reserved for later development

The chapter deliberately prepares, rather than exhausts:

- eyewitness testimony;
- apostolic testimony;
- divine manifestations;
- prophecy;
- miracles;
- Christ's historical testimony;
- resurrection evidence;
- manuscript evidence;
- preservation;
- canon;
- inspiration;
- biblical authenticity;
- Christ's confirmation of Scripture.

These remain later evidential chapters because the locked architecture requires the book to establish the **method for evaluating evidence before presenting the cumulative case**.

## Authoring Guardrails

> **Do not merely name evidence. Explain what proposition the evidence bears upon and why.**

> **Do not treat every piece of evidence as equally strong. Examine relevance, reliability, independence, sufficiency, corroboration, and inferential connection.**

> **Do not confuse the truth of a proposition with the warrant a person has for accepting it.**

> **Do not confuse quotation with ignorance. Accurate quotation can transmit genuine knowledge. The issue is whether the learner understands what has been quoted and what legitimately follows from it.**

> **Do not confuse evidence with authority. Authentication and authority are related but distinct questions.**

> **Do not use circular reasoning to establish the authority of the very source whose authority is being investigated.**

> **Do not respond to an evidential argument merely by inventing a logical possibility. Compare competing explanations according to evidence and explanatory adequacy.**

> **Do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture. Prepare the reader to reason through Scripture.**
