# 62.3 Define the Question: The Exact Statement and “Broken”

**The exact statement**, in this chapter, is the clause Christ asserts in John 10:35:

> **“…and the scripture cannot be broken…”**

In the received Greek of the verse the clause is:

> **…καὶ οὐ δύναται λυθῆναι ἡ γραφή…**

The statement is easily abused. In ordinary speech “the scripture cannot be broken” may mean a preacher’s motto, a claim that no manuscript can perish, a boast that every later tradition is thereby certified, a psychological remark that opponents will fail to refute a text, or a claim that Chapters 63–65 are already finished because a clause exists. None of those senses is this chapter’s object.

Four elements belong to the definition.

First, **the exact statement**. The object is the clause as spoken in the unit, not a later expansion, not a poster-fragment, and not a chain of neighbouring verses offered as substitutes for this sentence. Neighbouring uses of λύω will illuminate range. They will not replace the statement.

Second, **“broken” in its argumentative context**. The object is what λυθῆναι names as the argument uses it in this dispute: what cannot be done to the scripture here, such that the written lesser case remains standing and the appeal is not evacuated. Context is the Dedication blasphemy-trial already located, the citation already heard, the a fortiori already followed. Context is not a license to invent a setting the record does not give.

Third, **as Christ’s assertion of the Graphē’s standing in the court He has opened**. The statement is not a later church’s slogan attached to His name. It is His assertion, in the dispute, about ἡ γραφή. Standing, in this chapter, is the writing’s force as court-material that may not be loosed. Standing so defined is not yet the dedicated why, and it is not yet the Graphē-gate.

Fourth, **not those other claims**. The definition excludes, as not this object: the dedicated why from what Scripture is; John 17:17 as finished Graphē-doctrine; the Graphē-gate; a retry of Chapter 59’s scene; a retry of Chapter 60’s psalm; a retry of Chapter 61’s logic as if the statement had no remaining sense to examine; a claim that every later copy is physically unbreakable; a grant that every later interpreter’s use of a verse inherits this standing. Thus:

> **The question of this chapter is not whether John 10:34–36 is an argument from writing, what Psalm 82 argues, how the a fortiori is drawn, or why Scripture cannot be broken as a finished doctrine from what Scripture is. # 62.7 The Exact Statement Defined

**The exact statement**, controlled as wording rather than as a later expansion, is this:

> **“the scripture cannot be broken.”**

The statement has a subject, a modal verb of incapacity, and an infinitive of the act denied.

The **subject** is ἡ γραφή — the scripture, the writing already named in biblical usage as Holy Writ. In this argument the writing is the cited Scripture: the wording of Psalm 82:6 treated as written in their law, characterized as a calling of those to whom the word of God came. The subject is not the interpreter. The subject is not a later English volume as object. The subject is the writing.

The **incapacity** is οὐ δύναται — is not able. δύναμαι (*dynamai*, G1410) is to be able, to have power. With οὐ the clause denies ability. The denial is predicated of the writing: the scripture is not able to be subjected to the act named by the infinitive. The grammar is not first a command to the opponents, though it bars what they would have to do to sustain the charge. It is first an assertion about the writing.

The **act denied** is λυθῆναι — to be loosed, undone, broken, in the sense the next sections will control. The infinitive is aorist passive. The writing is the patient of the denied act.

The **conjunction** is καί — “and.” Chapter 61 already located this καί as joining the standing-clause to the condition as a parenthetical of force inside the sentence that reaches verse 36’s application. This chapter uses that location: the statement is not a new discourse. It is the asserted character of the writing being used as case.

The exact statement is therefore not three English words chosen for a motto. It is a Greek clause with inspectable joints, spoken as part of an argument already reconstructed. A motto can be moved to any controversy. This clause occupies this controversy: between “unto whom the word of God came” and “Say ye of him… Thou blasphemest.”

Two false substitutes must be refused at the definition.

**The statement as a later creed-sentence.** Later theology may formulate “Scripture is unbreakable.” Formulation may be accurate or inaccurate. The present object is His clause in His argument. The later creed-sentence, if it is true, will have to answer to this clause. It does not replace this clause.

**The statement as a heap of neighbouring verses.** Psalm 119, Proverbs 30:5–6, Isaiah 40:8, Matthew 5:18, and 1 Peter 1:25 are writings that bear upon the standing of God’s word. They are not this statement. Neighbouring illumination will be used as illumination. Substitution of a heap for the clause would be the proof-texting the methodology forbids.

Thus:

> **The exact statement is οὐ δύναται λυθῆναι ἡ γραφή, asserted of the writing in the argument of John 10:35–36. Subject, incapacity, and denied act are the joints. A motto and a heap are not the statement.**

# 62.8 “Broken” Defined for This Argument

**“Broken,”** in this chapter, is the English rendering of λυθῆναι in John 10:35, controlled by lexical range, grammatical form, Johannine and related biblical usage, and — decisively — the argumentative context of the Dedication blasphemy-dispute.

The definition for this argument is this:

> **To “break” the scripture here is to loose it, undo it, dissolve its force, annul it, or set it aside as court-material, so that the written case no longer stands as binding premise and the appeal from that case is evacuated.**

What cannot be done, therefore, is that act. The scripture cannot be treated as a disposable illustration, a dead relic, an optional colour around stones, or a wording that may be voided in order to keep the charge alive.

Four elements belong to this definition.

First, **an act upon the writing**. λυθῆναι is something that would be done to ἡ γραφή. The statement is not first a remark about the opponents’ feelings, the crowd’s noise, or the physical weather of the porch. It is about the writing.

Second, **force, not mere physical fracture as the governing image**. English “broken” easily suggests a snapped object. Greek λύω easily includes loosing what was bound, dissolving what stood, annulling what was in force. In a dispute whose first word is “Is it not written in your law,” the governing image is the loosing of a written standing, not the snapping of a scroll as artefact.

Third, **so as to evacuate the appeal**. The sense is not an abstract property floating free of verses 34–36. It is the sense required by the a fortiori already followed: if the lesser naming may be loosed at will, it cannot force the application in verse 36. The statement denies that loosing.

Fourth, **not the dedicated why**. The definition says what cannot be done. It does not, as this file’s achievement, say why the writing has that character from the doctrine of what Scripture is. Why remains Chapter 63. Defining the act denied is not answering that why.

Two further English traps must be named and refused.

**“Broken” as “incorrect.”** Modern English may say a statement is “broken” meaning faulty, failed, or untrue. That is not this clause. He does not say the scripture cannot be false as a later inerrancy-slogan detached from the dispute, though Movement VII has already warranted the record’s truthfulness as writing. He says the scripture cannot be λυθῆναι. The argumentative force is standing that cannot be loosed, not a modern idiom of malfunction.

**“Broken” as “sadly unheeded.”** A later piety may hear “cannot be broken” as “ought not to be ignored.” Ought is not cannot. The clause is οὐ δύναται, not οὐ δεῖ. He asserts incapacity of the writing to be so treated, not merely a moral preference that it not be so treated. Preference would not make the lesser case non-negotiable. Incapacity of the writing to be loosed does.

Thus:

> **“Broken” in this argument is the loosing, undoing, annulling, or setting aside of the writing’s force as court, such that the appeal would be evacuated. It is not a snapped artefact as the governing sense, not “incorrect,” and not a mere “ought.”**

# 62.9 English “Broken” and Greek λύω Distinguished

The investigation must not let an English word silently govern a Greek clause.

**Broken**, in English, commonly names fracture: a bone, a seal, a contract, a law, a promise. The legal uses — broken law, broken covenant — already approach the argumentative sense. The physical uses — broken jar, broken scroll — do not. A translation is not a crime. A translation that is then read only as physics becomes a capture.

**λύω (*lyō*, G3089)** has a range the next section will exhibit: to loose what is bound; to unbind; to set free; to undo; to dissolve; to destroy; to break up; to repeal or violate a commandment or a standing ordinance. The English “broken” in John 10:35 is one rendering of that range in the passive. It is not the whole range, and it is not a license to pick the most convenient English picture.

The methodological rule already in force is:

> **Original script → transliteration → lexical form → Strong’s number where relevant → basic lexical meaning → grammatical form → biblical usage → contextual meaning → argumentative significance.**

Lexical meaning is not yet contextual meaning. Contextual meaning is not yet a later theological slogan. The present file will walk that sequence for λυθῆναι and then return to the dispute as the control.

Honesty about rendering requires a further distinction. Other English versions may say “cannot be annulled,” “cannot be set aside,” “cannot be undone.” Those renderings are not rival scriptures. They are attempts to name the same Greek infinitive. This chapter does not select a commercial version as authority. It uses the received English “cannot be broken” as the locked title’s wording, and it controls that wording by the Greek and by the argument. Where “annulled” or “set aside” illuminates the argumentative sense, those English words are used as exposition, not as a replacement canon.

Thus:

> **English “broken” is the received rendering. Greek λύω is the verb. The argument, not a physical picture of fracture, controls which stretch of the range is in force here.**

# 62.10 Lexical Control of λύω (G3089)

The preferred lexical sequence may now be performed for the verb of the statement.

**Original script.** λύω

**Transliteration.** *lyō*

**Lexical form.** Present active infinitive stem as the lemma; the form in John 10:35 is λυθῆναι.

**Strong’s.** G3089.

**Basic lexical meaning.** To loose; to unbind; to undo; to dissolve; to destroy; to break up; to set free; by extension, to repeal, violate, or annul what stands as bound — a commandment, an ordinance, a law.

**Grammatical form in John 10:35.** λυθῆναι is the aorist passive infinitive. Aorist names the act as such, not a colourless present process. Passive names the writing as the patient of the act. Infinitive, under οὐ δύναται, names the act as that of which the writing is not capable of being the patient.

**Biblical usage**, sampled as range, not as a catalogue offered in place of John 10.

The verb looses what is bound in ordinary action: sandals (Mark 1:7); an animal on the Sabbath (Luke 13:15); Lazarus’s graveclothes (John 11:44). Those uses establish the root image: what was fastened is unfastened.

The verb destroys or dissolves what stood as a structure: “Destroy this temple” — λύσατε τὸν ναὸν τοῦτον (John 2:19); the middle wall of partition (Ephesians 2:14); the works of the devil (1 John 3:8); the heavens and elements in 2 Peter 3:10–12. Those uses establish that λύω can name demolition.

The verb looses in the legal and ordinance-sense that will prove nearest this dispute. John 5:18: He “had broken the sabbath” — ἔλυεν τὸ σάββατον. John 7:23: circumcision on the Sabbath is performed “that the law of Moses should not be broken” — ἵνα μὴ λυθῇ ὁ νόμος Μωϋσέως. Matthew 5:19: “whosoever therefore shall break one of these least commandments” — ὃς ἐὰν οὖν λύσῃ μίαν τῶν ἐντολῶν τούτων τῶν ἐλαχίστων. The related compound καταλύω in Matthew 5:17 names destroying or abolishing the Law and the Prophets. Those uses establish that written or standing ordinance can be the object of λύω: to break a commandment is to loose its force as binding, to treat it as not standing.

**Contextual meaning in John 10:35.** The object is ἡ γραφή. The setting is “your law.” The function is the standing of a written case used to try a capital charge. The nearest stretch of the range is therefore the ordinance-sense: to loose, annul, or set aside the writing as binding court-material. Demolition of a building is in the range and is not the governing picture here. Unbinding a sandal is in the range and is not the governing picture here. The sandal-uses teach the root. The law-uses teach the application.

**Argumentative significance.** If ἡ γραφή could be λυθῆναι in this sense, Psalm 82:6 could be treated as not standing, and verse 36 would have no written premise that must be answered. The statement denies that possibility as a possibility about the writing.

Lexical control illuminates. It does not replace the argument. A lexicon cannot, by itself, finish Chapter 63, and it cannot, by itself, convert this file into John 17:17.

Thus:

> **λύω ranges from loosing what is bound to dissolving what stands, including the annulling of commandment and law. In John 10:35 the contextual stretch is that ordinance-sense applied to ἡ γραφή: the writing cannot be loosed as standing court.**

# 62.11 Grammatical Control: οὐ δύναται λυθῆναι ἡ γραφή

Grammar is not a later system imposed upon piety. It is the statement’s own joints.

**ἡ γραφή.** Nominative singular, articular. The writing is the subject of δύναται. Chapters 20–22 already established the term. This chapter needs the grammatical fact: what “cannot” is said of is the scripture, not first the opponents, not first the stones, not first a later copyist.

**οὐ δύναται.** Third person singular present: is not able. The present of δύναται with this infinitive asserts a standing incapacity, not a momentary mood. He does not say that on this winter day, in this porch, the writing happens to be unbroken. He says it cannot be broken.

**λυθῆναι.** Aorist passive infinitive completing δύναται. The construction “is not able to be λυθῆναι” names an impossibility of the writing’s being subjected to that act. The aorist does not require a reconstructed timeline of a future snapping. It names the act as such.

**καί.** The clause is joined, not isolated. Grammar agrees with argument: parenthetical of force inside the sentence, not a new title.

What the grammar forbids is as important as what it states.

It forbids hearing the clause as first a psychological report: “they will not succeed in breaking it,” as if the subject were the opponents’ competence. The subject is ἡ γραφή.

It forbids hearing the clause as first a command: “do not break it,” as if the mood were imperative. The mood is indicative of incapacity.

It forbids hearing the clause as first a wish: “may it not be broken.” He asserts.

It does not, by itself, finish the why. Grammar can say that incapacity is asserted of the writing. Grammar cannot, in this file, consume Chapter 63’s account of that incapacity from what Scripture is.

Thus:

> **The grammar of John 10:35b asserts, in the indicative, that the writing is not able to be subjected to λυθῆναι. The subject is ἡ γραφή. The mood is not imperative, not optative, and not a report of the opponents’ skill.**

# 62.12 Neighbouring Johannine Uses: John 7:23 and John 5:18

The Fourth Gospel uses λύω of standing ordinance before it uses λυθῆναι of ἡ γραφή. Those uses are illumination. They are not a second Dedication-scene, and they are not this statement under another name.

## 62.12.1 John 7:23 — that the law of Moses should not be broken

> **“If a man on the sabbath day receive circumcision, that the law of Moses should not be broken; are ye angry at me, because I have made a man every whit whole on the sabbath day?”**
> — John 7:23

The purpose clause is ἵνα μὴ λυθῇ ὁ νόμος Μωϋσέως. λυθῇ is aorist passive subjunctive of the same verb. The object is the law of Moses. Circumcision on the eighth day, even when that day is a Sabbath, is performed so that Moses’ law not be loosed. The argument is a fortiori of a different controversy: if the law’s standing requires a Sabbath circumcision, how much more a Sabbath making-whole. The present file does not retry that controversy as Chapter 45’s works-file or as a finished Sabbath-doctrine.

What the verse contributes here is lexical and forensic control. In John’s Gospel, λύω in the passive, with law as subject-patient, names the loosing of written standing. “Broken” in that sentence is not the snapping of a scroll. It is the treating of Moses’ law as not in force at the point where it commands. That is the nearest Johannine neighbour to οὐ δύναται λυθῆναι ἡ γραφή.

The neighbour is not identity. John 7:23 concerns ὁ νόμος Μωϋσέως in a Sabbath-healing dispute. John 10:35 concerns ἡ γραφή in a blasphemy-dispute, with a psalm called written in their law. Law and scripture are related in the discourse; they are not collapsed into one lemma. The contribution is the sense of λυθῆναι when a standing writing is the patient: to be loosed as binding.

## 62.12.2 John 5:18 — he had broken the sabbath

> **“…because he not only had broken the sabbath, but said also that God was his Father, making himself equal with God.”**
> — John 5:18

ἔλυεν τὸ σάββατον. The imperfect active names what the opponents charge: He was loosing the Sabbath. Chapter 45 already used this verse as the height of the claim in the works-argument. This chapter takes only the verb. “Broken the sabbath,” in the narrator’s report of their charge, is violation or loosing of a standing ordinance, not demolition of a day as an astronomical object.

The contribution is again range under control. The same Gospel can use λύω of loosing Sabbath-standing. When the same Gospel later says the scripture cannot be λυθῆναι, it is not inventing a nonce-verb. It is applying, to ἡ γραφή, the ordinance-sense already in Johannine usage.

The neighbour is not a claim that John 10 is only a Sabbath-text. It is not a retry of equality-with-God as Chapter 45. It is the verb’s Johannine forensic use.

Thus:

> **John 7:23 and John 5:18 show λύω, in this Gospel, loosing standing ordinance — the law of Moses, the Sabbath. That stretch of the range illuminates John 10:35 without absorbing it. “Broken” as loosed standing is Johannine before it is said of ἡ γραφή.**

# 62.13 Neighbouring Synoptic Uses: Matthew 5:17–19, Used Not Retried

Chapter 48 already established Matthew 5:17–18 as confirmation: He has not come to destroy the Law or the Prophets; jot and tittle will not pass until all be fulfilled. That conclusion remains a premise. This chapter does not rewrite the Sermon as a confirmation-file. It takes the λύω-family in the neighbouring verse.

Matthew 5:17 uses the compound **καταλῦσαι (*katalusai*, from καταλύω, G2647)**: to loosen down, demolish, abolish. “Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets.” Destruction there is abolition of the received writings as standing. He refuses that role.

Matthew 5:19 uses λύω itself:

> **“Whosoever therefore shall break one of these least commandments, and shall teach men so, he shall be called the least in the kingdom of heaven: but whosoever shall do and teach them, the same shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven.”**

λύσῃ is aorist active subjunctive: whoever shall break, shall loose, one of the least commandments. Breaking a commandment, in that sentence, is not snapping a tablet. It is loosing a commandment’s force while teaching others to treat it as loosed. Doing and teaching is the opposite: treating the commandment as standing.

The contribution to John 10:35 is controlled and limited.

The Synoptic neighbour shows that the λύω-family, in Christ’s own speech about written commandment, names loosing of standing, not physical fracture as the governing sense.

The neighbour shows that “least commandments” can be the object. John 10:35’s object is ἡ γραφή as the writing whose wording is the case. A commandment loosed and a scripture loosed are analogous operations upon standing writing. Analogy is not identity of files.

The neighbour must not be used to steal Chapter 63. Matthew 5:18’s jot and tittle, already used in Chapter 48, bear upon why written marks are not treated as disposable. That bearing is confirmation-stage. The dedicated why of John 10:35 from what Scripture is remains later. Illumination is not consumption.

Thus:

> **Matthew 5:17–19, already in force as confirmation, illuminates λύω as loosing of commandment and καταλύω as abolition of Law and Prophets. That illumination serves the sense of John 10:35. It does not retry Chapter 48, and it does not finish Chapter 63.**

# 62.14 Range Without Capture: Other Senses of λύω

A range can be used honestly or captured dishonestly.

Capture of the first kind takes a physical-destruction use and makes it the whole of John 10:35. “Destroy this temple” (John 2:19) is λύω of a building. 2 Peter 3:10–12 is λύω of cosmic elements. Those texts are real. They do not govern a clause whose object is ἡ γραφή in a law-argument. If “cannot be broken” meant only “cannot be physically demolished as an object,” the a fortiori would be a claim about the indestructibility of a scroll in the porch. The argument is not about the porch’s furniture. It is about whether the written naming stands as premise.

Capture of the second kind takes a liberation-use and makes it the whole. John 11:44 looses graveclothes. Luke 13:16 looses a daughter of Abraham from a bond. Liberation is in the range. John 10:35 is not a claim that Scripture cannot be set free from a disease. Importing pastoral consolation as the sense would be a category error.

Capture of the third kind takes “binding and loosing” in Matthew 16:19 and 18:18 as the key to John 10:35. Those texts concern keys, church-discipline, and what is bound or loosed. They are not this statement. A later system that reads John 10:35 as a blank check for later binding-and-loosing authority has left the Dedication controversy.

The honest use of range is the use already performed: exhibit the root (loosing what is bound), exhibit demolition, exhibit ordinance-loosing, then let the argument select the stretch. The argument selects ordinance-loosing of ἡ γραφή as court.

Thus:

> **Other senses of λύω remain in the range and do not capture John 10:35. Physical demolition, pastoral liberation, and later binding-and-loosing are not the statement. The dispute selects loosing of written standing.**

# 62.15 What Cannot Be Done to the Scripture in This Dispute

The locked assignment requires the sense to be stated as what cannot be done to the scripture in this dispute, not as a floating dictionary.

The dispute is this. He has said, “I and my Father are one.” They have taken up stones. They have charged blasphemy: being a man, He makes Himself God. He has asked whether it is not written in their law, “I said, Ye are gods.” He has treated that wording as a calling of those to whom the word of God came. He then asserts that the scripture cannot be broken, and applies the case to the charge against the Father’s sanctified and sent Son.

What would “breaking” the scripture be, as an act available in that dispute?

It would be **to recuse the writing as court**. Stones have already been offered as court. If the writing may be loosed, the stones remain the real tribunal, and Psalm 82:6 is decoration.

It would be **to treat the cited wording as a disposable illustration**. “Your law once used this vocabulary; we may now ignore it.” That treatment would leave the blasphemy-standard — God-vocabulary used of a man is capital — untouched by the writing.

It would be **to annul the lesser case while keeping the charge**. The charge requires that a man not be named in God-vocabulary. The writing has so named those who received God’s word. If the writing can be set aside, the charge need not answer the writing. If the writing cannot be set aside, the charge must answer it.

It would be **to make the wording void as premise**. Voiding is not a later technical term imported from a law-school. It is the ordinary consequence of λυθῆναι in the ordinance-sense: what stood as bound no longer stands. Verse 36 would then be a shout beside an emptied text.

What cannot be done, therefore, is any of those acts as a successful operation upon ἡ γραφή. The writing’s force cannot be recused, disposed of, annulled, or voided so as to leave the appeal without a standing premise.

The statement does not say that opponents cannot attempt the act. They attempt it whenever they treat the writing as irrelevant to the charge. The statement says the act cannot succeed as an act upon the scripture. The writing remains unloosed. Refusal of the argument, which the record itself reports in John 10:39, is not the writing’s being broken. It is the opponents’ remaining unpersuaded. Chapter 61 already distinguished validity of inference from compelled consent. This chapter adds the matching distinction: unbreakability of the writing is not irresistibility of the hearers.

Thus:

> **In this dispute, what cannot be done to the scripture is the loosing of its force as court: recusal, disposal, annulment, voiding of the written case so as to evacuate the appeal. Attempt is possible. Success as an act upon ἡ γραφή is what the statement denies.**

# 62.16 The Clause in Its Place in the Unit

Place is part of sense. A clause moved to a poster is no longer in its place.

The unit’s order, already exhibited as a chain and not retried as a scene, is:

1. Charge: being a man, He makes Himself God (John 10:33).
2. Question of writing: “Is it not written in your law…?” (John 10:34a).
3. Citation: “I said, Ye are gods” (John 10:34b).
4. Written case: “If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came” (John 10:35a).
5. Standing: “and the scripture cannot be broken” (John 10:35b).
6. Application: “Say ye of him… Thou blasphemest; because I said, I am the Son of God?” (John 10:36).

The clause stands between characterization and application. That is not an accident of later versification. It is the argument’s joint. After the lesser case has been identified, before the greater case is pressed, He asserts that the writing which supplies the lesser case cannot be loosed.

Remove the clause in thought and watch the chain. The citation remains. The characterization remains. The application can still be spoken. What is lost is the asserted non-negotiability of the writing as premise. The application would then be a request that they consider a psalm. With the clause, the application is a demand that they answer a writing that cannot be set aside.

The place also forbids two misreadings of isolation.

**Isolation forward.** Taking only “the scripture cannot be broken” as a proverb for any later occasion, without the citation and the charge, is the slogan Chapter 59 already refused as an account of the complete passage. This chapter repeats the refusal as an account of the statement’s sense: sense is sense in this joint.

**Isolation backward.** Taking the clause as only a pious aside after “unto whom the word of God came,” with no work to do in verse 36, is the ornamental reading. Place forbids it. The application follows the standing. Standing is for the application.

Thus:

> **The clause occupies the joint between written case and application. That place is part of the sense: non-negotiable standing of the writing as premise of the a fortiori. A poster is not this place.**

# 62.19 The Writing's Force Cannot Be Set Aside

**Force**, in this chapter, is the writing’s standing as premise that must be answered. Chapter 61 defined weight as the force He assigns the text as court and settling-material. This chapter states the matching negative: that force cannot be set aside.

Setting aside is the forensic name of loosing. A bench that may be ignored is not a bench. A writing that may be set aside is not the court He has opened.

He applies the writing to “Thou blasphemest.” An application that survives the setting aside of its premise is no longer an application. It is a new shout.

Chapter 63 will ask why the writing has this character from what Scripture is. This chapter answers what the character is as the argument asserts it.

# 62.20 Made Void So as to Evacuate the Appeal

**Made void**, in this chapter, is the same act under another honest English name: the writing treated as empty of binding consequence for the charge.

**Evacuate the appeal**, in this chapter, is the result that would follow if that act succeeded: verses 34–36 would remain as sounds, and the blasphemy-charge would no longer have to answer Psalm 82:6.

# 62.21 “Cannot” as Asserted Incapacity of the Writing

**Cannot** is οὐ δύναται. The word is easy to weaken into courtesy.

Courtesy would be: one ought not to break it; it is unseemly to break it; a pious person would not break it. Courtesy is not the clause. The clause is incapacity.

Incapacity, as asserted of the writing, is not a claim about the opponents’ muscle. They can tear a copy. They can refuse to answer. They can take up stones again, as the record says they seek to take Him (John 10:39). Those acts are within human reach. They are not λυθῆναι of ἡ γραφή in the argumentative sense. Tearing a copy does not unmake the writing as Holy Writ. Refusing to answer does not recuse the court. Stones do not rewrite Psalm 82.

Incapacity is asserted of the writing’s force: that force is not able to be loosed. The statement is ontological about the writing’s standing in the argument, not psychological about the hearers. Ontology here is not Chapter 63’s full account from what Scripture is. It is the claim already in the verb: the scripture is not able to be subjected to that act.

A stacked hedge would say: “cannot” might be hyperbole, so we must wait until Chapter 63 to know whether He means it. Sequence does not require that evacuation. He means it as standing-premise of an a fortiori that tries a capital charge. Hyperbole that may be dropped at will cannot occupy that joint. The later why will explain the ground. It will not create the assertion, and it will not reduce “cannot” to “preferably not.”
