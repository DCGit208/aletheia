# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT II — GOD, TRUTH, AND REVELATION

# Chapter 9 — What Grounds Truth?

> **Central question:** If truth is objective, what ultimately grounds truth itself?

The preceding movement brought the investigation to a point that cannot be bypassed.

We began with truth.

We distinguished truth from assertion, opinion, perception, convention, and error. We examined knowledge, evidence, rational warrant, inference, dianoetic thinking, sound judgment, and the biblical summons to reason. We then asked what can finally govern judgment when competing authorities conflict.

That last question exposed a deeper one.

If a proposition is true, **what makes it true?**

If truth corresponds to reality, **what grounds the reality to which truth corresponds?**

If truth is more than human agreement, **what explains its objectivity?**

If an ultimate authority is required, **what ultimately grounds that authority?**

These questions move the investigation from epistemology toward ontology.

The question is no longer merely:

> **How can we know truth?**

It is now:

> **What is truth grounded in?**

This chapter must therefore resist two premature conclusions.

First, it must not simply announce:

> “God is the answer.”

The conclusion must be developed.

Second, it must not pretend that truth can be adequately grounded merely by appealing to human agreement, convention, preference, language, or institutional declaration.

The question is whether such proposals are sufficient.

---

# 1. A Truth Claim Presupposes Something About Reality

Consider a simple proposition:

> **“The object is actually present.”**

Whether that proposition is true does not depend upon whether someone prefers it.

It depends upon whether the object is in fact present.

The proposition is therefore answerable to reality.

This illustrates the correspondence dimension of truth established earlier:

> **A true statement corresponds to what actually is.**

That observation creates a further question.

If truth is correspondence to reality, then reality cannot be treated as an irrelevant backdrop.

The truth of the proposition depends upon the state of affairs to which the proposition refers.

Truth therefore has an ontological dimension.

It is not merely a property of sentences.

A sentence is true **of something**.

A judgment is true **because reality is a certain way**.

Thus:

> **Truth is inseparable from what is actually the case.**

---

# 2. Is Truth Merely a Human Convention?

Suppose a community decides that a particular object shall be called “X.”

The community has established a convention concerning a word.

It has not thereby created the object.

Likewise, a society may establish a convention concerning measurement, currency, etiquette, or terminology.

Such conventions can be real and consequential.

But convention does not automatically determine objective reality.

A society can agree that a particular event occurred.

Its agreement does not make the event occur.

A society can deny that an event occurred.

Its denial does not make the event cease to have occurred.

This distinction is fundamental:

> **Agreement can establish a convention; agreement cannot by itself establish actuality.**

Therefore, if objective truth exists, it cannot ultimately depend upon human agreement.

---

# 3. What If Truth Is Only a Property of Language?

Language is the vehicle through which propositions are communicated.

But language does not create everything to which language refers.

The statement:

> “Water is present”

is meaningful because there is something called water and because the proposition can correspond—or fail to correspond—to an actual state of affairs.

Changing the word does not change the thing.

A person may call water by another name.

The physical reality remains.

Therefore:

> **Changing linguistic description does not necessarily change the reality described.**

This distinction matters because truth concerns more than vocabulary.

A word can be conventional.

The reality to which a true proposition refers need not be.

---

# 4. What If Truth Is Created by Perception?

Perception is indispensable to human knowledge.

We see.

We hear.

We touch.

We measure.

We remember.

We compare.

But perception can also be mistaken.

An object may appear one way under particular conditions and another way under closer examination.

A distant object may be misidentified.

A witness may remember an event imperfectly.

Therefore:

> **Perception is a means by which reality may be encountered; perception is not itself the final creator of reality.**

This is why the earlier distinction between reality and perception remains necessary.

If truth depends ultimately upon what each person perceives, contradictory perceptions could generate contradictory realities.

That would not explain objective truth.

---

# 5. What If Truth Is Whatever a Person Believes?

Belief is a mental attitude toward a proposition.

A person may believe something true.

A person may believe something false.

The existence of belief therefore cannot determine the truth of its object.

A person can sincerely believe:

> “The road is clear.”

The road may nevertheless be occupied.

Sincerity concerns the person's relationship to the proposition.

Truth concerns the proposition's relationship to reality.

This distinction is why the investigation must never reduce truth to belief.

> **Belief may be directed toward truth or error.**

Truth does not become truth because it is believed.

---

# 6. What If Truth Is Determined by Authority?

The problem identified in Chapter 8 returns.

Suppose an institution declares:

> “This proposition is true.”

The declaration is itself a proposition.

The question remains:

> **Why should the declaration be accepted as true?**

If the institution is right because another institution says so, the problem simply moves upward.

If the institution is right because it possesses genuine authority, the source and grounds of that authority must still be examined.

Therefore:

> **Authority can declare, teach, or testify to truth; authority does not automatically create truth.**

This distinction is especially important for the later investigation of Scripture.

We must not say:

> “Scripture is true because an institution declares Scripture authoritative.”

The direction of the argument must eventually be established rather than assumed.

---

# 7. What If Truth Is Whatever Works?

Practical success is relevant evidence in some contexts.

A theory that repeatedly predicts observable outcomes has stronger evidential standing than one that consistently fails.

But usefulness and truth are not identical.

A false belief can sometimes produce a useful short-term result.

A true proposition can be inconvenient.

Therefore:

> **Practical consequence can bear upon rational evaluation without becoming the definition of truth itself.**

Truth remains answerable to actuality.

---

# 8. Objective Truth Requires an Objective Ground

We can now state the central philosophical pressure.

If there are truths that remain true independently of human preference, then those truths possess an objectivity that human preference cannot explain.

For example:

> A contradiction cannot become true merely because a majority votes for it.

> A past event cannot be changed by present opinion about it.

> A fact cannot become false merely because it is unpopular.

The issue is therefore not simply whether people know truth.

It is whether objective truth exists at all.

If it does, the investigation must ask:

> **What accounts for its objectivity?**

---

# 9. Necessary Truths and Contingent Truths

A further distinction helps sharpen the problem.

Some truths concern what **could be otherwise**.

For example:

> “This particular object is located here.”

Its truth depends upon the actual state of the world.

Other truths appear to hold necessarily.

For example:

> **A thing cannot both be and not be in the same respect at the same time.**

The distinction is between **contingent** and **necessary** truth.

Contingent truths depend upon how reality actually is.

Necessary truths do not depend upon a temporary human convention.

This raises an important question:

> **What ultimately accounts for a reality in which necessary truth holds and contingent truths can be objectively true?**

The question becomes increasingly difficult to answer if reality itself is reduced to human convention.

---

# 10. The Ground Cannot Simply Be Another Assertion

Suppose someone says:

> “Truth is objective because truth is objective.”

That merely repeats the conclusion.

Likewise:

> “Reality is real because reality is real.”

The statement may be verbally correct, but it does not provide the explanatory ground being requested.

The investigation requires more than restatement.

It requires an account of **why** objective truth has the status it has.

This is an application of the governing principle:

> **Do not merely tell the reader what to conclude. Give the reader the definitions, evidence, reasoning, distinctions, and inferential steps necessary to see why the conclusion follows.**

---

# 11. Can Matter Alone Ground Truth?

If reality is conceived as nothing more than physical matter in motion, another question arises.

Physical objects possess properties.

They occupy space.

They change.

They interact.

But the proposition describing them is not identical with the physical object itself.

The statement:

> “This object has property P”

is a truth claim about reality.

The object does not contain a sentence inside itself saying that it possesses P.

The question therefore becomes:

> **How does physical existence alone account for the objective truth of propositions about physical existence?**

This is not an argument that physical reality is unreal.

It is a question about whether material description is a sufficient ultimate account of truth.

The distinction is crucial.

---

# 12. Can Human Minds Be the Ultimate Ground?

Human minds can discover truths.

They can formulate propositions.

They can reason.

They can recognize correspondence and contradiction.

But individual minds are finite, variable, and capable of error.

If human minds collectively create truth, then truth would seem to vary with the collective.

Yet the investigation began by distinguishing objective truth from convention.

Therefore human cognition appears better understood as **a means of knowing truth** than as **the ultimate creator of truth**.

This returns us to the distinction established earlier:

> **Reason is indispensable to knowing truth without being the creator of truth.**

---

# 13. Can a Collective Human Mind Ground Truth?

Perhaps no individual human mind is sufficient, but perhaps humanity as a whole is.

The problem remains.

Humanity changes.

Human beings disagree.

Human knowledge expands.

Civilizations disappear.

Consensus reverses.

Ideas once considered established are sometimes abandoned.

If collective human judgment were the ultimate ground of truth, objective truth would become dependent upon a changing human population.

That would again confuse **recognition of truth** with **the ground of truth**.

Human beings may collectively recognize a truth.

They do not thereby necessarily constitute the reality that makes it true.

---

# 14. The Ground of Truth Must Not Be Less Stable Than Truth

This gives us another important criterion.

If truth is objective and stable, its ultimate ground cannot be merely an unstable human preference.

If truth transcends individual opinion, its ground must likewise transcend individual opinion.

If truth remains true when institutions change, the ground of truth cannot be reducible to institutional approval.

If truth remains true when cultures disagree, cultural agreement cannot be its ultimate foundation.

Therefore:

> **The proposed ground of objective truth must possess sufficient explanatory adequacy for objective truth.**

This does not yet prove what that ground is.

It establishes a requirement any proposed answer must satisfy.

---

# 15. The Ground of Truth and the Ground of Reality

The question becomes deeper still.

If truth corresponds to reality, and reality itself is contingent in significant respects, then the investigation must ask what ultimately accounts for reality.

Why is there something rather than nothing?

Why does reality possess order?

Why are there stable relations?

Why are rational structures discoverable?

Why can finite minds apprehend truths about reality?

These questions do not automatically prove one particular answer.

They do, however, expose the inadequacy of simply declaring:

> “Truth exists because people use the word truth.”

The investigation has moved beyond terminology.

It concerns being.

---

# 16. The Classical Causal Question

One of the longstanding approaches to this problem asks about causation.

If something begins to exist, what accounts for its existence?

If contingent reality exists, what accounts for that reality?

This is the basic pressure behind cosmological reasoning.

But caution is required.

A causal argument is not automatically a complete proof of every attribute traditionally predicated of God.

From:

> “There must be an adequate explanation for contingent reality”

one must not leap carelessly to:

> “Therefore every proposition of a particular theology has been demonstrated.”

The inference must be proportionate to the premises.

This is precisely why dianoetic reasoning matters.

Each conclusion must be distinguished from the conclusions that require additional premises.

---

# 17. The Order of the Argument Matters

The investigation therefore proceeds in stages.

### Stage 1

Objective truth is distinguished from subjective preference.

### Stage 2

Truth is understood in relation to actuality and reality.

### Stage 3

Human convention is shown to be insufficient as the ultimate explanation of objective truth.

### Stage 4

Individual and collective human cognition are distinguished from the ground of truth.

### Stage 5

The question of an ultimate ground of reality is raised.

### Stage 6

Possible explanations must be examined for adequacy.

This order prevents a premature conclusion.

The reader is not being asked to accept a philosophical slogan.

The reader is being taken through a problem.

---

# 18. Does God Provide an Adequate Ground?

We can now ask the question directly.

> **Could God be the ultimate ground of truth?**

The biblical answer is not merely that God possesses information that happens to be accurate.

The biblical record presents God as intrinsically related to truthfulness, faithfulness, reality, constancy, and the impossibility of falsehood.

This is why the next chapters matter.

If God is merely imagined as a powerful being, the argument is incomplete.

A powerful being could still be deceitful.

A being capable of knowledge could still communicate falsely.

Therefore the relevant question is not merely:

> **Does God exist?**

It is:

> **What is God like?**

and, more specifically:

> **Is truthfulness intrinsic to God's being and character?**

That question will be examined through the biblical record.

---

# 19. Truth as Something Grounded in Who God Is

This distinction is fundamental.

If God merely **possessed true information**, truth would remain conceptually external to Him.

But the biblical presentation goes further.

Jesus declares:

> **“I am the way, the truth, and the life.”**  
> — John 14:6

John's opening declaration concerning the Word is equally profound:

> **“In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.”**  
> — John 1:1

And:

> **“All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made.”**  
> — John 1:3

These passages do not present truth as merely a detached abstract object possessed by God.

They demand a much deeper investigation of the relationship between **God, the Word, reality, creation, and truth**.

That investigation belongs to the next chapter.

---

# 20. God Is Not Being Introduced as a Mere “Subject”

There is a linguistic danger here.

When we say:

> “God is the subject of the Bible,”

the statement can be misleading if it suggests that God is merely a topic about which human beings have written.

That is not the intended conception.

The biblical record presents God as **the living Creator, revealer, speaker, sustainer, judge, and active reality** encountered within the history it records.

Therefore the question is not merely:

> “What do people think about God?”

It is:

> **What is God, what has God done, what has God revealed, and how can those claims be known to be true?**

This distinction will become crucial when the investigation turns from the ground of truth to divine revelation.

---

# 21. What the Biblical Record Will Have to Establish

If God is to function as the ground of truth in the argument, several propositions must be established rather than assumed.

The investigation must consider whether the biblical record establishes that:

1. God is real.
2. God is the Creator.
3. God is truthful.
4. God is faithful.
5. God is immutable.
6. God cannot lie.
7. God's Word is reliable.
8. God's revelation is genuine.
9. God has communicated with humanity.
10. the written record faithfully communicates that revelation.

These propositions are related but not identical.

For example:

> **God's existence does not by itself establish the authenticity of a particular document.**

And:

> **A genuine divine revelation does not automatically establish the transmission history of every later manuscript.**

Each stage therefore requires its own evidence and reasoning.

---

# 22. Why the Next Question Is About God's Nature

This chapter has brought us to a decisive distinction.

If God is merely one possible being among many, His existence alone would not solve the truth problem.

But if God is the ultimate Creator and ground of reality, and if truthfulness belongs necessarily to His nature, then the relationship between God and truth becomes fundamentally different.

The question becomes:

> **Is God true merely in the sense that He tells the truth, or is truthfulness inseparable from who God is?**

The biblical texts identified for the next stage are therefore highly significant:

- **John 1:1–3** — the Word, God, and creation;
- **John 14:6** — Christ's declaration, “I am … the truth”;
- **James 1:17** — God as the unchanging source from whom good comes;
- **Hebrews 6:17–18** — God's immutability and the impossibility of His lying;
- **2 Corinthians 1:18** — the faithfulness of God;
- and the wider biblical testimony concerning God's truthfulness and constancy.

These passages must be examined as an interconnected argument rather than as isolated proof texts.

---

# 23. What Has Been Established—and What Has Not

### Established in this chapter

The investigation has established that:

- truth cannot be reduced to personal belief;
- truth cannot be reduced to popularity;
- truth cannot be reduced to institutional declaration;
- convention cannot by itself create objective reality;
- perception is not identical with reality;
- human cognition is a means of knowing rather than automatically the ground of truth;
- objective truth requires an adequate ultimate ground;
- the question of that ground is therefore unavoidable;
- God is now a legitimate candidate for examination, not an unexplained premise inserted at the beginning.

### Not yet established

This chapter has **not** completed the argument that:

- God exists;
- God is the ultimate ground of truth;
- God is necessarily truthful;
- God cannot lie;
- divine revelation exists;
- Scripture is that revelation;
- or Scripture is infallible.

Those conclusions require the subsequent investigation.

This distinction is not a weakness.

It is intellectual discipline.

---

# 24. The Argument Now Stands at a New Threshold

The first movement asked:

> **How can truth be known?**

Chapter 8 then asked:

> **What can ultimately govern judgment?**

Chapter 9 has now asked:

> **What grounds truth?**

The next question follows naturally:

> **If God is the ultimate ground of truth, what does the biblical record establish about God Himself?**

That is the subject of Chapter 10.

The investigation will therefore proceed without collapsing God into a philosophical abstraction.

It will examine the biblical presentation of God as Creator, reality, truth, and ground, while continuing to distinguish:

> **God Himself**

from

> **human accounts about God.**

That distinction is indispensable.

The object of inquiry is not what a theologian happens to say God is.

It is not what an institution has constructed.

It is not what a philosophical school has imagined.

It is not what a tradition has accumulated.

The question is:

> **What is established concerning God, and on what grounds?**

---

# Chapter Conclusion

Truth cannot be grounded merely in the fact that human beings call something true.

Reality is not created by vocabulary.

Truth is not created by popularity.

Truth is not created by institutional declaration.

Truth is not created by personal conviction.

Human reason can discover and articulate truth, but finite human minds do not thereby become the ultimate source of truth.

The investigation therefore requires an ultimate ground adequate to the objectivity, stability, and reality of truth.

At this point the question of God is no longer an arbitrary insertion into the argument.

It is the question generated by the argument:

> **What ultimately grounds truth and reality?**

The biblical record presents God as far more than a theoretical explanation.

It presents Him as Creator, source, revealer, faithful, unchanging, and inseparably related to truth.

But those claims must be established.

The next movement therefore begins where the present chapter has brought us:

> **God as the Ground of Truth.**

## Governing distinctions

| Question | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **What is truth?** | Whether a proposition corresponds to what is actually the case |
| **What is reality?** | What actually exists or obtains |
| **What is belief?** | A person's acceptance of a proposition |
| **What is authority?** | What has standing to govern judgment |
| **What is the ground of truth?** | What ultimately accounts for objective truth |
| **What is revelation?** | Truth communicated from its divine source |
| **What is authentication?** | What warrants recognition of a claimed revelation as genuine |

## Logical guardrails for the next chapters

- Do not infer all divine attributes from the bare proposition that God exists.
- Do not confuse the existence of God with the authentication of Scripture.
- Do not use theological vocabulary as a substitute for biblical demonstration.
- Do not make a philosophical argument carry a conclusion larger than its premises warrant.
- Do not treat every biblical quotation as performing the same evidential function.
- Do not reduce God to a “subject” or topic of human discussion.
- Do not substitute commentary about God for the biblical record concerning God.
- Do not prematurely use Scripture as an already-established infallible authority to prove the premises whose establishment will later ground Scripture's authority.
- Do not abandon reason in order to affirm revelation.
- Do not make reason the creator of the reality it investigates.
