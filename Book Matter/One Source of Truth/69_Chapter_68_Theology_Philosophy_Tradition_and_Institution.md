## The Doctrine of the Graphē

> **Central objective:** Examine secondary intellectual and institutional systems in relation to the Graphē — theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution — so that each is shown in its proper rank: able to serve under the writing, and unable to become the primary written authority merely by claiming Christian identity, academic status, antiquity, or institutional recognition. ## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 67 established Movement IX’s second word. Scripture is misread when the later act of stating sense fails to correspond to what the Graphē actually communicates. That failure is inspectable as five operations: poor perception, deficient understanding, faulty inference, context failure, and improper handling. The writing remains the measure of those operations. A correct reading remains possible. An incorrect reading remains incorrect. The catalog is in force.

Chapter 67 ended by naming the next necessary question, not a new curiosity:

> **How do theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution stand toward the Graphē, now that the modes of misreading have been examined, and now that the next file’s first word is to be those systems rather than a second catalog, and rather than a finished displacement-file?**

That is this chapter’s assignment.

The locked Table of Contents states the work in one sentence:

> **Examine secondary intellectual and institutional systems in relation to the Graphē. None becomes primary authority merely by claiming Christian identity, academic status, antiquity, or institutional recognition.**

The locked knowledge-flow places this examination after the modes of misreading and before the dedicated examination of what happens when secondary sources govern the meaning of Scripture instead of serving under it. Modes have been named as modes. Named modes still leave the systems that carry those modes into public, durable, and often official form. A failed reading in a person is an act. A theology, a philosophy, a tradition, or an institution is a system: an ordered way of teaching, inquiring, handing down, or ruling, which can claim the writing’s standing for itself. The present file is that examination, at the level the premises warrant, and not beyond it.

The present file does not re-catalog how Scripture is misread. It cites Chapter 67 as established. It does not re-draw the distinction between the authority of the text and the authority of interpretation. It cites Chapter 66 as established. It does not re-prove that the Graphē is the primary written authority. It cites Chapter 65 as established. It does not re-answer why the record does not fail as truthful divine writing of what it actually communicates. It cites Chapter 57 as established. It does not finish the account of secondary sources becoming primary. It does not write Chapter 69. It does not write tergiversation, accepted heresies, aridity, or *apeiros* as dedicated files. It does not write Movement X. It does not enter the First Principles. It examines the four systems without which Movement IX would possess a catalog of failed acts and still lack the earned account of the intellectual and institutional orders that stand beside the Graphē and claim a place toward it.

A catalogued failure of reading, now distinguished from the writing that measures it, still leaves a further question. If persons misread, what is a system that teaches, reasons, delivers, or rules in the writing’s name? If every such system were already the writing, the gate of Chapter 65 would be vacant. If every such system were already forbidden, service under the writing would be a crime. If the examination stopped at the four names, the relation would be a title. The present file is the relation, at the level the writings and the locked assignment require, and not beyond it.

The remaining question of the book’s sequence follows. Sequence continues the investigation. It does not convert the present line into hesitation.

# 68.1 The Question Created by the Preceding Chapter

Movement IX is open. Its first word has been stated. The authority of the Graphē and the authority claimed for a later statement of sense are two objects. Transfer fails. Despair fails. Its second word has been stated. The later act fails in inspectable modes: poor perception, deficient understanding, faulty inference, context failure, and improper handling. The writing remains the measure. A correct reading remains possible. An incorrect reading remains incorrect. The distinction-file is in force. The catalog is in force.

Chapter 67 established the modes. That conclusion is a premise here. Named modes still leave a further question: what are the ordered systems that stand beside the Graphē, teach or rule in its name, and claim a rank toward it? A mode is an operation of a later act. A system is a durable order of such acts: a way of organizing doctrine, a way of organizing wisdom, a way of handing down what the fathers received, a way of seating an office over a people. The catalog named the operations. The present file examines the orders.

A people can receive the five modes as a piety — “we must read carefully” — and still treat a Christian theology as if the theology were the writing, a philosophical school as if the school were the court, an ancient tradition as if age were inspiration, or a recognized institution as if recognition were the Graphē. They can quote Matthew 22:29 as a proverb about persons and still not have asked what a party’s doctrine is as a system. They can quote Mark 7 as a warning about a bad handling and still not have asked what the tradition of the elders is as a delivered order standing toward the commandment of God. Chapter 67 already refused to let the speaker’s size create a new mode. It also refused to let that refusal become this file. The size has now to be examined. Organizations misread by the same operations persons use. The organization, as an order that claims rank, is a further object.

The question is therefore not:

> **How is Scripture misread?**

That question has been answered.

The question is:

> **How do theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution stand toward the Graphē — as secondary systems that may serve under it, and that do not become primary authority merely by claiming Christian identity, academic status, antiquity, or institutional recognition?**

If Movement IX proceeded from the catalog to the displacement of meaning without examining the systems, it would skip the locked step. If it treated this file as already Chapter 67, it would convert a system-file into a second catalog. If it treated this file as already Chapter 69, it would close the movement’s next question inside its third word. The present file is the dedicated examination of the four systems in relation to the Graphē.

That is a different operation from piety. It is the operation this chapter must perform.

# 68.3 Define the Question: Secondary Systems in Relation to the Graphē

**Secondary systems in relation to the Graphē**, in this chapter, are the ordered intellectual and institutional ways of teaching, inquiring, handing down, and ruling that stand beside the writing and claim a place toward it, examined as systems with a rank, not as a further list of private misreadings.

The phrase is easily abused. In ordinary speech “theology” may mean whatever a religious person feels, “philosophy” may mean any use of the mind, “tradition” may mean whatever is familiar, and “institution” may mean whatever has a building. “Secondary” may be heard as an insult, and “in relation to the Graphē” may be heard as a programme for abolishing schools, memories, and churches. None of those senses is this chapter’s object.

Four elements belong to the definition.

First, **systems, not moods**. A system is an ordered, durable, teachable, and often corporate way of proceeding. A theology arranges claims about God into a body of teaching. A philosophy arranges inquiry into a school or a method that claims to measure reality, including divine reality. A tradition hands on a received way as the way of the fathers or of the elders. An institution seats offices, recognizes members, and binds a people to a common rule. A passing error is not yet any of these. Chapter 67 has already examined the passing error as a mode. The present object is the order.

Second, **relation, not a new primary**. The relation in view is standing toward the Graphē: under it as servant, beside it as commentator, or over it as pretended court. “Secondary” names the proper standing. It names secondness in relation to the primary written authority already earned. It does not name worthlessness. A true sentence inside a theology is true because it corresponds to reality, and, where the reality is what the writing communicates, because it states that communication. The sentence’s truth does not promote the system that carries it into the writing’s rank.

Third, **the four claims as non-conferring**. Christian identity, academic status, antiquity, and institutional recognition are the claims the locked assignment names. Each is a real property a system may possess. None of them is the Graphē. None of them moves a system from secondary standing to primary written authority. The definition includes that refusal. A system without those claims is not thereby the writing either. The claims are the ordinary routes by which secondary systems are treated as primary. The routes fail.

Fourth, **not those other claims**. The definition excludes, as not this object: a retry of Chapter 67’s catalog; a finished account of what happens when commentaries, traditions, institutions, teachers, systems, or experiences govern the meaning of Scripture; tergiversation, accepted heresies, aridity, and *apeiros* as finished files; Movement X’s labour-files; the First Principles as this file’s achievement. Those exclusions are part of the definition. They are not a later apology.

Thus:

> **The question of this chapter is not how a later act fails, and not yet how a secondary source comes to govern meaning. Those questions have been examined or remain later. The question is how theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution stand toward the Graphē.**

# 68.4 Distinguish Objects That Must Not Be Collapsed

The investigation fails at the first step if it treats different objects as one.

**This rank and Chapter 67’s catalog.** This chapter examines systems. Chapter 67 examined modes of a later act. A system may operate by those modes. The operation does not make the system a sixth mode, and the modes do not make this file a second catalog. To treat this file as Chapter 67 retried would hide the orders inside another list of failures. To omit the orders in order to protect the catalog would leave the catalog unjoined to the third word Movement IX assigns.

**The four systems and one another.** Theology is not philosophy, though a theology may borrow a philosophy’s premises. Philosophy is not tradition, though a school may hand itself down. Tradition is not institution, though an institution may enforce a tradition. Institution is not theology, though an institution may publish a theology as its rule. The four may inhabit one house. Inhabiting one house is not identity of object. The locked assignment names four. The examination keeps four.

**A system and a true sentence inside it.** A theology may contain a sentence the writing itself teaches. The sentence remains the writing’s communication. The system remains the arrangement. To collapse them is either to crown every arrangement that once quoted a true sentence, or to discard the true sentence because a system carried it. Both collapses fail. Truth of a carried sentence is not primary standing of the carrier. Secondary standing of the carrier is not falsehood of every sentence carried.

**Service and supremacy.** To serve under the Graphē is to hold, transmit, arrange, or uphold what the writing communicates, while remaining accountable to it. To claim supremacy is to treat the system as the measure of the writing, or as the writing’s necessary voice before the writing can bind. Service is a real office. Supremacy is a reversed rank. The same body may do the first on one day and the second on another. The acts are still two.

**Apostolic delivery and the tradition of men.** The writings use one Greek noun, παράδοσις (*paradosis*, G3862), for what the apostles delivered and for what the elders handed down against the commandment of God. The English word “tradition” must not silently shift from one to the other. What was delivered by the apostolic word and epistle, as the deposit, is not the same object as the tradition of men that makes the word of none effect. The lexical section below will hold the distinction. The distinction is named here so that the system-file does not ban the deposit in the act of refusing the rival.

**The church as house and the church as pretended author.** The assembly can be the pillar and ground of the truth in the sense the writing itself gives: the house that upholds a truth it did not invent, instructed by what is written. The assembly as later author of the Graphē, or as higher court over the Graphē, is a different claim. Chapter 52 already refused recognition as creation. The present file uses that refusal as rank. It does not re-try the canon.

**This chapter and later files.** Displacement of meaning, tergiversation, accepted heresies, aridity, *apeiros*, scholarly labour, *spoudazō*, and the First Principles remain later. A system may be the place where a later pathology appears. Appearance is not those files written now.

Thus:

> **The four systems are ordered ways of teaching, inquiring, handing down, and ruling, ranked toward the Graphē. They are not the modes retried, not one another, not identical with every true sentence they carry, not forbidden by being secondary, and not the later files under another name.**

What this chapter does not settle is a matter of sequence, not of doubt about what it does settle.

The investigation proceeds by levels. Each level answers its question with full force. The next level remains next because it is a different question.

**Already in force.** Truth is conformity to reality. God is the ground of truth and cannot lie. Revelation has been given and authenticated. Scripture as writing is God-breathed. The record does not fail as truthful divine writing of what it actually communicates. The Graphē is the primary written authority. The authority of that writing has been distinguished from the authority claimed for a later statement of sense. Scripture is misread in five inspectable modes, and the writing remains the measure of those modes. Recognition of the corpus is not creation of the corpus. Human accounts about God are accountable.

**This level.** Given those premises, theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution are secondary systems in relation to the Graphē. They may serve under the writing. They do not become the primary written authority merely by claiming Christian identity, academic status, antiquity, or institutional recognition. The warrant is full at this level. It is not postponed until Chapter 69 in order to keep the present rank weak, and it is not inflated into a displacement-file, a pathology-file, or a labour-file in order to make the rank sound larger than a relation of systems to a writing.

**Next levels, named once.** Secondary sources becoming primary belongs to Chapter 69. Tergiversation, accepted heresies, aridity, and *apeiros* belong to Chapters 70–73. Scholarly labour, perception as a dedicated stage-file, reasoning through the Word as a labour-file, quotation and knowledge as a dedicated file, *spoudazō*, exercise, maturity, rightly dividing, and readiness for the Six First Principles belong to Movement X. Those files remain later.

**Limits that belong to this level itself, not to later hesitation.** This rank is not a second catalog of misreading. This rank is not a denial that a system can say what the writing says. This rank is not a ban on doctrine, on reason, on apostolic delivery, or on the house of God. This rank is not a grant that every system is harmless because it is called secondary. Secondary names the proper standing. It does not bless a reversal. Using Chapters 14, 52, 57, 65, 66, and 67 is not rewriting them. Christian identity is not a sneer at Christ. The chapter will therefore state the positive warrant with full force, locate the limits as belonging to the doctrine rather than as apologies, and name later files once as later files. It will not repeat, under every paragraph, that the present conclusion is not proof of a later conclusion. Sequence is already the account of that difference.

Thus:

> **The present rank is in force at this level. Later verdicts remain later because they are different questions. Limits named here are the shape of the doctrine, not a delayed doubt about the doctrine.**

# 68.7 Define “Theology” at This Stage

**Theology**, in this argument, is a human intellectual system that organizes teaching about God, about His works, and about what His writing is taken to require.

The English word does not reproduce a single New Testament technical term in the way γραφή reproduces a biblical use. The writings speak of διδαχή (*didachē*, G1322), teaching, and of διδασκαλία (*didaskalia*, G1319), doctrine or instruction. They speak of sound doctrine, of doctrine according to godliness, of the doctrine of the Pharisees, and of commandments of men taught as doctrines. “Theology,” as this chapter uses it, is the later ordered form of such teaching: a body of propositions arranged, taught, and often treated as the Christian account of God.

Four notes belong to the definition at this stage.

First, **organization is real**. To gather what the writing says of God, of Christ, of man, of sin, of promise, and of duty, and to state the gather in an order a learner can hold, is an intellectual act. Chapter 14 already granted that organization is not itself the problem. The present file inherits the grant. A theology can be a servant of attention.

Second, **organization is not the writing**. The arrangement is a later construction. Its propositions must still be traced to their warrant. A proposition the writing establishes is the writing’s. A proposition inferred must show the inference. A proposition imported from a philosophy, a tradition, or an institutional decision must show that origin. The label “theology” is not a warrant. The label “Christian theology” is not a second warrant.

Third, **sound doctrine is doctrine that holds the faithful word**. The writings do not treat all teaching as suspect. They require teaching that accords with the words of the Lord Jesus Christ and with the writing that is profitable for doctrine. Soundness is correspondence to that word. Soundness is not the system’s self-description.

Fourth, **not the later files**. Theology as a system that has come to govern the meaning of the writing, theology as an accepted heresy, and the labour of rightly stating doctrine remain later. The present definition is the system and its rank.

Thus:

> **Theology, at this stage, is an ordered human teaching about God and about the writing, legitimate as service, secondary as rank, and powerless to become the Graphē by calling itself Christian.**

# 68.8 Define “Philosophy” at This Stage

**Philosophy**, in this argument, is a human intellectual system that organizes wisdom and inquiry — claims about what is, how it is known, and how one ought to live — and that may offer itself as a measure of reality, including the reality of God and of His writing.

The New Testament uses φιλοσοφία (*philosophia*, G5386) once. The word names the love of wisdom, φιλο- joined to σοφία (*sophia*, G4678). Its single occurrence stands inside a warning, which the biblical record below will examine. The wider wisdom-vocabulary of the writings is not identical with that one warning. There is a wisdom of God. There is a wisdom of this world. There is the reason Chapters 4–7 already established as an inspectable operation. Philosophy, as this chapter’s system, is the ordered claim of a wisdom-school or a wisdom-method to be a court.

Four notes belong to the definition at this stage.

First, **inquiry is real**. To ask what is, why it is, and how it is known is a human act the opening movements have already refused to despise. A philosophical argument can expose a contradiction. It can clarify a concept. It can show that a proposed inference does not follow. Chapter 14 already refused the sentence “therefore philosophy is evil.” The present file inherits the refusal.

Second, **a system of wisdom is not revelation**. Chapter 13 already distinguished knowledge supplied by God’s making-known from speculation about God. A school that constructs an account of God out of its own principles has produced a philosophy. It has not produced the Graphē. Failure of such constructions to supply what only revelation supplies is a rank-fact. It is not a census of every sentence ever spoken by a person called a philosopher.

Third, **a true clause inside a school is still a clause**. The writings can quote a poet’s true sentence without installing the poet as Scripture. A philosophical sentence that happens to correspond to what is the case is true as a sentence. The school that framed it does not, by that correspondence, become the primary written authority.

Fourth, **not a ban on reason, and not the labour-file**. Refusing philosophy as primary is not refusing Isaiah 1:18. Reasoning through the Word remains Movement X. The present definition is the system and its rank.

Thus:

> **Philosophy, at this stage, is an ordered human wisdom and inquiry, legitimate as examination, secondary as rank, and powerless to become the Graphē by academic dignity or by the name of wisdom.**

# 68.9 Define “Tradition” at This Stage

**Tradition**, in this argument, is a system of handing down: what is received from a prior bearer and delivered to a later one as the thing to be held.

The noun is παράδοσις (*paradosis*, G3862), from παραδίδωμι (*paradidōmi*, G3860), to hand over, to deliver. The act is transmission. The system is the transmitted order treated as binding. The writings use the noun for two objects that must not be fused. They use it for what the apostles delivered, whether by word or by epistle. They use it for the tradition of the elders and of the fathers, by which the commandment of God can be laid aside. The English word covers both. This chapter will not.

Four notes belong to the definition at this stage.

First, **handing down is real**. A people cannot know what they were not given. Delivery of what God has spoken, of what Christ taught, and of what the apostles received and wrote, is a necessary act of continuity. Chapter 40 already distinguished transmission of the text from the inspired writing. The present file is not that manuscript-file. It is the system that claims the delivered thing as a rule.

Second, **the content of the delivery decides the object**. Apostolic paradosis, as the writings describe it, is what was taught by the apostolic word and contained in the apostolic epistle. It is the deposit. The tradition of men, as the writings describe it, is commandments of men taught as doctrines, able to make the word of God of none effect. Same noun. Different relation to the writing. The system-question is the relation.

Third, **antiquity is a historical property**. Age tells how long a thing has been handed down. Age does not tell whether the thing corresponds to what God commanded. An old path may be the good way. An old conversation may be the vain conversation from which men are redeemed. The property and the correspondence are two. Chapter 14 already stated that antiquity is not truth. The present file applies that premise to tradition as a system standing toward the Graphē.

Fourth, **not the displacement-file**. How a tradition comes to govern the meaning of a sentence, so that the sentence is read only as the tradition permits, remains Chapter 69. The present definition is the system, its two biblical uses, and its rank.

Thus:

> **Tradition, at this stage, is a system of delivery. Apostolic delivery of the deposit serves the writing, and is in part the writing. The tradition of men is a rival rule. Neither becomes primary merely by being old.**

# 68.10 Define “Institution” at This Stage

**Institution**, in this argument, is an ordered body: a people gathered, an office seated, a continuing rule by which members are recognized, taught, disciplined, or cast out.

The writings’ nearest ordinary word is ἐκκλησία (*ekklēsia*, G1577), the assembly called out, rendered “church.” Not every use of that word is a later denomination, and not every institution in this chapter is a church. Scribes sit in a seat. A council commands silence. A synagogue agrees on an expulsion. A man loves preeminence in a church and refuses the apostolic witness. Schools, sanhedrins, and houses appear. “Institution” names the ordered body as a system, not a particular century’s polity.

Four notes belong to the definition at this stage.

First, **order is real**. The writings address churches, charge elders, speak of a house of God, and record gatherings that consider a matter. An assembly is not a mistake. An office that holds the faithful word is not a rival by existing. Service is an office the writings themselves describe.

Second, **order is not authorship**. The church does not become the Graphē by being the church. Recognition of writings that already stand is not the creation of those writings. A seat derivative of Moses is not a new Moses. A gathering that appeals to what is written does not replace what is written. Chapter 52 and Chapter 65 already established the order of rank. The present file examines the institution as the system that either keeps that order or claims the reverse.

Third, **institutional recognition is a social act**. To be received by a body, to be put out of a synagogue, to be acknowledged by those who seem to be pillars, to carry the name of a school — these are real acts of recognition. They establish recognition. They do not, by themselves, establish that a proposition is what the writing communicates, or that the body is the primary written authority.

Fourth, **not a polity, and not the displacement-file**. This chapter does not write a doctrine of church government, and it does not trace how an institution comes to govern meaning. It ranks the institution toward the Graphē.

Thus:

> **Institution, at this stage, is an ordered body or office, legitimate as service and as uphold of the truth, secondary as rank, and powerless to become the Graphē by recognition, expulsion, or the claim to be the house.**

---

# 68.11 Define the Four Claims That Do Not Confer Primary Standing

The locked assignment names four claims. They are the ordinary promotions. Each must be defined before it is refused, or the refusal will strike a caricature.

**Christian identity** is the claim that a system is Christian: that it follows Christ, bears His name, or constitutes the Christian account of God and of the writing. The writings use Χριστιανός (*christianos*, G5546) of disciples. Methodology already in force for this manuscript requires that “Christian” stay tied to Christ, and that an organization not become Christian merely by adopting the label. Identity, in the present argument, is a claim of relation to Christ. The claim is accountable to Christ and to the writing that testifies of Him. The claim is not the relation, and it is not the writing.

**Academic status** is the claim that a system possesses learning, letters, a school, a recognized wisdom, or the standing of those who know. Scribes, disputers, philosophers, and men who marvel that another knows letters without having learned are the biblical neighbourhood. Status is a property of training and recognition among the learned. It is not a property of inspiration.

**Antiquity** is the claim that a system is old: the tradition of the elders, the traditions of the fathers, what was said by them of old time, the old paths. Age is how long a delivery has run. Age is evidence of duration. Duration is not correspondence, and duration is not God-breathed writing.

**Institutional recognition** is the claim that a competent body has received, seated, approved, or excluded, and that this reception is the measure. Recognition by apostles of a fellowship is a real act inside the apostolic generation, and even that act, as Galatians 2 will show, added nothing to the gospel. Recognition by a later body is also a real act. Neither act is the Graphē. Chapter 52’s distinction — reception is not creation — is the premise. The present definition applies it to the claim as a claim of primary standing.

The four claims may combine. A Christian institution of ancient academic theology claims all four at once. Combination does not create a fifth warrant. Four failures do not become a success by standing in one house.

Thus:

> **Christian identity, academic status, antiquity, and institutional recognition are real claims and real properties. They confer membership, learning, age, and recognition. They do not confer the primary written authority of the Graphē.**

---

# 68.12 Why the Four Are Systems, and Why Rank Is the Question

A mode can be committed once. A system can be taught to the next generation as the way the writing is to be held. That durability is why Movement IX does not stop at the catalog.

Theology endures as a curriculum. Philosophy endures as a school. Tradition endures as what “we have received.” Institution endures as an office that outlives the officer. Each can preserve a true service. Each can also preserve a misreading until the misreading no longer looks like a person’s act and looks like the faith itself. The preservation is the system-fact. The rank-question is whether the preserved order has become the measure.

Chapter 67 established that the speaker’s size does not create a new mode of misreading. The present chapter uses that conclusion without repeating the catalog. Size does create a different object of examination: not a sixth way of failing a sentence, but a public order that can claim primary standing over many sentences and many persons. The Sadducees’ not-knowing was a mode. The doctrine of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees, warned of as leaven, is a teaching-system. The Corban device was a handling. The tradition of the elders, held by the party as the measure of transgression, is a delivery-system. The scribe who rejects the word while claiming the law is an institutional-intellectual system. The distinction is the reason this file exists.

Rank answers a precise question: under the Graphē, or over it? A system under the Graphē can be used, corrected, and, where it contradicts the writing, set aside, without insult to learning, memory, or the assembly. A system over the Graphē cannot be corrected by the writing, because the writing has been made the dependent object. Chapter 14 already named the reversal as the danger of beginning with a human system. The present file is no longer the warning in general. It is the rank of these four.

Thus:

> **The four are systems because they endure as orders. The question is their rank toward the Graphē. Service keeps the writing as measure. Supremacy makes the system the measure. The writings judge the second.**

---

# 68.13 Positive Warrant: The Graphē Remains the Measure of Every Later System

The first member of the positive warrant is the rank already earned, now applied.

Because the God who cannot lie has made known, because all Scripture is God-breathed as writing, because the record does not fail as truthful divine writing of what it actually communicates, and because that writing is the primary written authority by which truth is known, tested, reasoned through, taught, discerned, and lived, every later system stands in front of a measure that does not take its rank from the system. The measure is not granted by the system’s permission. The measure is the writing’s own standing.

This is not a new gate. Chapter 65 drew the gate. The present use is the consequence for systems. A theology is true in what it affirms only so far as what it affirms is true, and, where it claims to state the writing, only so far as the writing communicates that sense. A philosophy is a court of divine things only if it is the writing, which it is not. A tradition binds as God’s commandment only if it is God’s commandment. An institution binds the conscience as the word of God only if what it binds is the word of God. In each case the test is the writing, not the system’s name.

The writings already speak this way, and later sections will exhibit the speeches. The warrant at this opening is the join itself. Primary written authority that could be displaced by a system’s identity, learning, age, or recognition would not be primary. Chapter 65 already refused that displacement as a limit. The present chapter takes the limit as the work: the refusal is the doctrine of the relation, not a footnote on the gate.

A system, then, is always the second speaker. The writing is the first. The second speaker may repeat the first truly. Repetition is service. The second speaker may also correct the first, supplement the first as though the supplement were the first, or require the first to mean what the second needs. Those acts are claims of rank. They are judged by the first speaker, who remains in force.

Thus:

> **The Graphē remains the measure of theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution. Their truth, where they are true, is truth they do not own as primary. Their rank is second because the writing’s rank is first.**

---

# 68.14 Positive Warrant: A System May Serve Under the Writing

The second member of the positive warrant is service. Secondary is an office, not a slur.

Scripture is profitable for doctrine. The man of God is furnished by that profit. A theology that gathers this profit into an order a church can teach is doing what the profit is for, so long as the gather remains under the writing and can be checked by it. Sound doctrine is a commanded good. The servant who holds the faithful word and is able by sound doctrine to exhort and to convince is commended, not suspected.

Reason is a commanded engagement. Isaiah 1:18 remains in force. A philosophy that clarifies a question, exposes an invalid inference, or states a true observation about the world can serve the investigator who then tests every claim about God and about the writing by revelation and by the Graphē. The service is real precisely because the philosophy is not asked to be the revelation.

Delivery is a commanded continuity. The brethren are told to hold the traditions they were taught by word and by epistle. Transmission of the deposit is not a rival of the deposit. A tradition-system that hands on what the epistle hands on is a servant of the writing. The manuscript-transmission already examined in Chapter 40 is one form of this service. Memory of apostolic teaching, where the memory is the teaching, is another. Neither is a second Graphē.

The house is a commanded uphold. The church of the living God is addressed as pillar and ground of the truth in a letter that tells the house how to behave. To uphold the truth in the world, to confess it, to order life by it, is a service. The letter is the instructor of the house. The house is not the author of the letter.

These four services are the positive rank. Abolish them, and the chapter has refused the writings’ own commands in the act of defending the writings. Crown them, and the chapter has given the servant the master’s seat. The warrant holds both: service is required; supremacy is refused.

Thus:

> **Theology may teach under the writing. Philosophy may examine under the writing. Tradition may deliver the deposit under the writing. Institution may uphold the truth under the writing. Service is the proper rank, stated here as a good, not as a concession.**

---

# 68.15 Positive Warrant: The Writings Themselves Rank the Systems

The third member of the positive warrant is that the rank is not an inference imposed on silent texts. The writings distinguish the objects and assign the relation.

They distinguish sound doctrine from commandments of men taught as doctrines. They distinguish the wisdom of God from the wisdom of this world, and Christ, in whom the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hid, from a philosophy that is not after Christ. They distinguish traditions taught by apostolic word and epistle from the tradition of men that makes the word of none effect, and from the vain conversation received by tradition from the fathers. They distinguish Moses’ seat from the works of those who sit in it, the prophets’ writing from a gathering that cites it, and the truth a house upholds from the house as if the house were the truth’s author. They distinguish disciples called Christians from those who say “Lord, Lord” and do not the Father’s will. They distinguish having been with Jesus from having letters. They distinguish pillars who add nothing from a gospel that would be revised by the impressive.

Those distinctions are the biblical form of this chapter’s thesis. Later sections will open them. The warrant at this point is that the thesis is a reading of the distinctions, not a preference for solitary readers against every ordered life. The writings know ordered teaching, ordered inquiry, ordered delivery, and ordered assembly. They rank all four by the word of God.

Thus:

> **The writings themselves teach the rank. Doctrine, wisdom, delivery, and house are acknowledged. Each is ordered to the word. None is installed as the word’s higher court.**

---

# 68.16 Theology as a System: Doctrine Under the Word, and Doctrine as a Rival

Theology becomes visible in the writings first as doctrine. The system-question is whether the doctrine holds the word or replaces it.

Where doctrine holds the word, the writing is profitable for it, and the teacher is a servant of a profitability that belongs to the writing. The doctrine does not add a second inspiration. It states, in an order, what the writing gives. Its Christian name, if it truly follows Christ, is a confession that Christ and His word are the measure. The name is not an independent credential.

Where doctrine replaces the word, commandments of men are taught as doctrines, and worship that uses God’s name is vain. The system may be the doctrine of a party. It may be called the teaching of those who sit in the place of the learned. It may claim great age. The claim does not alter the object. A doctrine of men taught as if it were God’s teaching is a theology in the sense this chapter has defined: an organized human teaching occupying the place of the writing.

The two can use the same vocabulary. Both may say “God,” “Scripture,” “holiness,” “tradition.” Chapter 66 already established that quotation is not the writing. The present file uses that premise at the level of systems. A theology’s quotation of the Graphē is service when the sense stated is the sense communicated. The same quotation is a rival when the system’s prior arrangement decides what the quotation will be allowed to mean. The second case is the reversal of rank. How that reversal, once habitual, governs a people’s reading is the next chapter’s question. That the reversal is a reversal of rank is this chapter’s question, and it is already answerable.

Thus:

> **Theology serves when it holds the faithful word as doctrine. Theology rivals when it teaches the commandments of men as doctrines. The name of the system does not decide which act has occurred. The writing decides.**

---

# 68.17 Biblical Record: 2 Timothy 3:16–17 and Titus 1:9

2 Timothy 3:16–17 states that all Scripture is given by inspiration of God and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness, that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works. Titus 1:9 charges the elder to hold fast the faithful word as he hath been taught, that he may be able by sound doctrine both to exhort and to convince the gainsayers. Titus 2:1 commands: speak thou the things which become sound doctrine.

**Context.** The speaker is Paul. The addressees are Timothy and Titus, charged with teaching and with ordering the churches. The occasion is instruction for ministers who will themselves teach. The writing in view is Scripture, already established as God-breathed, with “all” unweakened. The doctrine in view is a use of that writing, and a speech that becomes what that writing supports.

**What is explicit.** Scripture is profitable for doctrine. The profit runs to a furnished man of God. Sound doctrine is a capacity that depends on holding the faithful word. Speech is to become sound doctrine. An office of teaching is assumed, and the office is tied to the word.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Theology, as ordered doctrine, has a ministerial place under the Graphē. The writing is the source of the profit. The doctrine is the profit’s exercise. The elder’s soundness is not a private inspiration and not an institutional self-warrant. It is holding the faithful word. Gainsayers are to be convinced by that doctrine, not by the elder’s status. The system of teaching is therefore affirmed as service and denied as origin. Primary profitability belongs to the Scripture. Derivative capacity belongs to the one who holds it.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That a later systematic theology, by calling itself the doctrine for which Scripture is profitable, inherits the verse. That the labour of rightly dividing has been written because a minister is told to hold the word. That every body of propositions published as doctrine is sound. Soundness is the holding. The holding can be tested. The test is the word.

Thus:

> **Doctrine is a commanded use of a writing that is already profitable. The theology that holds the faithful word serves. The writing remains the profit and the measure of the holding.**

---

# 68.18 Biblical Record: Matthew 15:9 and Matthew 16:6–12

Matthew 15:9, citing Isaiah, states the verdict: in vain they do worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men. Matthew 16:6–12 records the warning to beware the leaven of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees, and the disciples’ later understanding that the leaven was not bread but the doctrine of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees.

**Context.** The speaker is Jesus. The audience of the Isaiah citation is Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem, then the multitude. The audience of the leaven saying is the disciples. The occasion of the first is a dispute about the tradition of the elders. The occasion of the second is a departure from that region and a warning the disciples first mishear. Chapter 67 already used Matthew 16 as faulty inference from a genuine sentence to the wrong object, and used Matthew 15 as a handling that evacuates. The present function is the doctrine as a system: a party’s teaching, corporate enough to be warned of as leaven, capable of being taught as doctrine while consisting of commandments of men.

**What is explicit.** Commandments of men can be taught as doctrines. Worship can be vain while that teaching proceeds. A party’s doctrine can be a leaven the disciples must beware. The doctrine is distinguished from bread, and from a private slip of hearing.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** A theology — an organized teaching offered as doctrine — can be a rival of God’s commandment while occupying the language of worship. The rival is not a mood. It is a doctrine of named parties. Christian identity is not the claim these parties make in those words, and the chapter will not pretend that it is. The warrant that transfers is the structure of the claim: a teaching-system, religiously serious, learned, and ancestral, does not become God’s doctrine by being taught as doctrine. Any later system that teaches the commandments of men as the Christian doctrine of God repeats the structure. The label does not heal it.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That every doctrinal disagreement is leaven. That the parties’ system has been traced as a finished displacement of biblical meaning. That heresy, as the later file, has been catalogued because a party’s doctrine is warned against. The warning is rank: this teaching is not the measure. The measure remains the commandment of God.

Thus:

> **A doctrine-system can be taught as worship and still be the commandments of men. Party, learning, and religious seriousness do not promote it. The commandment of God remains the measure of the doctrine.**

---

# 68.19 Biblical Record: Romans 16:17 and 1 Timothy 6:3–4, 20

Romans 16:17 beseeches the brethren to mark them which cause divisions and offences contrary to the doctrine which ye have learned, and to avoid them. 1 Timothy 6:3–4 describes the man who teaches otherwise and consents not to wholesome words, even the words of our Lord Jesus Christ, and to the doctrine which is according to godliness. 1 Timothy 6:20 charges Timothy to keep that which is committed to his trust, avoiding profane and vain babblings, and oppositions of science falsely so called.

**Context.** The speaker is Paul. The audiences are the Roman believers and Timothy. The occasion is closing and pastoral charge. “Science” in the English of this verse renders γνῶσις (*gnōsis*, G1108), knowledge. The phrase is ἀντιθέσεις τῆς ψευδωνύμου γνώσεως (*antitheseis tēs pseudōnymou gnōseōs*): oppositions of falsely named knowledge. The object is a knowledge-claim bearing a name it does not deserve. It is not a verdict on every later inquiry into nature.

**What is explicit.** There is a doctrine the believers have learned, and divisions contrary to it are to be marked. Wholesome words are the words of the Lord Jesus Christ. Doctrine according to godliness is the consent those words require. Something has been committed to Timothy’s trust. A knowledge that is falsely named is to be avoided, and some who profess it have erred concerning the faith.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Theology is accountable to a doctrine already learned and to wholesome words already given. Novelty of teaching is not a credential. A system of knowledge can wear a name — knowledge, and, in later centuries, any dignified academic name — and the name can be false. The falsity is measured by the deposit and by the words of Christ, not by the confidence of the profession. Academic status, which is the social form of a knowledge-claim, does not settle the faith. The committed trust is kept by avoiding the false opposition, not by submitting the trust to it.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That every new sentence about Scripture is a division to be marked before it is tested. Testing remains possible because the doctrine learned and the words of Christ are public. That falsely named knowledge has been identified with a particular modern discipline. The text names the false name, not a later faculty. That the mechanisms of doctrinal turning aside have been written. The charge is the rank of the knowledge-claim under the deposit.

Thus:

> **Doctrine is measured by the words of Christ and by the doctrine learned from the deposit. A knowledge-system’s name does not make it knowledge. Academic opposition does not outrank the trust committed.**

---

# 68.20 Philosophy as a System: Wisdom That Examines, and Wisdom That Spoils

Philosophy becomes visible where a wisdom-system offers itself as the way of knowing, beside or instead of Christ and the writing that testifies of Him.

The writings do not begin by insulting the intellect. They speak wisdom among the mature. They invite reason. They record an apostle reasoning in synagogues and marketplaces, and quoting a poet where the poet has said a true thing. Inquiry and the use of a true observation are in the record. What the record refuses is the identification of that use with the wisdom of this world as a saving and measuring court, and the identification of a philosophy after the tradition of men with the fullness that dwells in Christ.

The system-question is therefore not whether a Christian may think. The system-question is whether a philosophical order — a school, a method, a set of rudiments treated as the elements of reality — stands under the writing or claims to spoil the believer away from Christ by becoming the prior frame. A prior frame that decides what Christ is allowed to be, and what the writing is allowed to say, has taken the rank of a court. A frame that is brought to the writing and corrected by it has remained an inquiry.

Chapter 13’s distinction is the premise: speculation about God is not the making-known of God. The present file does not re-argue revelation. It ranks the philosophical system. The school’s academic status is the ordinary claim by which the ranking is resisted. The claim is part of what the next biblical sections judge.

Thus:

> **Philosophy may examine. A philosophy that becomes the prior frame over Christ and the writing has claimed a rank the writings refuse. Wisdom of God and wisdom of the world are not one system under two names.**

---

# 68.21 Biblical Record: Colossians 2:3–8

Colossians 2:3–4 states that in Christ are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge, and that this is said lest any man should beguile with enticing words. Colossians 2:8 states the warning: beware lest any man spoil you through philosophy and vain deceit, after the tradition of men, after the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ. Verses 9–10 continue: in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily, and ye are complete in him.

**Context.** The speaker is Paul. The audience is the church at Colosse. The occasion is a warning against a capture of the believers by a persuasive alternative to Christ. Philosophy here is φιλοσοφία, the single New Testament use. It is paired with κενὴ ἀπάτη (*kenē apatē*), empty deceit. It is characterized as κατὰ τὴν παράδοσιν τῶν ἀνθρώπων (*kata tēn paradosin tōn anthrōpōn*), after the tradition of men, and after the στοιχεῖα (*stoicheia*, G4747) of the world, and not after Christ.

**What is explicit.** Wisdom and knowledge have their treasures in Christ. Enticing words can beguile. A philosophy joined to empty deceit, shaped by human tradition and by the world’s rudiments, and not according to Christ, is a spoiling influence. Fullness and completeness are in Christ, not in the alternative.

**What the passage warrants for this system.** Philosophy, as a system, is capable of standing in a definite relation: not after Christ. That relation is the reversed rank. The system draws its shape from the tradition of men and from elemental principles treated as sufficient, and it meets the believer as a capture. The passage does not say that every act of reasoning is this capture. It says that this philosophy is. The test is the “after”: after men and the world’s rudiments, or after Christ. Academic impressiveness is the native atmosphere of enticing words. It is not a defence. The treasures of wisdom are not relocated into the school that refuses to be after Christ.

**What the passage does not warrant here.** That the word φιλοσοφία in a later century, applied to every disciplined inquiry, is already the Colossian error. The text defines the philosophy it warns against by its companions: empty deceit, tradition of men, rudiments of the world, not after Christ. That tradition as a whole system has been exhausted because παράδοσις appears. The tradition of men is here the shape of the philosophy. The dedicated tradition-examination continues below. That the First Principles have been taught because fullness in Christ is confessed.

Thus:

> **A philosophy after the tradition of men and the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ, is a system that spoils. Wisdom and knowledge remain treasured in Christ. The school does not become that treasure by being a school.**

# 68.22 Biblical Record: Acts 17:16–34

Acts 17:16–34 records Paul at Athens. His spirit is stirred as he sees the city wholly given to idolatry. He reasons in the synagogue and in the market daily. Certain philosophers of the Epicureans and of the Stoicks encounter him. Some ask what this babbler will say. Others take him as a setter forth of strange gods, because he preached Jesus and the resurrection. They bring him to the Areopagus, asking to know this new doctrine. Paul addresses them as very religious, notes an altar inscribed to the unknown god, and declares the God who made the world, who is not served by men’s hands, who has made of one blood all nations, and who has appointed a day in which he will judge the world by that man whom he has raised. He quotes their own poets: “For we are also his offspring.” Some mock. Some defer. Some believe and cleave to him.

**Context.** The speaker of the address is Paul. The audience is pagan philosophers and the curious of Athens, not a Christian school. The occasion is encounter with existing wisdom-systems and with the city’s worship. The resurrection is the point of offence and of announcement. The poets are cited as “certain also of your own poets.”

**What is explicit.** There are philosophers, named by school. Paul reasons. A true clause can be taken from a poet without the poet being treated as Scripture. An altar and a religious city are not the knowledge of God. The resurrection is preached as act, not submitted to the Areopagus as a hypothesis the council must authorize. The hearers divide. The division does not edit the announcement.

**What the passage warrants for this system.** Philosophy as a living system of schools can be met, reasoned with, and even quoted where it has said what is true, without becoming the measure of the gospel. Academic status at Athens is as high as the narrative’s world offers. It does not decide whether Jesus rose. The unknown god of civic religion is not converted into knowledge by being inscribed. Declaration stands over the inscription. The poet’s true clause remains a clause in a poet. It is not added to the Graphē, and it is not the ground of the resurrection. Reason is exercised in public. The schools are not the court. This is the positive and the negative warrant together: philosophy can be a partner in encounter and cannot be the primary authority of what is announced.

**What the passage does not warrant here.** That every sentence of Epicurus or Zeno has been adjudicated. The schools are named as the encounter, not as a syllabus. That natural theology has been written as a system because creation is preached. The sermon is evidence of rank, not that later file. That mockery by the learned disproves, or that belief by some of them proves, by the social fact alone. The word preached is the object. The reaction is the hearers’.

Thus:

> **Among the philosophers, reason is used and a true clause may be quoted. The schools do not authorize the resurrection, and the city’s altar does not constitute knowledge of God. Academic status is not primary written authority.**

# 68.23 Biblical Record: 1 Corinthians 1:18–25 and 2:1–7

1 Corinthians 1:18–25 states that the preaching of the cross is to them that perish foolishness, but to the saved the power of God. It asks where the wise, the scribe, and the disputer of this world are, and states that God has made foolish the wisdom of this world. The world by wisdom knew not God. It pleased God by the foolishness of preaching to save them that believe. The foolishness of God is wiser than men. 1 Corinthians 2:1–5 states that Paul’s speech and preaching were not with enticing words of man’s wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power, that faith should not stand in the wisdom of men but in the power of God. Verses 6–7 add that wisdom is spoken among the perfect, yet not the wisdom of this world, but the wisdom of God.

**Context.** The speaker is Paul. The audience is the church at Corinth, already troubled by allegiance to impressive persons. The occasion is the nature of the gospel’s standing against the world’s wisdom-systems. The scribe and the disputer appear beside the wise: learning and debate are included in the set that the cross displaces as a court.

**What is explicit.** There is a wisdom of this world by which the world did not know God. There is a wisdom of God spoken among the mature. Faith is not to stand in the wisdom of men. Enticing words of man’s wisdom are refused as the ground of faith. The wise, the scribe, and the disputer are asked for, and the question is a judgment.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** A philosophical or scribal system, as the wisdom of this world, is not the means by which God is known and not the ground on which faith stands. Academic status — wise, scribe, disputer — is named and refused as that ground. The refusal is not a refusal of wisdom as such. Wisdom of God is affirmed in the next breath. The rank is the content of the affirmation: the wisdom that measures is God’s, spoken in the gospel, not the system of man’s wisdom. A later Christian philosophy that offers itself as the prior wisdom in which faith must stand repeats the Corinthian error under a new name. A later use of reason that remains under the preaching and under the writing does not.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That learning is a vice. The call of not many wise after the flesh, in verse 26, is a description of the Corinthian calling, not a law against knowledge. That the labour of reasoning through the Word has been forbidden because enticing words are refused. The ground of faith is the object. The labour remains later. That “foolishness” means the writings are irrational. The foolishness is the world’s name for the cross, not the apostle’s account of Scripture.

Thus:

> **The wisdom of this world, including the wise, the scribe, and the disputer, is not the court by which God is known. The wisdom of God is another wisdom. Philosophy as the world’s system does not become primary by its claim to be wisdom.**

# 68.24 Tradition as a System: One Noun, Two Relations

Tradition becomes visible wherever a people holds a delivered order as binding. The system-question is what was delivered, and how the delivered thing stands toward the word of God.

The apostolic charge to hold the traditions taught by word or epistle is a charge to hold the deposit. The epistle is writing. The word, in that charge, is the apostolic teaching, not a permission for every later speaker to add an unwritten rule and call it the same tradition. What the apostle received of the Lord and delivered, and what the epistle then writes, stands as apostolic delivery. A later custom may be old and may be orderly. It is that delivery only if it is that content.

The tradition of the elders is a different system using the same noun. It is corporate. Mark records it as held by the Pharisees and all the Jews. It functions as the measure of transgression: the disciples are accused of transgressing it. It is able to set aside the commandment of God while being held as the religious way. Its antiquity is the antiquity of the elders and of the fathers. Its religious seriousness is worship. Its effect, where it conflicts, is that the word of God is made of none effect.

A third biblical note prevents a simple scorn of age. The old paths are to be asked for, where the good way is. Antiquity of the good way is a reason to walk in it. Antiquity without the good way is not a reason. What was heard as said of old may be the commandment, or the commandment with an addition the commandment does not contain. The hearer does not settle the question by the age of the hearing.

The system of tradition is therefore judged by content and by relation, not by the honourable sound of the word “tradition,” and not by a reflex against memory. Memory of the deposit serves. Memory installed as a court over the deposit rivals.

Thus:

> **Tradition is a system of delivery. Delivery of the apostolic deposit, including the epistle, serves the Graphē. Delivery of the commandments of men over the commandment of God rivals it. Age is not the difference. Relation to the word is the difference.**

# 68.25 Lexical Note: παράδοσις (*paradosis*, G3862)

The noun παράδοσις occurs in the New Testament in a closed set of places. Matthew 15:2, 3, 6 and Mark 7:3, 5, 8, 9, 13 use it of the tradition of the elders and of men, by which the commandment and the word of God are transgressed, laid aside, rejected, or made of none effect. Galatians 1:14 uses it of the traditions of the fathers, the object of Paul’s former zeal in Judaism. Colossians 2:8 uses it of the tradition of men as the pattern of the warned philosophy. 1 Corinthians 11:2, 2 Thessalonians 2:15, and 2 Thessalonians 3:6 use it of what the apostle delivered and what the believers received from him, including delivery by epistle.

The lexical fact is the distinction. One noun, two relations. In the apostolic uses, the content is what Paul delivered, received in turn, in the supper passage, from the Lord, and taught by word and by epistle. In the other uses, the content is the tradition of men, of the elders, or of the fathers, set over against the commandment of God, the gospel, or Christ.

The English word “tradition” is therefore not allowed, in this argument, to borrow the honour of 2 Thessalonians 2:15 for the substance of Mark 7:8. It is equally not allowed to borrow the condemnation of Mark 7:8 for the act of holding the apostolic epistle. The system is identified by asking: what was handed over, by whom, and does the handed thing sit under the word of God or over it?

The verb παραδίδωμι confirms the same split. The Lord delivers a supper tradition to Paul, and Paul delivers it in writing. Men deliver a tradition through which the word is made of none effect. Delivery as an act is neutral until its object and its relation are named. The system called tradition is the delivered object treated as the rule.

Thus:

> **παράδοσις names handing-over. Apostolic handing-over of the deposit is one system. The tradition of men over the commandment of God is another. The word does not permit the second to wear the first’s authority, or the first to be condemned as the second.**

# 68.26 Biblical Record: 2 Thessalonians 2:15, 3:6, and 1 Corinthians 11:2, 23

2 Thessalonians 2:15 commands: stand fast, and hold the traditions which ye have been taught, whether by word, or our epistle. 2 Thessalonians 3:6 commands withdrawal from every brother who walks disorderly and not after the tradition which he received of us. 1 Corinthians 11:2 praises the Corinthians that they keep the ordinances — the traditions — as Paul delivered them. 1 Corinthians 11:23 states: I have received of the Lord that which also I delivered unto you, and the delivery is then written: the supper on the night of the betrayal.

**Context.** The speaker is Paul. The audiences are the Thessalonian and Corinthian churches. The occasion of 2 Thessalonians 2 is the charge to stand, after instruction about the day of the Lord, in what they were already taught. The occasion of 1 Corinthians 11 is order in the assembly and the supper. The epistle is named as one of the two modes of the teaching. The reception is from the Lord, and the delivery is the apostle’s, now inscribed.

**What is explicit.** There are traditions to be held. They were taught. One mode is word; the other is the apostolic epistle. Disorder is departure from the tradition received from the apostle. The supper-tradition was received from the Lord and delivered, and the delivery stands in the letter as sentences the church can read.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Tradition, in the apostolic sense, is a servant and a form of the deposit, not a licence for a later unwritten rule to govern the epistle. The epistle is itself the tradition in written form. Holding it is holding the writing. The spoken teaching of the apostle is not a second and higher canon beside his writing; the church is commanded to hold both as what he taught, and what he taught is now possessed, for these matters, in the writing that has come down inside the Graphē. A later system cannot appeal to “tradition” in the abstract and inherit this charge. It must show that what it holds is what was taught by that word and that epistle. Where it shows this, it serves. Where it adds a further rule and gives the further rule the same name, it has changed the object.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That every custom of a later century, if old enough, is “the tradition ye have been taught.” The teachers are identified. That unwritten expansion is authorized because “by word” is mentioned. The word in view is the apostolic teaching already given to those churches, not an open category. That the displacement of Scripture by later tradition has been fully traced. The rank is the point: this tradition is the deposit, and it includes the epistle as writing.

Thus:

> **Apostolic tradition is the deposit taught by word and by epistle, received from the Lord where the apostle so received it. Holding it is service of the writing. The name does not travel to a later rule the epistle does not teach.**

# 68.27 Biblical Record: Mark 7:1–13 and Matthew 15:1–9 as a System

Mark 7:1–13 and Matthew 15:1–9 record Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem asking why the disciples transgress the tradition of the elders, for they eat bread with unwashed hands. Mark notes that the Pharisees and all the Jews, holding the tradition of the elders, do not eat unless they wash, and that there are many other such things they have received to hold. Jesus answers that Isaiah wrote of them: this people honoureth me with their lips, but their heart is far from me; in vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men. He says they lay aside the commandment of God and hold the tradition of men. Moses said, Honour thy father and thy mother. Their tradition of Corban releases a man from that duty. He concludes: making the word of God of none effect through your tradition, which ye have delivered. And many such like things do ye.

**Context.** The speaker of the verdict is Jesus. The accusers are a learned delegation invoking a corporate tradition. The audience includes the multitude. The occasion is a purity practice treated as transgression when omitted. The writings cited are Isaiah 29:13 and the command of Moses. Chapter 66 already used the scene to distinguish two objects, the word of God and the tradition of men. Chapter 67 already used it as improper handling that evacuates force. The present function is the tradition as a system: received to hold, corporate across “all the Jews,” many such things, delivered by the party, functioning as the definition of transgression, and capable of a particular device, Corban, that nullifies a named commandment.

**What is explicit.** The tradition of the elders is a held, received, plural order. It is the standard by which the disciples are said to transgress. The commandment of God is a different object, and it is laid aside, rejected, and made of none effect through the tradition. Commandments of men are taught as doctrines. Lip-honour and farness of heart accompany the teaching. The tradition is “your tradition, which ye have delivered.” Delivery is conceded. The thing delivered is the problem. The practice is not a single gesture. “Many such like things” names a system.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Tradition as a system can be ancient, learned, corporate, religious, and delivered, and can still stand over the word of God as a rival rule. Antiquity is the tradition of the elders. Academic and institutional status is the scribal and Pharisaic seat, come from Jerusalem. Religious identity is worship, honour of God with the lips, and the claim to guard holiness at table. None of these confers on the tradition the standing of the commandment. The system’s own question — why do thy disciples transgress the tradition? — assumes the tradition is the measure. Jesus’ question — why do ye also transgress the commandment of God by your tradition? — reverses the measure. The reversal is the doctrine of rank. The word of God remains in force. The tradition’s delivery does not rewrite it.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That every received practice of washing or of order is Corban. The text identifies the relation: commandment laid aside. A practice that does not lay the commandment aside is not this verdict. That the finished account of tradition governing interpretation has been written. The system is ranked here. The way a later commentary-tradition comes to be the only permitted sense of a sentence remains Chapter 69. That the Pharisees’ doctrine has been equated with every later body that honours elders. The structure of the claim is the warrant, not a map of later names.

Thus:

> **The tradition of the elders is a system: received, corporate, delivered, and used as the measure of transgression. Christian identity is not required for the structure to be clear. Learning, age, worship, and institutional seat do not make the tradition the word of God. The commandment remains the measure.**

# 68.28 Biblical Record: Galatians 1:13–14 and 1 Peter 1:18

Galatians 1:13–14 records Paul’s former manner of life in the Jews’ religion: beyond measure he persecuted the church of God, and profited in the Jews’ religion above many his equals, being more exceedingly zealous of the traditions of his fathers. The neighbouring argument is that the gospel he preached is not after man, that he neither received it of man nor was taught it, but by the revelation of Jesus Christ. 1 Peter 1:18–19 states that the believers were not redeemed with corruptible things from their vain conversation received by tradition from their fathers, but with the precious blood of Christ.

**Context.** In Galatians the speaker is Paul, proving the origin of his gospel to churches tempted to another gospel. In 1 Peter the speaker is Peter, grounding the manner of life in redemption. Both name ancestral tradition. One names it as the object of exceptional zeal and religious profit. The other names it as the conversation from which redemption delivers.

**What is explicit.** Traditions of the fathers can be the centre of a zeal that advances a man above his contemporaries. That zeal and that profit are not the origin of the gospel. A conversation can be received by tradition from the fathers and still be vain, and redemption from it is by Christ, not by a further refinement of the same conversation.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Antiquity and religious success do not promote a tradition-system to the rank of the gospel or of the redeeming work of Christ. Paul does not describe his ancestral tradition as ignorant of Scripture in these verses. He describes it as zealously held, and as not the source of the gospel. Academic and communal superiority — “above many my equals” — accompanies the traditions. It does not authenticate them as the message. Peter does not say the fathers’ tradition was recent or frivolous. He says the conversation received by it was vain, and that the price of deliverance was Christ’s blood. Ancestral delivery is a real system. It is a secondary system. Where it is the vain conversation, it is not healed by age.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That every practice of the fathers in Israel was vain. Paul elsewhere honours the oracles committed to Israel. The object here is the traditions of which he was zealous as an alternative to the revealed gospel, and the vain conversation Peter names. That turning aside from truth has been examined as tergiversation. The contrast of origins is the rank. The pathology of departure remains later.

Thus:

> **Zeal for the traditions of the fathers, and religious profit in them, is not the gospel. A conversation received by ancestral tradition can be the bondage from which Christ redeems. Antiquity and advancement do not confer primary standing.**

# 68.29 Biblical Record: Jeremiah 6:16 and Matthew 5:21, 43

Jeremiah 6:16 says: Stand ye in the ways, and see, and ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk therein, and ye shall find rest for your souls. But they said, We will not walk therein. Matthew 5:21 records, Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill; and Jesus continues, But I say unto you that whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause is in danger of the judgment. Matthew 5:43 records, Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy; and He commands love of enemies.

**Context.** Jeremiah speaks the word of the Lord to Judah, who refuse the good way. Matthew 5 stands inside the Sermon on the Mount, after the statement that He came not to destroy the law or the prophets but to fulfil, and that one jot or tittle shall in no wise pass from the law. The speaker of “But I say” is Jesus. The “ye have heard” is the people’s reception of what was said of old.

**What is explicit.** There are old paths, and they are to be sought where the good way is. Refusal to walk is named. What was said of old includes “Thou shalt not kill,” which is the commandment, and Jesus presses that commandment to the anger of the heart. What was heard as said also includes “hate thine enemy,” which the commandment to love the neighbour does not say. His word addresses both the commandment and the addition.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Antiquity is not despised, and antiquity is not enthroned. The old paths matter because they are the good way, and the hearer must ask and see, not merely inherit a slogan of age. A saying received as ancient may be the writing itself, in which case His fulfilment does not loosen it. A saying received as ancient may add what the writing does not contain, in which case the addition’s age is not authority. The tradition-system that identifies “what we have always heard” with the commandment has skipped the test these passages perform. The test is correspondence to what was commanded and to what He says, not the calendar of the hearing.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That Jesus opposes the law by being later than Moses. The fulfilment-statement forbids that reading. That every ancestral sentence is an addition. “Thou shalt not kill” is not an addition. The distinction between the commandment and the heard addition is the warrant. That a full account of the law’s relation to the gospel has been written. The antiquity-claim is the object.

Thus:

> **Ask for the old paths where the good way is. What was said of old may be the commandment or the commandment plus a heard addition. Age does not decide which. The word decides. Antiquity remains a property, not a court.**

# 68.30 Institution as a System: The House That Upholds, and the House That Claims the Seat

Institution becomes visible wherever an ordered body teaches, recognizes, commands, or expels, and wherever that body is treated as the measure of the word rather than as the servant and uphold of the word.

The writings honour order. They speak of a house of God. They seat a teaching chair derivative of Moses and then judge the sitters. They record apostles and elders gathering to a question and citing what is written. They call churches to hear what the Spirit saith. They also record a council commanding the apostles to be silent, a synagogue casting out a man whose eyes were opened, rulers who will not confess Christ lest they lose their place, and a man who loves preeminence and will not receive the apostolic witness. The same category — ordered body — contains service and usurpation. The category is not the verdict. The relation is the verdict.

The strongest institutional claim, and the one this chapter must meet rather than avoid, is that the church is the pillar and ground of the truth, and therefore the institution is primary and the writing is the institution’s book. The section on 1 Timothy 3:15 will meet that claim from the sentence itself. The warrant in advance is only this: a pillar upholds. It does not become the building’s author by upholding. A ground supports. It does not become the truth it supports. The letter in which the phrase occurs is written to tell the house how to behave. The writing instructs the institution in the same breath in which it honours the institution’s office.

Chapter 52’s premise governs the rest. Bodies receive writings. Reception is not creation. A later claim that the receiving body is therefore the higher court repeats, at the level of institution, the error Chapter 65 already refused as a limit and this chapter now examines as a system.

Thus:

> **Institution is a real order, capable of upholding the truth and capable of commanding against God. The house is not the author of the word that addresses the house. Recognition is not creation. Office is service unless it claims the measure.**

# 68.31 Biblical Record: 1 Timothy 3:14–15

1 Timothy 3:14–15 states that Paul writes these things in order that Timothy may know how he ought to behave himself in the house of God, which is the church of the living God, the pillar and ground of the truth.

**Context.** The speaker is Paul. The addressee is Timothy. The occasion is instruction about conduct in the house, after qualifications for overseers and deacons. The church is called the house of the living God, στῦλος καὶ ἑδραίωμα τῆς ἀληθείας (*stylos kai hedraiōma tēs alētheias*): pillar and ground, stay, support, of the truth. The sentence occurs inside a letter. “These things write I unto thee” is the frame.

**What is explicit.** There is a house of God. It is the church of the living God. It is pillar and ground of the truth. Behaviour in it is the subject of the writing. The writing is the means by which Timothy is to know the behaviour.

**What the passage warrants for this system.** The institution has a lofty office: it upholds the truth in the world as the living God’s house. That office is service of the truth, not authorship of the truth, and not a court above the letter that orders the house. The truth is already the truth the letter is concerned with. The pillar does not precede the writing as its judge. The writing tells the pillar how to behave. Institutional dignity is here at its highest biblical description, and the description still subordinates the house to the truth and instructs the house by writing. A later claim that “pillar and ground” means “author and higher court of Scripture” adds what the sentence, in its frame, does not say. The addition is itself a theological claim, and it stands under the same rank this chapter is establishing.

**What the passage does not warrant here.** A completed polity of bishops and deacons as this chapter’s achievement. The qualifications are the letter’s context, not the system-file’s subject. That every later body calling itself the church inherits the title automatically. The house of the living God is identified by the truth it upholds and by the conduct the writing requires, not by the claim of the title. That the displacement of Scripture by church authority has been traced as a history. The sentence’s own relation is the warrant.

Thus:

> **The church is pillar and ground of the truth: the house that upholds the truth. The writing instructs the house. The pillar does not become the truth’s author, and the institution does not become the Graphē’s higher court, by being given this office.**

# 68.32 Biblical Record: Matthew 23:2–3 and Jeremiah 8:8–9

Matthew 23:2–3 states that the scribes and the Pharisees sit in Moses’ seat; all therefore whatsoever they bid you observe, that observe and do; but do not ye after their works, for they say, and do not. The chapter then pronounces woes on the same sitters for burdens, for shut kingdoms, and for straining at a gnat and swallowing a camel. Jeremiah 8:8–9 asks how they can say, We are wise, and the law of the Lord is with us. The pen of the scribes is in vain. The wise men are ashamed. They have rejected the word of the Lord; and what wisdom is in them?

**Context.** In Matthew the speaker is Jesus, in the temple, addressing the multitude and the disciples about the teachers who currently sit in the teaching seat of Moses. In Jeremiah the speaker is the prophet, against a wisdom that claims possession of the law. Both scenes have an institutional-intellectual object: a seated, scribal order.

**What is explicit.** There is a seat of Moses, and identifiable men sit in it. Observance is commanded of what they bid from that seat, and imitation of their works is forbidden. The same chapter condemns their practice. In Jeremiah, a claim of wisdom and of possessing the law coexists with a vain scribal pen and with rejection of the word of the Lord.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** A teaching institution can be derivative of the writing. Moses’ seat is Moses’ seat, not the scribes’ own invention as a new source. What binds in the bidding is what belongs to that seat, which is Moses’ teaching, the writing. The sitter is not the seat. His works are not the measure of his bidding. Mark 7’s tradition, held by the same parties, is not baptized by the seat. Occupancy and the tradition of men remain two, and only the writing’s content, not the occupant’s status, is the reason for observance. Jeremiah adds the scribal claim in its institutional voice: wisdom, possession of the law, and a pen. The claim can be loud while the word is rejected. Academic status and official handling of the law do not guarantee standing with the word. They are compatible with rejection.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That the scribes are infallible whenever they speak, because “whatsoever they bid” is absolute in English isolation. The seat is Moses’ seat; the works are excluded; the woes follow; the tradition that voids the commandment has already been judged. Isolation of verse 3 from those neighbours would be the context-failure Chapter 67 already catalogued. That every scribe in Israel rejected the word. The prophetic charge names the claim and the rejection it can conceal. The rank is the point.

Thus:

> **Moses’ seat is derivative of Moses. The sitter is not Moses. A scribal institution can claim wisdom and the law while rejecting the word. Institutional seat and academic pen do not confer primary standing.**

# 68.33 Biblical Record: Acts 15:1–29

Acts 15:1–29 records a dispute from Judaea: except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be saved. Paul and Barnabas dissent. The church at Antioch sends them to Jerusalem to the apostles and elders. The apostles and elders come together to consider the matter. Peter asks why God should be tempted by putting a yoke upon the disciples which neither their fathers nor they were able to bear, and he appeals to God’s giving of the Spirit and to cleansing of hearts by faith. James answers that to this agree the words of the prophets, as it is written, and cites the rebuilding of the tabernacle of David from Amos. The apostles and elders, with the whole church, send a letter: it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us, to lay upon you no greater burden than these necessary things.

**Context.** The speakers are apostles and elders, with the church assembled. The occasion is a teaching that makes circumcision after Moses necessary to salvation for Gentiles. The cited writing is the prophets. The decision is a letter. Chapter 53 has already established apostolic authority as authority of that commissioned witness, not as a blank cheque for every later title. The present use is the gathering as an institution under that already-established limit.

**What is explicit.** A gathered body of apostles and elders considers a doctrinal crisis. The consideration appeals to what God has done and to what is written in the prophets. A sentence is reached and written as a letter. The “us” of “it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us” is that apostolic gathering, not a pronoun without a referent.

**What the passage warrants for this system.** An ordered institution can serve a question under the writing. The prophets are cited as agreement, not as raw material the gathering is free to overrule. The letter serves the churches by relieving them of a yoke the gathering refuses to impose. Service of this kind is real. What the passage does not do is make every later institution that says “it seemed good to us” the same “us.” The speakers are apostles and elders in the generation of the witness. Their act does not promote a subsequent body into the Graphē, and it does not make the gathering itself a writing that outranks the prophets it cites. A later council that treats Acts 15 as a precedent for institutional supremacy has used a scene of subordination to the prophetic writing as a charter for the reverse. The use is a claim. The scene remains a scene of service.

**What the passage does not warrant here.** A general theory that church councils are inspired authors of doctrine. The text identifies these persons and this appeal to what is written. That the content of the necessary things has been turned into a new legal system. The burden refused is the point of rank. That later controversies about the decree have been settled as history. The relation of gathering to writing is the warrant.

Thus:

> **Apostles and elders gathered can serve a question by appeal to what God has done and to what is written. The gathering is not the Graphē. A later “it seemed good to us” does not inherit this gathering’s pronoun.**

# 68.34 Biblical Record: Acts 17:11 and Galatians 2:1–9

Acts 17:11 states that the Bereans were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so. Galatians 2:1–9 records Paul’s conference with those who seemed to be somewhat. Whatsoever they were, it makes no matter to him: God accepteth no man’s person. Those who seemed to be somewhat in conference added nothing to him. James, Cephas, and John, who seemed to be pillars, gave him and Barnabas the right hands of fellowship, that they should go to the Gentiles.

**Context.** At Berea the speakers of the tested word include Paul. The testers are the synagogue community. The measure of the test is the scriptures. In Galatians the speaker is Paul. The persons of repute are the Jerusalem pillars. The occasion is the defence of a gospel not received from men. Chapter 66 already used Acts 17:11 as testing speech by the writings, and Galatians 1:8–9 as even an apostle judged by the deposit. The present function is institutional recognition: a noble community’s relation to a teacher, and a pillars’ conference as recognition that adds nothing.

**What is explicit.** Searching the scriptures is nobility, exercised upon an apostolic word, to see whether those things were so. Seeming to be somewhat, and seeming to be pillars, is a real public status. God does not accept a man’s person. The conference added nothing. Fellowship was given. Addition and fellowship are distinguished by the narrative itself.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Institutional recognition, even at the highest level inside the apostolic generation, does not create the content of the gospel and does not outrank the scriptures. The Berean institution — the synagogue that searches — is noble because it uses the writings as the measure of a teacher, not because the synagogue is the measure. The pillar institution is honoured with fellowship and denied the power to add. If recognition by James, Cephas, and John added nothing, recognition by a later body does not add the primary written authority the pillars themselves neither possessed as authors of Paul’s gospel nor conferred. Chapter 52’s reception-is-not-creation is the same structure at the level of corpus. Here it is the structure at the level of persons and offices.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That teachers are unnecessary because Bereans search. They received the word with readiness and tested it. Reception of a teacher and testing by the writings are compatible. That fellowship among apostles is worthless because addition is denied. Fellowship is recorded as given. The denial attaches to addition, not to communion. That the displacement of a teacher’s word over the text has been written as a pathology. The rank of recognition is the point.

Thus:

> **Nobility searches the scriptures whether the teacher’s things are so. Pillars may give the right hand of fellowship and still add nothing. Institutional recognition, even apostolic recognition, is not authorship and not a higher court.**

# 68.35 Biblical Record: Acts 4:19–20, 5:27–29, and 3 John 9–10

Acts 4:19–20 records Peter and John before the council: whether it be right in the sight of God to hearken unto you more than unto God, judge ye; for we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard. Acts 5:28–29 records the high priest’s charge that they had filled Jerusalem with their doctrine, and Peter’s answer, with the other apostles: we ought to obey God rather than men. 3 John 9–10 records Diotrephes, who loveth to have the preeminence among them, receiveth us not, and who prates against the brethren with malicious words, refuses the brethren, forbids those who would receive them, and casts them out of the church.

**Context.** In Acts the institution is the Sanhedrin, commanding silence about Jesus. The speakers who refuse are apostles, appealing to what they have seen and heard and to obedience to God. In 3 John the institution is a church in which one man holds preeminence, and the witness refused is the elder. The speaker is the elder. Diotrephes prates against the elder with malicious words, does not receive the brethren, forbids those who would, and casts them out of the church.

**What is explicit.** A council’s command can stand in conflict with hearkening to God. Obedience to God is required rather than obedience to men when the two diverge. Preeminence in a church can coexist with refusal of the elder, malicious words against him, and expulsion of those who receive the brethren.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Institution as a commanding and excluding body is not self-authenticating. The council has jurisdiction in its own eyes. The apostles do not treat that jurisdiction as the measure of what they have seen and heard or of what God requires. Diotrephes has the preeminence and the power to cast out. The elder does not treat the preeminence or the expulsion as the truth of the matter. Institutional recognition and institutional rejection are acts of the body. They establish who is in and who is out of the body’s fellowship. They do not establish what God has said, and they do not erase a witness the body refuses. The positive counterpart is already in force from the pillar passage: a house may uphold. These passages are the house and the council when they claim the hearer’s obedience against God and against the witness.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** A general licence to disregard every ordered decision. The conflict named is hearkening to men more than to God, and refusal of the apostolic witness for the sake of preeminence. Order that upholds the truth is not these scenes. That the psychology of preeminence has been written as a pathology. The institutional act is the rank-evidence.

Thus:

> **A council can command silence, and a churchman can cast out, without becoming the measure of God or of the witness. Obedience to God outranks the institutional command when the two conflict. Preeminence is not truth.**

# 68.36 Biblical Record: John 7:15–17, Acts 4:13, and Matthew 23:8–10

John 7:15–17 records the Jews marvelling: How knoweth this man letters, having never learned? Jesus answers that His doctrine is not His, but His that sent Him. If any man will do His will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God, or whether He speaks of Himself. Acts 4:13 records the council perceiving that Peter and John were unlearned and ignorant men, and marvelling, and taking knowledge of them that they had been with Jesus. Matthew 23:8–10 tells the disciples not to be called Rabbi, for one is their Master, even Christ, and all they are brethren; and not to call any man father upon the earth, for one is their Father; and not to be called masters, for one is their Master, even Christ.

**Context.** In John 7 the setting is the feast, and the marvel is academic: letters without the school. The answer relocates doctrine in the Sender. In Acts 4 the perceivers are the rulers and elders and scribes. The perception of unlearnedness is theirs; the knowledge they cannot avoid is that the men had been with Jesus. In Matthew 23 the speaker is Jesus, immediately after the saying about Moses’ seat, and the subject is titles of instructional mastery among His disciples.

**What is explicit.** Knowing letters is a status the hearers treat as the ordinary condition of doctrine. Jesus’ doctrine is from the One who sent Him. Doing the will is the path to knowing whether the doctrine is of God. Unlearned men are recognized as having been with Jesus. Titles that install a human rabbi, father, or master over the brethren are refused, because the Master and the Father are one.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Academic status is neither necessary nor sufficient for a doctrine to be of God. The absence of the school does not disqualify what the Sender gave. The presence of the school, in the scribes of the same Gospel, does not qualify the tradition of men. “Having been with Jesus” is a different credential from letters, and the council’s own perception records it. Titles of mastery are real social institutions of learning. They are refused as a second master over the disciples. The refusal is not a ban on the word “teacher” wherever the writings appoint teachers. It is a ban on the title becoming the master. Christ remains the Master. The writing that testifies of Him remains the measure of those who teach in His name. Academic status does not confer primary authority.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That study is disobedience. The marvel of the unlearned is the council’s category, not a command to remain ignorant. The scholarly labour of the Graphē remains Movement X. That every use of a respectful title is Matthew 23:8. The evil named is the installation of a human master in the place of Christ. The rank of academic institution is the warrant.

Thus:

> **Letters without the Sender are not the origin of doctrine. Unlearnedness with Jesus is not the disqualification the council imagines. A human master-title does not become the Master. Academic status does not confer the Graphē’s standing.**

# 68.37 Biblical Record: Acts 11:26, Matthew 7:21–23, 2 Timothy 3:5, and Revelation 2:9

Acts 11:26 states that the disciples were called Christians first in Antioch. 1 Peter 4:16 adds that if any man suffer as a Christian, let him not be ashamed, but let him glorify God on this behalf. Matthew 7:21–23 states that not every one that saith Lord, Lord, shall enter the kingdom, but he that doeth the will of the Father. Many will say in that day that they prophesied, cast out devils, and did many wonderful works in His name. He will profess, I never knew you. 2 Timothy 3:5 describes men having a form of godliness but denying the power thereof. Revelation 2:9 and 3:9 speak of those who say they are Jews, and are not.

**Context.** “Christian” is a name attached to disciples at Antioch, and a name under which a person may suffer. Matthew 7 is Jesus’ own closing of the Sermon, distinguishing saying and doing, and distinguishing works done in the name from being known by Him. 2 Timothy 3 stands in a charge about perilous times and men of corrupt mind, set beside the holy scriptures Timothy has known. Revelation 2–3 is Christ’s word to the churches. The claim to be Jews is a claim of covenant identity. The verdict is that the claim is false.

**What is explicit.** Disciples bear the name Christian. The name can be the name of suffering that glorifies God. Saying “Lord” and working in the name can coexist with not doing the Father’s will and with not being known. A form of godliness can deny the power. A claim to be what one is not can be made in religious identity, and the claim can be false while it is confidently made.

**What the passages warrant for this system.** Christian identity is a real name for disciples of Christ. It is not a property a system acquires by using the name. Works in the name, a form of godliness, and a claim of covenant identity are exactly the kinds of religious self-description that later theology and later institutions offer as warrants. The writings treat the self-description as accountable. Doing the Father’s will, the power the form denies, and the true identity as against the claim, are not established by the claim. A theology, a tradition, or an institution that presents its Christian name as the reason it cannot be measured by the Graphē has used a name the writings never treat as immune. The name sends the bearer back to Christ. Christ is known in the writing that testifies of Him. The name is not a second Graphē.

**What the passages do not warrant here.** That the word “Christian” is illegitimate. The disciples were called Christians, and Peter honours suffering as a Christian. The evil is the untested claim, not the name of a disciple. That every professing body is Matthew 7:23. The passage names a verdict Christ will speak, not a permission for this chapter to pronounce it on later houses. That accepted heresies have been listed because a form of godliness appears. The form is rank-evidence: profession is not power, and identity-claims are not primary authority.

Thus:

> **“Christian” names disciples, and it can name a true suffering. Saying the name, working in the name, and wearing the form are claims. They do not confer the primary written authority, and they do not exempt a system from the writing.**

# 68.38 The Four Claims Joined

The four systems and the four claims are not two parallel lists that never meet. They meet in the ordinary promotion.

A theology is most often promoted by Christian identity: this is the Christian doctrine, therefore it is what the Graphē teaches. A philosophy is most often promoted by academic status: the wise, the scribe, and the disputer have settled it, therefore the writing yields. A tradition is most often promoted by antiquity: the elders and the fathers delivered it, therefore it is the living form of the word. An institution is most often promoted by recognition: the house received the books, seated the teachers, and cast out the dissenters, therefore the house is the court.

The fit is typical, not exclusive. An institution may claim all four. A tradition may claim Christian identity. A theology may claim a university. The locked assignment refuses the claims as claims, wherever they are attached. Biblical record has now exhibited each claim inside the writings’ own judgments. Christian identity does not make saying “Lord” into doing the Father’s will. Academic status does not make letters into the Sender’s doctrine, and does not make Athens the court of the resurrection. Antiquity does not make the tradition of the elders into the commandment, and does not make the fathers’ conversation into redemption. Institutional recognition does not make the pillars authors, the council God, or the pillar the truth it upholds.

The join is the chapter’s particular addition to Chapter 14 and Chapter 65. Those chapters already refused the promotions as limits on human accounts and on the gate. This chapter has examined the systems the promotions inhabit. The refusal is now a doctrine of relation, with each system defined, each claim defined, and each claim matched to the writings that judge it.

Thus:

> **Identity, academy, antiquity, and recognition are the four ordinary ways a secondary system is treated as primary. Each way fails in the writings. The failure is one failure of rank, stated four times because the systems enter by four doors.**

# 68.39 Reconstructing the Bible-Introduction Proposition: The Notes Are a Secondary System, and the Canon Was Not Created by the Church

A reconstructible proposition from the Bible Introduction materials states two things that must be kept apart. First, the work itself is a body of notes, headings, cross-references, and helps drawn from commentaries, dictionaries, concordances, and doctrinal syntheses; its stated aim is to prove the teachings of the notes by plain Bible references and to invite the reader to see what the related passages are actually saying; novel interpretations and theories supported by personal opinion alone are laid aside. Second, the Old Testament canon was at first gradual and not created by ecclesiastical authority; the beginning was made by Moses; the writings were a settled subject in the days of Christ and the apostles, proved by their own appeal to them; and that settled subject should not have been reopened by any church as if the church were the author of the collection. The same materials also narrate later councils as the place where the New Testament question was finally settled, and narrate a later council as accepting books previously rejected.

The proposition is not adopted as “the source says.” It is tested.

**What is claimed.** A note-system is a servant of the writing, obligated to show the passages. The corpus was not created by a church’s decision. A later church ought not to treat a settled scriptural subject as raw material for its own authority.

**Premises already in force.** The Graphē is primary (Chapter 65). Recognition is not creation (Chapter 52). A commentary is not the text (Chapter 14). Notes about the writing are not the writing (Chapter 66’s use of this same materials’ distinction).

**Biblical correspondence.** Christ and the apostles appeal to the writings as already Scripture: Matthew 21:42, Matthew 22:29, Luke 24:44, John 10:35, 2 Timothy 3:15–17. No scene in those appeals waits for a later council before the writing binds. Timothy is told how to behave in the house by a letter, not told that the house will decide what the letter is. The Bereans search the scriptures whether apostolic things are so.

**What follows.** The note-method, controlled as subordination, coheres with the rank of theology: a doctrinal note is a secondary system, true only as it states what the passages say, and obligated to let the reader past the note to the writing. The claim that ecclesiastical authority did not create the Old Testament canon coheres with recognition as reception. The claim that a later church must not reopen the settled writings as their author coheres with this chapter’s refusal of institutional supremacy.

**What does not follow, and is not adopted.** The narrative that particular later councils are the moment the books became Scripture is extra-biblical colour. It is not a premise of this chapter. Taken as a story of reception, it is neighbourhood of Chapter 52, not a new origin. Taken as a story of creation, it contradicts the Introduction’s own sentence that the canon was not created by ecclesiastical authority, and it contradicts the premises already in force. A later institutional act that receives additional books as inspired is an institutional claim. The act does not, by being a council, confer inspiration. That is the rank. The finished account of a secondary source governing the corpus remains later.

Thus:

> **The reconstructible note-method is received as coherence: notes are a secondary system under the passages. The reconstructible claim that the church did not create the canon is received as coherence with recognition as reception. A council-narrative of origin is not adopted. Institutional decision remains a claim, not the Graphē.**

# 68.40 Reconstructing the Bible-Introduction Proposition: What Philosophical Construction Does Not Supply

A second reconstructible proposition from the same materials states that the ignorance of man regarding his origin, his past, and his eternal future, his lack of knowledge concerning the will of God, and the failure of philosophers to construct a complete, coherent, and adequate religion, together make revelation absolutely necessary. Christianity is described as a revealed religion, the record being the sacred Scriptures. Revelation discovers new truth; inspiration superintends the communication and recording of it. A further note in the same materials says that a deity who had to be interpreted every time he spoke, before his speech could mean, would not be worthy of consideration, and that the fault in complications lies with men who will not take what God says.

The proposition is not adopted as “the source says.” It is tested.

**What is claimed.** Philosophical construction does not supply what revelation supplies. The record of revelation is the Scriptures. A system that inserts itself as the necessary interpreter before the speech can bind has reversed the order between the Speaker and the system.

**Premises.** Knowing God is by revelation rather than speculation (Chapter 13). The record does not fail as communication (Chapter 57). Stating sense is a real and accountable act, and is not forbidden (Chapter 66). Misreading is the reader’s failure, not the writing’s silence (Chapter 67).

**Biblical correspondence.** 1 Corinthians 1:21: the world by wisdom knew not God. Colossians 2:3, 8: treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hid in Christ; a philosophy not after Christ spoils. Acts 17: Paul declares God to the philosophers rather than receiving God from them. Deuteronomy 29:29: the revealed things belong to the people that they may do them. 2 Timothy 3:16: the writing is profitable for doctrine without a proviso that a philosophical school must first authorize the profit.

**What follows.** The proposition coheres at the level of rank. A philosophical system is not a substitute for the making-known, and therefore cannot be the primary written authority concerning the things only that making-known gives. The universal census “all philosophers have failed” is stronger than the writings’ claim and is not adopted. What is adopted is the rank: failure to be revelation is enough. The further note coheres only in a controlled form. The writing communicates, and a system may not claim that the writing is silent until the system speaks. The note’s stronger suggestion, that any stating of sense is a fault, has already been refused. Chapter 66 distinguished interpretation from authority. Chapter 67 refused a ban on sense. This chapter uses only the coherent remainder: the system is not the condition of the writing’s speech.

**What does not follow here.** That reason is excluded from the investigation. That every philosophical sentence is false. That Movement X’s account of reasoning through the Word has been pre-empted by a warning against schools.

Thus:

> **The reconstructible proposition is received as coherence: philosophical construction does not supply revelation, and a system is not the condition of the writing’s speech. A census of philosophers is not adopted. A ban on stating sense is not adopted. The rank stands.**

# 68.41 Reconstructing the Aletheia Proposition: The Graphē as Premiere Convention for Persons and for Organizations

A reconstructible proposition from the project’s own doctrinal materials states that true life in conformity with Christ pillows in the unleavened sincerity and truth of the Graphē — Holy Writ as writings of unchallenged standing — whether for persons, organizations, governments, or nations; and that this writing is the premiere convention for hearing from God. The same materials name analogical, nonliteral religiousism as the substitute likeness that will not sit under the writing. They also use “imprimatur” as a gloss of that unchallenged standing.

The proposition is not adopted as “the source says.” It is tested.

**What is claimed.** The measure is the same for an ordered body as for a person. An organization does not escape the Graphē by being an organization. The writing’s standing is its own.

**Premises.** The Graphē is the primary written authority (Chapter 65). A later claim is not the writing (Chapter 66). A later likeness that will not sit under the writing is already a misreading (Chapter 67’s tested form of this proposition). Institutions and traditions are secondary (the present argument).

**Biblical correspondence.** Isaiah 8:20 sends a people, not a private reader only, to the law and to the testimony. Acts 17:11 is a synagogue searching. Revelation 2:7 and its parallels call the churches to hear what the Spirit saith. Mark 7 addresses a party’s tradition, not only a private scruple. Matthew 7:21–23 addresses those who say “Lord” and work in the name. The measure is not resized because the speaker is a house.

**What follows.** The proposition coheres as scope. Theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution fall inside the jurisdiction of the premiere convention. They are not a fourth world beside persons, in which another primary authority applies. “Imprimatur,” controlled as a gloss of the writing’s own unchallenged standing, coheres with Chapter 65. Controlled as a later office’s stamp that must be added before the writing binds, it contradicts the rank this chapter has established and is refused. Analogical religiousism, already tested as a mode, is now also visible as a system-temptation: a house may use the writing as decoration of a likeness that will not sit under it. The temptation’s rank is clear here. Its career as governing interpretation remains later.

**What does not follow here.** That governments and nations have been given a political theology. The proposition’s scope is the measure, not a constitution. That the First Principles have been taught because the cornerstone is named in the same materials. That every organization is in rebellion. Scope is not a verdict on each house. A house that upholds the truth is the pillar, not the spoiler.

Thus:

> **The reconstructible proposition is received as coherence: the Graphē is the measure for organizations as for persons. A later stamp is not the writing’s standing. A house may serve under that measure. It does not become the measure by its size.**

# 68.42 Reconstructing the Aletheia Proposition: Organizations That Claim the Name Remain Under the Writing

A second reconstructible proposition from the same materials states that the absence of reasonability and the presence of unskillfulness are responsible for aridity, tergiversation, and accepted heresies among persons and among age-old organizations ostensibly Christianized; and that this lack is blamed on failure to realize the scholarly impetus of the scripture and to labour accordingly.

The proposition is not adopted as “the source says.” It is tested.

**What is claimed.** Bearing the Christian name, and bearing it for a long time, does not remove an organization from accountability to the writing. The name “ostensibly Christianized” is already a distinction between claim and reality.

**What may be used now.** Christian identity and antiquity do not confer primary standing. An organization is within the measure’s scope. Sound judgment is already a defined operation. The five modes are already the ways a later act fails. A system can carry those modes into a public order.

**What may not be used as finished.** The causal chain as a pathology: aridity (Chapter 72), tergiversation (Chapter 70), accepted heresies (Chapter 71), *apeiros* (Chapter 73), scholarly impetus and *spoudazō* (Chapters 74 and 78). Chapter 67 already named that chain as neighbourhood. This chapter does not arrive at it by a second door. The new use is only the rank inside the sentence: an age-old organization that claims the Christian name is still a secondary system. The claim and the age are the two promotions the locked assignment refuses. The materials’ own adjective “ostensibly” agrees with Matthew 7 and Revelation 2:9: saying is not being.

**Biblical correspondence available now.** Matthew 7:21–23; 2 Timothy 3:5; Revelation 2:9; Mark 7: the party is old, learned, and religious, and the word is made of none effect; Galatians 1:14: zeal and ancestral tradition are not the gospel.

Thus:

> **The reconstructible proposition is received only as coherence with the rank: an organization ostensibly Christian, and old, remains under the Graphē. The pathology-files and the labour-files remain later. The name and the age have already failed as promotions.**

# 68.43 Competing Claim: Christian Theology Is the Graphē’s Living Voice

The reduction says:

> **If the system is Christian theology, it is the voice in which the Graphē now speaks. To distinguish the theology from the writing is to deny the faith. The church’s doctrine is what the Bible means. Identity is authority.**

The reduction fails.

Christian identity has been defined and tested. The disciples were called Christians. The name is honourable. The name is not the writing. Matthew 7 distinguishes saying “Lord” and working in the name from doing the Father’s will. 2 Timothy 3:5 distinguishes a form of godliness from the power. Revelation 2:9 distinguishes saying one is a Jew from being one. A theology that follows Christ will be glad to be measured by the writing that testifies of Christ. A theology that claims to be the writing’s living voice, immune to that measurement, has ceased to follow and has begun to rule.

Chapter 66 already refused the transfer of the writing’s authority to the interpreter. The present reduction is that transfer at the level of a system. The system is a corporate interpreter with a Christian name. The name does not perform the transfer the individual name could not perform. Sound doctrine remains a good, and it remains sound only while it holds the faithful word. The moment the doctrine is treated as the word’s necessary voice, the holding has been inverted into a lordship.

The reduction often presents itself as loyalty to the faith once delivered. Loyalty to the deposit is exactly the holding of the apostolic tradition, which includes the epistle. Loyalty to a later system under the deposit’s name is a different loyalty. The writings supply the test. The system’s self-description does not.

Thus:

> **“Christian theology is the Graphē’s living voice” fails. The name is real. The name does not transfer the writing’s standing. Sound doctrine holds the word. It does not replace the word as the word’s official speaker.**

# 68.44 Competing Claim: Academic Status Settles the Sense

The reduction says:

> **Those who know letters, the wise, the scribes, the disputers, the schools, have settled what the writing can mean. An unlearned reading is a private opinion. Academic status is the court of sense.**

The reduction fails.

John 7:15–17 relocates doctrine in the Sender, against the marvel of letters without the school. Acts 4:13 records the council’s perception of unlearnedness and the knowledge that the men had been with Jesus. 1 Corinthians 1:20 asks for the wise, the scribe, and the disputer, and does not hand them the court. Acts 17 lets the Areopagus hear the resurrection and does not let the Areopagus decide it. 1 Timothy 6:20 warns against oppositions of falsely named knowledge. Jeremiah 8:8–9 places a claim of wisdom and of possessing the law beside rejection of the word.

Learning remains a real good. A person who has laboured in the languages can see what a careless reader misses. That good is service. Chapter 14 already gave scholarship its place as evidence and help, and refused it as authority. The present file uses that refusal at the level of the academy as a system. Status can make a sense impressive. Impressiveness is not correspondence. Falsely named knowledge is still falsely named when the name is a chair.

The reduction often presents itself as the only alternative to ignorance. The alternative to ignorance is attention to the writing, which learned and unlearned alike may give or refuse. Movement X will examine the labour. This chapter examines the claim that the labouring class, by being the class, has become the measure. The claim fails.

Thus:

> **“Academic status settles the sense” fails. Letters are a tool. The Sender’s doctrine, the scriptures searched, and the wisdom of God are the measure. A school is a secondary system, however high its status.**

# 68.45 Competing Claim: Antiquity of Tradition Is the Living Graphē

The reduction says:

> **What the elders and the fathers delivered is the living form of the word. The written text, taken without that delivery, is incomplete, dead, or dangerous. Age is continuity, and continuity is authority. To test the tradition by the writing is to prefer a private page to the church’s memory.**

The reduction fails.

παράδοσις has been distinguished. The traditions taught by apostolic word and epistle are to be held, and the epistle is writing. The tradition of the elders is the system Jesus judged as making the word of none effect. Galatians 1:14 and 1 Peter 1:18 refuse to let ancestral tradition function as the gospel or as the conversation of the redeemed. Jeremiah 6:16 commends the old paths where the good way is, which is a test, not a coronation of age. Matthew 5 distinguishes the ancient commandment from a heard addition.

Continuity of the deposit is a real good. Chapter 40’s transmission and Chapter 52’s recognition already honoured it. Continuity of a delivery that lays aside the commandment is the evil Mark 7 names. The reduction collapses the two by the one word “tradition,” and then treats the collapse as piety. The piety is the system’s self-protection. A tradition that sits under the Graphē can be old and good. A tradition that sits over the Graphē can be old and voiding. Age is present in both. Age decides neither.

The reduction often presents itself as the only alternative to amnesia. The alternative to amnesia is holding what was taught, which includes the epistle. Holding the epistle is not amnesia. Installing a later memory as the epistle’s judge is not memory of the deposit. It is a new primary.

Thus:

> **“Antiquity of tradition is the living Graphē” fails. Apostolic delivery, including the epistle, is to be held. The tradition of men is not that delivery. Age confers duration. It does not confer primary written authority.**

# 68.46 Competing Claim: The Institution That Recognized the Canon Is the Higher Court

The reduction says:

> **The church recognized the canon. Therefore the church is prior to the canon, and the church’s ongoing recognition is the higher court. The writing binds because the institution binds. Pillar and ground means author and judge.**

The reduction fails.

Chapter 52 already established that recognition is reception of writings that stand, not the creation of their standing. Chapter 57 already refused infallibility of the church as church. Chapter 65 already refused institution as the higher court. The present chapter uses those premises and meets the strongest textual form of the claim.

1 Timothy 3:15 calls the church pillar and ground of the truth inside a letter written to instruct behaviour in the house. The pillar upholds. The letter instructs the pillar. Acts 15 cites the prophets; it does not replace them. Galatians 2:6 records that those who seemed to be pillars added nothing. Acts 17:11 honours a community for searching the scriptures whether the teacher’s word was so. Acts 5:29 obeys God rather than the council. 3 John 9–10 records preeminence and expulsion without treating either as truth. Jeremiah 8:8–9 records a scribal institution claiming the law and rejecting the word.

The institution can receive, uphold, gather, and teach. Those are services. The inference from service to authorship is the transfer fallacy of Chapter 66, now worn by a house rather than by a reader. A house is a large reader with a seal. The seal is institutional recognition. The seal does not change the logic of the transfer. The writing was the measure by which recognition was noble, where it was noble. It remains the measure after the recognition.

The reduction often presents itself as humility before the body of Christ. Humility before Christ is obedience to His word. Humility before a body against His word is the scene Acts 4 and Acts 5 already judged. The body is honoured as pillar when it upholds the truth. It is not honoured as a second Christ when it claims the seat of the word.

Thus:

> **“The recognizing institution is the higher court” fails. Recognition is not creation. The pillar upholds the truth the writing instructs. Office may serve. Office does not become the Graphē.**

# 68.47 Competing Claim: Because Systems Are Secondary, Systems Are Forbidden

The reduction says:

> **Theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution are not the Graphē. Therefore they are illicit. Doctrine is pride. Reason is unbelief. Memory is corruption. The assembly is a rival. The only faithful act is a solitary reader with a closed system of no teachers, no creeds, and no churches.**

The reduction fails.

It is the despair fallacy of Chapter 66, moved from readings to orders. Chapter 66 refused the inference from “readers err” to “all readings are equal,” and equally refused the inference that would abolish stating sense. The present file refuses the inference from “systems are secondary” to “systems are crimes.”

2 Timothy 3:16 makes Scripture profitable for doctrine. Titus 1:9 requires an elder able by sound doctrine. Isaiah 1:18 and Acts 17 commend and exhibit reason. 2 Thessalonians 2:15 commands holding the traditions taught by word and epistle. 1 Timothy 3:15 honours the church as pillar and ground of the truth. Matthew 23:2 acknowledges a seat derivative of Moses. These are not embarrassments to the rank. They are the rank’s positive half. Secondary standing is the condition under which the service is clean. Forbidden standing would make the commands unintelligible.

The reduction often presents itself as purity. Purity that repeals the writings’ own provision for doctrine, reason, delivery, and house is not purity. It is a fifth system, unacknowledged: the system of the solitary absolutist, claiming primary standing for his refusal. His refusal is still a claim. It stands under the Graphē with the others. Where the writings command a service, the service is not rebellion. Where a service claims the master’s seat, the claim is judged. Both sentences belong to the conclusion.

Thus:

> **“Secondary means forbidden” fails. Doctrine, reason, apostolic delivery, and the house are commanded services. Their proper rank is under the Graphē. Abolishing them is not fidelity. It is a rival simplicity the writings do not teach.**

# 68.49 Reason Through the Evidence

The inference can now be exhibited.

**Premise 1.** The Graphē is the primary written authority by which truth is known, tested, reasoned through, taught, discerned, and lived. Tradition and institution are not a higher court over it (Chapter 65, used not retried).

**Premise 2.** The authority of that writing is distinct from the authority claimed for a later statement of sense. Transfer fails. Despair fails (Chapter 66, used not retried).

**Premise 3.** The record does not fail as truthful divine writing of what it actually communicates. That non-failure is not the infallibility of the church or of every later tradition (Chapter 57, used not retried).

**Premise 4.** Scripture is misread in five inspectable modes, and the writing remains the measure of the modes. A system’s size does not create a sixth mode. A system is a further object (Chapter 67, used not retried).

**Premise 5.** Human accounts about God are accountable; a theological system is not Scripture; a philosophical argument is not revelation; institutional recognition does not create divine authority; antiquity is not truth (Chapters 13–14, used not retried). Recognition of the corpus is not creation (Chapter 52, used not retried).

**Premise 6.** The writings themselves rank the four systems. Scripture is profitable for doctrine, and sound doctrine holds the faithful word (2 Timothy 3:16; Titus 1:9). Commandments of men can be taught as doctrines, and a party’s doctrine can be leaven (Matthew 15:9; 16:12). Falsely named knowledge is not the deposit (1 Timothy 6:20). A philosophy not after Christ spoils, while the treasures of wisdom are in Christ (Colossians 2:3–8). Among the schools, reason and a true clause do not make the schools the court of the resurrection (Acts 17). The wisdom of this world is not the wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1–2). Apostolic paradosis includes the epistle and is to be held (2 Thessalonians 2:15; 1 Corinthians 11:23). The tradition of the elders can make the word of none effect (Mark 7). Ancestral tradition is not the gospel and can be the vain conversation (Galatians 1:14; 1 Peter 1:18). The old paths are the good way, and a heard ancient saying may add what the commandment does not say (Jeremiah 6:16; Matthew 5). The church is pillar and ground of the truth in a letter that instructs the house (1 Timothy 3:15). Moses’ seat is derivative; a scribal pen can reject the word it claims (Matthew 23:2–3; Jeremiah 8:8–9). A gathering of apostles and elders serves under what is written (Acts 15). Pillars add nothing; Bereans search the scriptures (Galatians 2:6; Acts 17:11). A council and a preeminent man can command and expel against God and against the witness (Acts 4–5; 3 John 9–10). Letters are not the Sender; the Master is one (John 7:15–17; Matthew 23:8–10). The Christian name and religious works in the name are claims, not immunity (Acts 11:26; Matthew 7:21–23; 2 Timothy 3:5).

**Premise 7.** Reconstructible Introduction and Aletheia propositions, tested rather than cited as authority, cohere: notes are a secondary system under the passages; the church’s decision is not the creation of the canon; philosophical construction does not supply revelation and is not the condition of the writing’s speech; the Graphē is the measure for organizations as for persons; an organization ostensibly Christian and old remains under the writing. Council-origin, a ban on stating sense, and the later pathology-chain are not adopted.

**Premise 8.** Competing reductions fail: Christian theology as the writing’s living voice; academic status as the court of sense; antiquity of tradition as the living Graphē; the recognizing institution as the higher court; secondary standing as a ban on service; this file as already Chapter 69, Chapters 70–73, or Movement X.

**Inference.** Therefore theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution stand toward the Graphē as secondary systems. They may serve under the writing. None becomes the primary written authority merely by claiming Christian identity, academic status, antiquity, or institutional recognition. The writing remains the measure. A system that claims the writing’s standing has reversed a rank the writings themselves teach. The investigator is warranted, with full force, in receiving this as Movement IX’s third word.

The inference is not a leap from “human accounts exist” to “these four names.” The four names are the locked assignment’s systems, filled by the writings’ own distinctions and tested against the reconstructible source-propositions. The inference is not a leap from “the rank is real” to “displacement, heresy, and labour have been written.” Sequence forbids that leap.

Thus:

# 68.51 The Warranted Conclusion Stated with Full Force

The conclusion may now be stated without postponement and without inflation.

Theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution are secondary systems in relation to the Graphē.

**Theology** organizes teaching about God and about the writing. It serves when it holds the faithful word as sound doctrine, for which Scripture is profitable. It rivals when it teaches the commandments of men as doctrines. Its Christian name does not decide which act it has performed.

**Philosophy** organizes wisdom and inquiry. It serves when it examines under the writing and does not pretend to be revelation. It spoils when it is a system after the tradition of men and the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ, or when it offers the wisdom of this world as the court by which God is known. Its academic status does not make it the treasures of wisdom, which are hid in Christ.

**Tradition** hands down what has been received. It serves when it holds what the apostles taught by word and by epistle, the epistle being writing, and what was received of the Lord and delivered in the writing. It rivals when it is the tradition of men and of the elders, able to lay aside the commandment and make the word of none effect, or when it is the vain conversation of the fathers. Its antiquity does not decide which object it is.

**Institution** orders a house, a seat, or a body. It serves when it upholds the truth as pillar and ground, when it sits in a seat derivative of the writing, when it gathers under what is written, and when it searches the scriptures whether a teacher’s word is so. It rivals when it claims authorship of the canon because it recognized the canon, when it commands hearkening above God, when it treats expulsion or preeminence as truth, or when it installs a human master in the place of Christ. Its recognition does not decide the rank.

None of the four becomes the primary written authority merely by claiming Christian identity, academic status, antiquity, or institutional recognition. Those are properties and claims. The Graphē is the measure. A true sentence carried by a system is true as what the writing communicates, not as a promotion of the carrier. A false sentence is not healed by the carrier’s name, learning, age, or seal.

Service is a good. Supremacy is a reversal. The writings teach both halves. This continues Movement IX. It is not Chapter 67 retried. It is not Chapter 69. It is not Chapters 70–73. It is not Movement X. That is this chapter’s conclusion, stated here with full force.

Thus:

> **How theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution stand toward the Graphē has been examined as a rank. The rank is in force. Later questions remain later because they are different questions.**

# 68.52 Apply the Standard

The standard has been applied.

A later order that teaches, inquires, delivers, or rules in the neighbourhood of the Graphē is now to be asked not only, “By which mode, if any, does this statement fail of the writing?” — Chapter 67’s question — but, “What system is speaking, and what rank is it claiming?” Is it a theology holding the faithful word, or a theology teaching the commandments of men as doctrines? Is it a philosophy examining under Christ, or a philosophy after the tradition of men? Is it apostolic delivery, including the epistle, or the tradition of the elders? Is it a house upholding the truth, or a house claiming to be the truth’s author and judge?

The questions are practical without becoming a manual of church polity or a history of schools. A people who love the name of Christ may still treat a Christian system as the writing. A people who honour learning may still let the school settle the sense. A people who honour their fathers may still let age void a commandment. A people who honour the assembly may still let recognition function as creation. A people who fear those errors may still forbid the doctrine, the reason, the delivery, and the house the writings themselves commend.

The standard also protects. A true doctrinal sentence is not false because a system arranged it. A true observation is not false because a philosopher said it. An old path is not corrupt because it is old, where it is the good way. A church is not a rival because it is a pillar. Service remains service. The protection is the same fact as the judgment: the writing is the measure of both the service and the claim.

The standard is the Graphē. The systems are secondary. The four claims do not promote them. The rank is the join.

Thus:

> **The standard has been applied: theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution have been ranked toward the Graphē as Movement IX’s third word, not announced as a slogan, and not postponed as if the relation were weak.**

# 68.53 From Systems Beside the Graphē to Secondary Sources Becoming Primary

The system-file is in force. Theology, philosophy, tradition, and institution are secondary. They may serve. They do not become primary by identity, academy, antiquity, or recognition. If the rank were not stated, the next question would still be how the systems stand. The rank has been stated. If this chapter were already that later examination, the next file would be repetition.
