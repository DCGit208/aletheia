# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 22 — Graphē, Holy Writ, Scripture, Bible, and Written Word

> **Movement III — What Is the Bible?**

---

## Chapter Assignment

The preceding chapters have progressively identified the object under investigation.

Chapter 16 established the Bible as a **divine library and written record**.

Chapter 17 examined the Bible as **historical testimony**.

Chapter 18 examined its **eyewitness and apostolic testimony**.

Chapter 19 gathered the Bible's own claims concerning its **origin, divine communication, inspiration, prophetic speech, apostolic testimony, and Scripture**.

Chapter 20 introduced **γραφή (*graphē*)**, Strong's G1124.

Chapter 21 developed the relationship between **writing, Holy Writ, and Scripture**.

Chapter 22 now asks a necessary terminological question:

> **How do Graphē, Holy Writ, Scripture, the Scriptures, the Bible, and the Written Word relate to one another?**

The purpose is not to flatten these expressions into interchangeable synonyms.

Neither is it to manufacture distinctions where the biblical and historical evidence shows substantial overlap.

The purpose is precision.

Before the investigation proceeds to **how the written record was given**, the reader must know exactly what is meant by the written authority under investigation.

---

# Central Objective

> **To establish the precise relationship among Graphē, Holy Writ, Scripture, the Scriptures, the Bible, and the Written Word, distinguishing their lexical origins, biblical usage, historical development, and conceptual functions while establishing the authoritative written corpus that is the object of the investigation.**

The chapter therefore moves through four tasks:

1. **Define the principal terms.**
2. **Distinguish their respective functions.**
3. **Show where their meanings overlap.**
4. **Establish precisely what is meant by the biblical written authority being investigated.**

---

# 22.1 Why Terminology Matters

An investigation cannot remain precise if the object being investigated is allowed to change meaning from one paragraph to another.

Consider the following expressions:

- writing;
- Holy Writ;
- Scripture;
- the Scriptures;
- Bible;
- Word of God;
- Written Word;
- God's Word.

These expressions are closely related.

But they are not all formed from the same word, do not all have the same lexical history, and do not always have the same referent in every biblical context.

That does not weaken their relationship.

It clarifies it.

The governing principle is therefore:

> **Where terms overlap, establish the overlap. Where they differ, preserve the distinction.**

This prevents two opposite errors.

### Error 1 — Flattening

Treating every expression as though it were linguistically identical.

### Error 2 — Fragmenting

Treating closely related expressions as though they referred to unrelated realities.

The correct method is to determine the relationship from the evidence.

---

# 22.2 The First Distinction: Writing Is Broader Than Scripture

Chapter 20 established the basic lexical field of **γραφή (*graphē*)**.

Writing is a broad human activity.

People write:

- histories;
- letters;
- laws;
- records;
- poems;
- contracts;
- genealogies;
- reports;
- personal correspondence;
- inscriptions;
- administrative documents;
- and many other forms of communication.

Therefore:

> **Writing by itself does not mean Scripture.**

This is an elementary distinction, but it is foundational.

The Bible itself contains different kinds of written material.

The question is not:

> “Is it written?”

The more precise question is:

> **“What kind of writing is it, and what authority does it possess?”**

The biblical category of Scripture is therefore more specific than the generic category of writing.

---

# 22.3 Graphē — γραφή

The Greek noun:

> **γραφή (*graphē*)**

is derived from the verbal field associated with writing.

Its fundamental lexical sense concerns:

> **writing, a writing, or what has been written.**

In the New Testament, however, *graphē* acquires a highly significant specialized usage.

It is repeatedly used for:

> **Scripture / Holy Writ.**

This is why the word must be understood at two levels.

### Lexical level

> **γραφή — writing / what is written.**

### Biblical usage

> **γραφή — Scripture, the sacred written text.**

The second meaning is not an arbitrary invention.

It arises from the repeated way the New Testament uses the word in its Scriptural context.

---

# 22.4 Singular and Plural Scripture

The New Testament uses both singular and plural forms.

A singular expression can identify:

> **a particular Scripture or written passage.**

A plural expression can identify:

> **the Scriptures as a body of sacred writings.**

This distinction is visible in passages such as Matthew 21:42 and Mark 12:10.

Jesus can ask:

> **“Have ye not read in the scriptures...?”**

while a particular passage can be identified as:

> **“this scripture.”**

Therefore the word *graphē* can function both for:

- a particular Scriptural text;
- and the broader Scriptural corpus.

The context determines the scope.

This is why it is inaccurate to say that *graphē* mechanically means “the complete Bible” every time it appears.

Its biblical usage is richer and more precise.

---

# 22.5 Holy Writ — ἱερὰ γράμματα

2 Timothy 3:15 provides another important designation:

> **ἱερὰ γράμματα (*hiera grammata*)**

commonly rendered:

> **“holy scriptures,”**
>
> **“sacred writings,”**
>
> or
>
> **“holy writings.”**

The expression contains two significant elements.

### ἱερά (*hiera*)

This identifies the writings as:

> **holy / sacred.**

### γράμματα (*grammata*)

This belongs to the field of:

> **letters, writings, written material.**

The combined expression therefore identifies:

> **sacred or holy writings.**

Paul then immediately speaks of:

> **πᾶσα γραφή (*pasa graphē*)**

in verse 16.

The literary connection is significant.

The passage therefore gives us two closely related designations:

> **holy writings**

and:

> **Scripture.**

The distinction in terminology should be preserved while the close relationship should also be recognized.

---

# 22.6 Holy Writ Is a Category of Sacred Writing

The adjective **holy** matters.

The Bible does not simply identify a collection of old writings.

It identifies sacred writings as belonging to a distinct category.

This means that the biblical question is not merely:

> “Is this document ancient?”

Nor:

> “Was this document written by a religious person?”

Nor:

> “Has this document been preserved?”

The more fundamental question is:

> **Does this writing belong to the category of Holy Writ?**

That question leads naturally into the biblical concept of Scripture.

---

# 22.7 Scripture — The English Designation

The English word **Scripture** corresponds to the biblical concept expressed principally by the Greek **γραφή** in New Testament usage.

The English term therefore carries the idea of:

> **sacred writing / Holy Writ.**

This is why expressions such as:

> **“the Scripture”**

and:

> **“the Scriptures”**

refer not simply to any written document but to the sacred written testimony recognized in the biblical record.

The word “Scripture” is therefore a designation of **status and function**, not merely physical form.

A document can be physically written without being Scripture.

Scripture is written testimony possessing a sacred and authoritative identity.

---

# 22.8 “The Scriptures”

The plural expression:

> **“the Scriptures”**

is particularly important.

Jesus and the apostles repeatedly appeal to:

> **“the Scriptures.”**

Examples include:

- Matthew 21:42;
- Matthew 22:29;
- Mark 12:24;
- Luke 24:27;
- John 5:39;
- Acts 17:11;
- Romans 15:4;
- 2 Peter 3:16.

The expression therefore functions as a recognized designation for a body of sacred writings.

This is important for understanding the Bible as a corpus.

The Bible is not presented as a single human composition written at one time.

It is a collection of writings that came through different authors, periods, circumstances, and literary forms.

The term **“the Scriptures”** naturally accommodates that plurality.

---

# 22.9 “It Is Written”

One of the most important Scriptural formulas is:

> **“It is written.”**

Jesus repeatedly uses this expression.

Examples include Matthew 4:4, 7, and 10.

The formula does something more than identify the existence of writing.

It appeals to the written text as an authoritative witness.

The reasoning is:

> **What has been written in Scripture carries authority in the present argument.**

This is why Jesus can answer temptation by appealing to what is written.

The written text is not merely an historical artifact.

It remains authoritative testimony.

---

# 22.10 Scripture as an Authoritative Written Witness

The cumulative biblical pattern is therefore clear.

Scripture is:

- read;
- searched;
- quoted;
- interpreted;
- fulfilled;
- appealed to;
- reasoned from;
- and treated as authoritative.

The Bereans examine Paul's preaching in relation to the Scriptures:

> **“whether those things were so.”**

Jesus argues from Scripture.

The apostles argue from Scripture.

Prophetic fulfillment is explained from Scripture.

The written text therefore functions as a continuing witness.

This is one of the most important characteristics of the written record.

---

# 22.11 The Bible — What Does the Name Mean?

We now come to the word:

> **Bible.**

The English word **Bible** is not the original biblical name for the collection.

It comes through Latin and related Western-language development from the Greek:

> **βιβλία (*biblia*)**

meaning:

> **“books.”**

The underlying Greek vocabulary is associated with **βιβλίον (*biblion*)**, a book, scroll, or written document, and **βίβλος (*biblos*)**, a term associated historically with writing material and books.

The essential point is simple:

> **Bible originally carries the idea of “books.”**

This is fitting because the Bible is a collection of writings rather than a single continuous composition.

---

# 22.12 The Historical Development of “Bible”

The historical development of the designation is especially illuminating.

The Greek expression:

> **τὰ βιβλία (*ta biblia*)**

means:

> **“the books.”**

The expression occurs in the Greek Old Testament tradition, including Daniel 9:2, where Daniel speaks of the books in connection with the prophetic writings.

The designation subsequently became associated with the sacred books of the Christian Scriptures.

The historical development then moved through several stages.

### Stage 1 — Books

The sacred writings were understood as:

> **the books.**

### Stage 2 — The Sacred Collection

Christian usage increasingly applied the designation to the corpus of sacred writings.

### Stage 3 — The Divine Library

Jerome, in the fourth century, used the significant designation:

> **Bibliotheca Divina**

or:

> **“The Divine Library.”**

That expression captures something important.

The Bible is simultaneously:

> **a unified corpus**

and:

> **a library of individual writings.**

### Stage 4 — From “Books” to “Book”

Over time, the plural designation came to be treated in Western usage as a singular collective designation.

Thus:

> **The Books**

became:

> **The Book**

and ultimately:

> **the Bible.**

This historical development helps explain why the modern word can refer to the whole collection as one entity.

---

# 22.13 The Bible Is Both “The Book” and “The Books”

This development does not erase the plurality of the biblical writings.

It actually gives us a useful conceptual distinction.

The Bible is:

> **the Book**

as a unified corpus.

And it is:

> **the Books**

as a collection of individual writings.

This is not a contradiction.

A library can be considered:

- collectively;
- and book by book.

Likewise, the Bible can be examined as:

- one corpus;
- or its individual books.

The historical designation therefore reflects a genuine feature of the object.

---

# 22.14 Bible as Corpus

The Bible's corpus character is evident from its structure.

In the standard Protestant Christian arrangement, the Bible contains:

> **66 books**

comprising:

- **39 books of the Old Testament**;
- **27 books of the New Testament**.

These writings were produced across different historical periods and literary settings.

They were written through different human agents and in different linguistic environments.

The Old Testament is principally associated with Hebrew, with portions in Aramaic.

The New Testament is written in Koine Greek.

Thus the Bible is not one manuscript in the ordinary sense.

It is a corpus.

That is why the historical development from:

> **“books”**

to:

> **“the Book”**

is conceptually significant.

The unity of the corpus does not erase the plurality of its constituent writings.

---

# 22.15 The Bible and Its Original Languages

The terminology must also be distinguished from the languages in which the writings were originally composed.

The biblical corpus comes to us through:

- Hebrew;
- Aramaic;
- and Greek.

This means that the English word:

> **Bible**

is not itself the original-language name found throughout the biblical texts.

It is a later historical designation for the collection.

That distinction is important because it prevents the modern English title from being projected backward as though it were the original name used by every biblical writer.

---

# 22.16 Bible, Scripture, and Holy Writ Are Related but Not Identical Terms

We can now state the relationship more precisely.

### Graphē

Emphasizes:

> **writing / what is written**

and in New Testament usage frequently:

> **Scripture.**

### Holy Writ

Emphasizes:

> **the sacred character of the writings.**

### Scripture

Emphasizes:

> **the authoritative sacred written testimony.**

### The Scriptures

Emphasizes:

> **the recognized body of sacred writings.**

### Bible

Historically emphasizes:

> **the collection of books as a corpus.**

These are not competing definitions.

They are different linguistic windows onto closely related aspects of the same written corpus.

---

# 22.17 The “Written Word”

The expression:

> **“Written Word”**

is a useful descriptive designation for God's Word in written form.

It should not be confused with the lexical meaning of *graphē* itself.

Nor should it be confused with every biblical use of:

> **λόγος (*logos*)**

or:

> **Word of God.**

The phrase “Written Word” emphasizes the form in which divine communication has been recorded and preserved:

> **written.**

Thus:

> **God's Word**
>
> ↓
>
> **communicated**
>
> ↓
>
> **received and witnessed**
>
> ↓
>
> **written**
>
> ↓
>
> **preserved as Scripture**

This relationship will become central in Movement IV.

---

# 22.18 “The Word of God” Is Broader Than One Technical Term

The expression:

> **“Word of God”**

must be handled with contextual precision.

In Scripture, God's Word can refer to:

- God's command;
- God's spoken declaration;
- prophetic proclamation;
- divine revelation;
- apostolic proclamation;
- Christ as the Word;
- and God's revealed message in written Scripture.

Therefore the expression should not be reduced mechanically to:

> **“the Bible.”**

At the same time, this distinction does not weaken the relationship between Scripture and God's Word.

The written Scriptures are presented as God's authoritative written testimony.

The correct conclusion is therefore:

> **“Word of God” is a broader biblical category whose particular referent is determined by context, while Scripture is the authoritative written category within that broader field.**

---

# 22.19 Christ as the Word and Scripture as Written Testimony

John 1:1 identifies Christ as:

> **ὁ λόγος — the Word.**

John 1:14 declares:

> **“the Word was made flesh.”**

This is fundamentally different from saying:

> “the Bible became flesh.”

Christ is personally the Word.

Scripture is written testimony.

The distinction must remain absolute.

Yet the relationship is profound.

God communicates Himself and His truth through multiple forms of divine communication.

The Bible records that communication.

Therefore the investigation must distinguish:

> **the divine Person**

from:

> **the divine message**

from:

> **the written testimony preserving that message.**

This distinction prevents confusion while allowing the full unity of divine revelation to be seen.

---

# 22.20 Scripture and the Written Record

The written record is therefore not identical with every event or every spoken word that occurred in biblical history.

It is the preserved written testimony.

For example:

> a prophet may receive a divine message;

> the prophet may proclaim it;

> the message may be witnessed;

> the message may be written;

> the written text may then be preserved;

> and the resulting writing may function as Scripture.

The written record is therefore a historically mediated form of divine communication.

That fact does not make it less significant.

It explains how revelation becomes accessible across generations.

---

# 22.21 Scripture Can Outlive Its First Audience

A spoken message is immediately available to those who hear it.

A written testimony can remain available long after the original audience has disappeared.

This is one reason the written record is so important.

Moses can be read by generations who never met Moses.

David's writings can be read by generations who never lived in David's court.

The prophets can be read by people centuries after their ministries.

The apostles can be read by people who were never present in Jerusalem, Galilee, Corinth, Ephesus, or Patmos.

The writing carries the testimony across time.

Thus:

> **The written form of Scripture makes the testimony historically transmissible across generations.**

---

# 22.22 Scripture, Manuscript, and Copy Must Be Distinguished

Another distinction is essential.

A **manuscript** is a physical written witness.

A **copy** is a reproduction of an earlier textual witness.

A **translation** renders the text into another language.

An **edition** presents a textual form according to editorial decisions.

A **Scriptural writing** is the authoritative sacred text belonging to the Scriptural corpus.

These categories are related but not identical.

This distinction will later become essential when the investigation turns to:

- manuscript evidence;
- textual transmission;
- copying;
- textual variants;
- preservation;
- and the history of the biblical text.

For now, the conclusion is sufficient:

> **The physical artifact carrying Scripture must not be confused with Scripture as the written textual authority.**

---

# 22.23 Scripture and Commentary Must Be Distinguished

The same principle applies to interpretation.

There is a fundamental difference between:

> **Scripture**

and:

> **a commentary on Scripture.**

There is a difference between:

> **the biblical text**

and:

> **a theological explanation of the biblical text.**

There is a difference between:

> **what the text says**

and:

> **what an interpreter concludes that it means.**

This distinction is necessary because the Doctrine of the Graphē is concerned first with the primary written testimony.

Secondary interpretation may be useful.

But it cannot silently replace the primary source.

The governing methodological rule remains:

> **What Scripture says must be distinguished from what someone says Scripture says.**

---

# 22.24 The Bible as a Library

The designation:

> **“The Divine Library”**

is therefore conceptually valuable.

A library contains:

- many books;
- different authors;
- different genres;
- different historical settings;
- and different subjects.

Yet the collection can still be treated as one library.

Likewise, the Bible contains diverse writings while forming one recognized corpus.

This is why:

> **diversity of authorship**

does not mean:

> **absence of corpus unity.**

And:

> **unity of corpus**

does not mean:

> **one human author.**

The distinction is fundamental to understanding the Bible as a written object.

---

# 22.25 The Bible's Unity Does Not Erase Its Diversity

The biblical corpus includes:

- law;
- narrative;
- poetry;
- wisdom;
- prophecy;
- Gospel narrative;
- apostolic history;
- letters;
- and apocalyptic literature.

The diversity is real.

The historical periods are real.

The human authors are real.

The languages are real.

The literary forms are real.

The individual books remain identifiable.

Yet these writings are received as one Scriptural corpus.

Therefore:

> **The Bible's unity must be understood as corpus unity, not as the denial of its literary and historical diversity.**

This observation will become increasingly important when later chapters examine inspiration and the means by which God gave the written record.

---

# 22.26 The Terminological Map

The relationship can now be summarized:

| Designation | Primary emphasis |
|---|---|
| **γραφή (*graphē*)** | Writing / what is written; frequently Scripture in New Testament usage |
| **ἱερὰ γράμματα (*hiera grammata*)** | Holy or sacred writings |
| **Scripture** | Sacred and authoritative written testimony |
| **The Scriptures** | The recognized body of sacred writings |
| **Bible** | The historical designation of the collected biblical books |
| **Written Word** | God's Word considered in its written form |
| **Word of God** | A broader biblical expression whose referent is determined by context |

This table should be read as a relationship map, not as a list of unrelated definitions.

---

# 22.27 What These Terms Have in Common

Although their emphases differ, the terms converge on the object now under investigation.

The Bible is the corpus.

The Scriptures are the sacred writings within that corpus.

Holy Writ emphasizes their sacred character.

Graphē identifies writing and, in biblical usage, Scripture.

Written Word emphasizes God's Word in written form.

Thus the investigation has arrived at a precise object:

> **the sacred and authoritative written corpus identified as Scripture and historically designated as the Bible.**

---

# 22.28 What These Terms Must Not Be Made to Mean

Precision also requires limits.

We should not say:

> **Every writing is Scripture.**

We should not say:

> **Every occurrence of γραφή means the entire Bible.**

We should not say:

> **Every occurrence of λόγος means the Bible.**

We should not say:

> **The physical manuscript is identical with the Scriptural authority it carries.**

We should not say:

> **A commentary is Scripture because it explains Scripture.**

We should not say:

> **The English word “Bible” was the original name used by all biblical writers.**

Each statement would confuse distinct categories.

---

# 22.29 Why “Bible” Is a Particularly Useful Name

The historical name “Bible” has an important conceptual strength.

Its original plural sense:

> **“books”**

reminds us that the Bible is not a single document produced in one sitting.

It is a collection.

It contains individual books.

Those books possess their own historical identities.

Yet they belong to a unified corpus.

The later singular use:

> **“the Book”**

emphasizes the unity of the collection.

Therefore the history of the name itself reflects an important feature of the object:

> **many writings, one recognized corpus.**

---

# 22.30 The Written Authority Being Investigated

We can now state the object of the investigation with precision.

The object is not:

- paper;
- ink;
- a particular printed edition;
- a modern translation;
- a commentary;
- a theological system;
- or a later author's explanation.

The object is:

> **the corpus of sacred writings identified as Scripture and historically collected and designated as the Bible.**

Its material witnesses are manuscripts.

Its individual components are books.

Its biblical designation is Scripture.

Its sacred character is expressed as Holy Writ.

Its written form may properly be described as the Written Word.

And its historical collective designation is:

> **the Bible.**

---

# 22.32 The Bible Is a Collection — Not a Random Collection

The fact that the Bible consists of many books must not be misunderstood.

It is not simply a random shelf of ancient religious literature.

The writings belong to a recognized Scriptural corpus.

They have been transmitted as a body.

They are repeatedly treated within the biblical record as sacred written testimony.

The later historical questions concerning recognition, canon, transmission, and preservation are therefore not questions about whether unrelated books happened to be placed together.

They are questions concerning:

> **which writings belong to the Scriptural corpus and how that corpus was recognized and transmitted.**

Those questions have their proper place later in the investigation.

---

# 22.33 The Terminology Prepares the Next Question

The chapter has now answered:

> **What do we mean by the written authority?**

But another question immediately follows.

If Scripture is written testimony:

> **How did the written testimony come into existence?**

If the Bible is a corpus of sacred writings:

> **How were those writings given?**

If the Written Word is divine communication in written form:

> **How did divine communication become written testimony?**

That is not a question of terminology.

It is a question of origin and transmission.

And it is precisely the question assigned to the next movement.

# 22.35 Chapter Conclusion

The terminology has now been placed in its proper relationship.

> **Graphē** identifies writing and, in its New Testament Scriptural usage, Scripture.

> **Holy Writ / holy writings** identifies sacred written material.

> **Scripture** identifies the authoritative sacred written testimony.

> **The Scriptures** identifies the recognized body of such writings.

> **Bible** is the historical designation that developed from the concept of “the books” and came to designate the collected biblical corpus.

> **Written Word** describes God's Word in its written form.

These expressions overlap because they converge upon the same Scriptural corpus, but they are not linguistically or conceptually identical.

The central conclusion is therefore:

> **The Bible is the collected corpus of sacred writings identified as Scripture; Graphē is a principal biblical designation for Scripture; Holy Writ emphasizes the sacred character of the writings; and “Written Word” describes God's Word in its written form. Together, these designations identify the written authority whose origin, transmission, authentication, and preservation the following movements will investigate.**

The terminological question has therefore been settled sufficiently to move forward.

The next question is no longer:

> **What are we talking about?**

It is:

> **How did God give the written record?**

# Source-Use Guardrail

The chapter follows the governing Source Integration and Evidence Protocol:

> **The TOC determines where an argument belongs; source material determines how accurately, completely, and evidentially that argument can be developed.**

Accordingly:

- biblical texts remain the primary textual evidence;
- lexical claims are kept within the lexical evidence;
- historical naming claims are treated as historical claims;
- theological conclusions are drawn only after the relevant evidence has been established;
- and later questions concerning canon, inspiration, authentication, textual preservation, and transmission are not prematurely absorbed into this chapter.

The chapter establishes the object before the investigation turns to the manner in which that object came into existence.

# Chapter's Central Conclusion

> **The Bible is the collected corpus of sacred writings identified as Scripture. Graphē is a principal New Testament designation for Scripture; Holy Writ identifies the sacred character of the writings; the Scriptures identifies the body of those writings; Bible is the historical designation that developed from “the books”; and Written Word describes God's Word in its written form. These designations converge upon the authoritative written corpus that is now ready to be investigated according to the question: How was the written record given?**
