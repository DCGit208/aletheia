# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 20 — What Is γραφή (*graphē*)?

> **Movement III — What Is the Bible?**

---

## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 19 established the Bible's own claims concerning divine communication, human witnesses and authors, Scripture, and divine origin.

Chapter 20 now takes the next necessary step:

> **What exactly does the Bible mean when it speaks of γραφή (*graphē*)?**

The question is deliberately precise.

The English words **Bible**, **Scripture**, **Holy Writ**, and **written Word** are familiar terms, but the investigation cannot simply assume that these English designations are interchangeable with every occurrence of a particular biblical word.

The biblical term **γραφή (*graphē*)**, Strong's G1124, must therefore be examined on its own terms.

The chapter will establish its lexical field, its relationship to the verb **γράφω (*graphō*)**, its usage within the biblical text, and the distinctive manner in which it comes to designate Scripture or Holy Writ.

This is not yet the complete doctrine of inspiration.

It is not yet the investigation of canon.

It is not yet the full question of how divine revelation became written testimony.

Those questions belong to later chapters.

The immediate question is simpler and more foundational:

> **What is γραφή (*graphē*)?**

---

## Central Objective

> **To establish from lexical evidence and biblical usage what γραφή (*graphē*) means, how the term functions in the biblical text, and why it becomes a principal biblical designation for the written Scripture under investigation.**

---

# 20.1 Why Begin With the Word?

The investigation has now reached a point where terminology matters.

Chapter 15 asked what the Bible is.

Chapter 16 examined it as a divine library and written record.

Chapter 17 examined its historical character.

Chapter 18 examined eyewitness and apostolic testimony.

Chapter 19 examined the Bible's own claims concerning its divine origin and its human historical mediation.

The investigation therefore now arrives at the written object itself.

Before asking:

- How was the written record given?
- What is inspiration?
- What does “all Scripture” mean?
- How was the canonical corpus recognized?
- What authority does Scripture possess?

we must first establish what the biblical writers mean when they refer to **γραφή**.

This is not a merely academic exercise.

A conclusion built upon an undefined term is unstable.

If a biblical word is translated as “Scripture,” the reader must know whether the underlying term itself always means “Scripture,” whether it has a broader lexical field, and how the context determines its particular sense.

Therefore the method is:

> **Lexical meaning first.  
> Biblical usage second.  
> Theological inference third.**

The order matters.

---

# 20.2 The Greek Form: γραφή

The Greek noun under investigation is:

> **γραφή**

Transliterated:

> ***graphē***

Strong's:

> **G1124**

It is a feminine noun, and it is related to the verbal family represented by:

> **γράφω (*graphō*)**

The lexical relationship is significant.

The verb **γράφω** has the basic sense of writing, with the broader historical Greek usage also extending into related senses such as scratching, drawing, or inscribing. The noun **γραφή** is correspondingly associated with writing or something written. The LSJ lexical record gives *graphē* the basic senses “drawing, writing,” while Thayer's entry for G1124 begins with “a writing, thing written.”

The first conclusion is therefore straightforward:

> **γραφή is fundamentally a noun belonging to the lexical field of writing and what is written.**

That is the starting point.

It is important not to begin with the later theological designation and then work backward.

We should not reason:

> γραφή means “Scripture”  
> therefore γραφή must mean “the Bible” in every occurrence.

That would reverse the proper lexical method.

The word must first be understood in its lexical and contextual range.

---

# 20.3 From γράφω to γραφή

The relationship between **γράφω** and **γραφή** helps explain why the noun naturally belongs to the domain of writing.

**γράφω** concerns the act or result of writing.

**γραφή** is the corresponding noun that can refer to writing or what has been written.

This relationship should not be overstated. A cognate relationship does not by itself determine every contextual meaning.

Nevertheless, it establishes the appropriate semantic starting point.

The word is fundamentally concerned with something **written**.

This is why Strong's definition identifies G1124 as:

> “a document, i.e. holy Writ (or its contents or a statement in it)”

while its outline of biblical usage distinguishes:

1. a writing or thing written;
2. Scripture, referring to the book itself or its contents;
3. a particular portion or section of Holy Scripture.

The lexical evidence therefore gives us a range rather than a single English gloss.

---

# 20.4 A Necessary Lexical Guardrail

The manuscript's Source Integration & Evidence Protocol establishes an important rule:

> **γραφή (*graphē*, G1124) must not be reduced to “the Bible.” Its basic lexical field concerns writing / a written thing, with biblical usage extending to Holy Writ / Scripture.**

That distinction must remain throughout this chapter.

There are therefore at least three levels that must be kept separate:

### 1. Lexical level

**γραφή** belongs to the semantic field of writing and what is written.

### 2. Contextual level

A particular occurrence may refer to a writing, a written statement, a passage, or Scripture.

### 3. Biblical-theological level

In many New Testament contexts, **γραφή** functions as the designation of authoritative Scripture.

The third conclusion is established from the biblical usage of the term, not merely from its dictionary entry.

---

# 20.5 The Bible's Use of γραφή

The New Testament provides the decisive field in which this investigation must take place.

The word does not remain merely a generic noun for anything ever written.

It is repeatedly used in contexts where a written text carries recognized authority.

The biblical writers speak of:

> **“the scripture”**

and:

> **“the scriptures.”**

They appeal to them.

They interpret them.

They argue from them.

They describe their fulfillment.

They identify them with prophetic testimony.

They place them within God's purposes.

They describe them as something that can be known, understood, fulfilled, searched, and believed.

That usage is what must now be examined.

---

# 20.6 “The Scripture” — ἡ γραφή

One of the most important forms is:

> **ἡ γραφή**  
> *hē graphē*  
> **“the Scripture”**

The expression is especially significant because the term is no longer functioning merely as an abstract reference to the act of writing.

In its biblical contexts, it can designate an authoritative written text.

Thayer's lexical entry identifies **ἡ γραφή** as “the Scripture” and notes its use for the holy Scripture, including references where the term can denote the book itself, its contents, or a particular section of Holy Scripture.

This does not mean that every occurrence of **γραφή** has precisely the same scope.

The context remains decisive.

But it does establish something important:

> **The biblical writers use γραφή as a recognized designation for authoritative sacred writing.**

That is the point that must be established here.

---

# 20.7 γραφή as “What Is Written”

A useful example is found in passages where Scripture is cited as a written statement.

Jesus repeatedly appeals to what has been written.

For example, in Matthew 4, during the temptation in the wilderness, Jesus answers the tempter by appealing to written Scripture:

> **“It is written”**  
> (Matthew 4:4, 7, 10).

The significance is not simply that Jesus remembers something that was once written.

The written text functions as authoritative testimony.

The same pattern occurs throughout His ministry.

When confronted with questions concerning marriage, resurrection, the commandments, or the identity of the Messiah, Jesus repeatedly appeals to what is written.

The written record is therefore not treated as inert historical material.

It functions as authoritative testimony.

---

# 20.8 Jesus and Scripture

The Gospel accounts provide particularly important evidence because Jesus Himself repeatedly treats Scripture as authoritative.

Consider the pattern:

- He answers temptation by Scripture (Matthew 4:4, 7, 10).
- He appeals to what was written concerning human marriage and creation (Matthew 19:4–5).
- He appeals to Scripture in discussion with religious opponents (Matthew 21:42; 22:31–32).
- He identifies Scripture as something that cannot be broken (John 10:35).
- He appeals to Scripture concerning His death and resurrection (Luke 24:44–46).
- He explains the things concerning Himself from Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms (Luke 24:27, 44).

The significance of these passages is cumulative.

Jesus does not treat the written Scriptures merely as ancient religious literature.

He treats them as authoritative written testimony.

---

# 20.9 “The Scripture Cannot Be Broken”

John 10:35 contains one of the most significant statements concerning Scripture:

> **“the scripture cannot be broken.”**

This statement must not be isolated from its context.

Jesus is responding to an accusation concerning His claim to be the Son of God.

He cites Psalm 82:6:

> **“I said, Ye are gods.”**

His argument depends upon the wording and authority of the written text.

The force of the argument is therefore connected to the status of Scripture itself.

The Source Integration & Evidence Protocol specifically requires John 10:34–36 to be handled in context rather than reduced to the isolated phrase “the scripture cannot be broken.”

The conclusion relevant to this chapter is:

> **Jesus treats the written Scripture as an authoritative textual witness whose wording carries decisive force in argument.**

The full theological implications of that authority belong to later chapters.

But the usage of **γραφή** is already becoming clear.

---

# 20.10 γραφή and Fulfillment

Another major biblical use of **γραφή** concerns fulfillment.

The New Testament repeatedly describes historical events as occurring:

> **“that the scripture might be fulfilled.”**

This formula occurs throughout the Gospel narratives.

The importance of this usage is substantial.

A written text is being treated as containing an authoritative testimony concerning events that are subsequently fulfilled in history.

Thus Scripture is not merely:

> something written in the past.

It functions as:

> **an authoritative written witness whose contents stand in relation to God's purposes and their historical fulfillment.**

This is especially evident in the Passion narratives and in the resurrection accounts.

Jesus Himself interprets His suffering, death, and resurrection in relation to the written Scriptures.

Luke 24:44–46 is particularly important:

Christ identifies what was written concerning Him in:

- the Law of Moses;
- the Prophets;
- and the Psalms.

The written record therefore functions as a coherent witness to God's purposes across the biblical history.

---

# 20.11 γραφή as Prophetic Written Testimony

The relationship between Scripture and prophecy is also important.

Romans 16:26 speaks of:

> **“the scriptures of the prophets”**

and Matthew 26:56 speaks of:

> **“the scriptures of the prophets.”**

This establishes an important connection.

The written Scripture can preserve prophetic testimony.

Thus we must distinguish:

> **prophecy as divine communication**

from:

> **the written prophetic testimony preserved as Scripture.**

That distinction anticipates Movement IV.

Chapter 23 and following will investigate the various ways God communicated.

Chapter 20 is concerned with the written category into which such communication may enter.

---

# 20.12 Scripture as Something That Speaks

The biblical writers sometimes speak of Scripture in a striking manner.

Paul writes:

> **“the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the heathen through faith, preached before the gospel unto Abraham”**

(Galatians 3:8).

The text is striking because Scripture is presented as speaking or acting as an agent of testimony.

Romans 9:17 similarly introduces the divine message concerning Pharaoh through the language of Scripture:

> **“For the scripture saith unto Pharaoh...”**

The grammatical construction is significant.

The written Scripture is treated as the authoritative bearer of the divine message.

This does not mean that paper, parchment, ink, or letters possess personal consciousness.

Rather, the written text is treated as the enduring authoritative form through which the message speaks.

The Scripture remains present as testimony.

Thus:

> **What God has caused to be written continues to function as a speaking witness to the reader.**

That is an important feature of biblical *graphē*.

---

# 20.13 Scripture as Something to Be Known and Understood

The biblical writers also treat Scripture as something that can be:

- known;
- searched;
- understood;
- read;
- interpreted;
- fulfilled;
- and believed.

Jesus says:

> **“Ye do err, not knowing the scriptures”**

(Matthew 22:29).

In John 5:39:

> **“Search the scriptures.”**

Luke 24:45 describes Jesus opening the disciples' understanding so that they could understand the Scriptures.

Acts repeatedly describes people reasoning from the Scriptures.

The Bereans are commended because they:

> **“searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so”**

(Acts 17:11).

The written text is therefore not presented as inaccessible sacred material.

It is an object of rational examination.

This is directly relevant to the methodology of the present work.

The biblical model itself treats Scripture as something that can be **read, examined, compared with claims, understood, and used in reasoning**.

---

# 20.14 γραφή and Apostolic Proclamation

The apostles likewise appeal to Scripture as the written foundation of their proclamation.

Paul summarizes the Gospel in 1 Corinthians 15:

> **“how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures;  
> And that he was buried, and that he rose again the third day according to the scriptures.”**

The phrase:

> **“according to the scriptures”**

is significant.

The apostolic proclamation of historical events is connected to the written biblical testimony.

The written Scripture therefore serves both as:

1. a record of God's prior revelation; and
2. an authoritative framework within which subsequent historical events are understood.

This connects the themes already established in Chapters 17–19:

> **historical event → witness → proclamation → written testimony → Scripture.**

But the full process by which divine communication reaches written form belongs to Movement IV.

---

# 20.15 γραφή and 2 Timothy 3:15–16

Two passages are especially important for understanding the relationship between Scripture and the written record:

> **2 Timothy 3:15–16**

Paul tells Timothy that from childhood he has known:

> **“the holy scriptures”**

and immediately continues:

> **“All scripture is given by inspiration of God...”**

The terminology is significant.

Verse 15 uses:

> **ἱερὰ γράμματα**  
> *hiera grammata*  
> **“holy writings”**

while verse 16 uses:

> **πᾶσα γραφή**  
> *pasa graphē*  
> **“all Scripture.”**

The passage therefore brings together the concepts of:

- writing;
- holiness;
- Scripture;
- and divine origin.

This chapter should not yet exhaust Paul's doctrine of inspiration.

Chapter 30 is specifically assigned to the detailed investigation of:

> **Inspiration — 2 Timothy 3:15–17.**

Chapter 31 is then specifically assigned to the scope of:

> **“All Scripture.”**

The present chapter therefore establishes the lexical and conceptual importance of *graphē* while leaving those later questions to their designated chapters.

---

# 20.16 γραφή and 2 Peter 1:20–21

Peter likewise uses the language of Scripture and prophecy in a context concerned with divine origin.

2 Peter 1:20–21 states that prophecy did not come by the will of man, but that holy men spoke as they were moved by the Holy Spirit.

This passage belongs centrally to the later examination of prophetic revelation and inspiration.

Its relevance here is narrower.

It demonstrates that the biblical category of Scripture cannot be separated from the question of how divine communication was received and transmitted.

Thus the conceptual sequence is becoming visible:

> **divine communication → human reception → prophetic or apostolic testimony → writing → Scripture.**

The later chapters will investigate each stage more fully.

---

# 20.17 γραφή Is Not “The Bible” in Every Occurrence

This distinction is essential.

The Bible contains occurrences of **γραφή** where the term can refer to:

- a written statement;
- a particular passage;
- Scripture generally;
- or the sacred writings.

The lexical evidence itself recognizes this range. Thayer's entry distinguishes “a writing, thing written,” “the Scripture,” and “a certain portion or section of holy Scripture.”

Therefore:

> **γραφή should not be mechanically translated “the Bible” every time it occurs.**

That would impose a later English conceptual category onto every Greek occurrence.

Instead, the reader must examine:

1. the form of the word;
2. the immediate context;
3. the surrounding argument;
4. whether a particular passage is intended;
5. whether Scripture as a recognized corpus is intended;
6. and how the term functions in the larger biblical argument.

This is lexical discipline.

---

# 20.18 Singular and Plural Usage

The New Testament uses both singular and plural forms.

### Singular

> **ἡ γραφή — the Scripture**

can identify Scripture or a particular written passage depending upon context.

### Plural

> **αἱ γραφαί — the Scriptures**

can refer to the collection of sacred writings or multiple passages.

The distinction is important because the biblical writers can move between:

> **a particular written passage**

and:

> **the recognized body of sacred writings.**

The word therefore operates at more than one level of reference.

The context determines the scope.

This also helps explain why the New Testament can cite one specific passage while simultaneously referring to the larger body of Scripture.

---

# 20.19 The Written Text and Its Contents

Another important feature of *graphē* is that the term can refer not only to the physical written object but also to what is contained in that writing.

This is reflected in lexical descriptions that allow *graphē* to denote either the book itself or its contents.

This distinction matters because Scripture's authority is not located merely in physical materials.

The biblical writers appeal to:

- words;
- statements;
- propositions;
- narratives;
- commandments;
- promises;
- prophecies;
- and written testimony.

The written object preserves a communicative content.

Thus when the Bible appeals to *graphē*, the authority of the written Scripture encompasses both:

> **the written record**

and:

> **the message conveyed by that written record.**

---

# 20.20 The Remarkable Persistence of Written Testimony

A spoken statement exists as an event of speech.

A written statement can remain available for later generations.

This is one reason writing becomes so significant in the biblical record.

A message spoken to one generation can be written and subsequently read by another.

A prophetic word can be preserved.

A testimony can be transmitted.

A covenant can be recorded.

A historical event can be documented.

A command can be preserved.

A promise can remain before later generations.

Thus the movement from speech to writing is not a trivial change of medium.

Writing gives the testimony a durable form.

This is why the biblical category of *graphē* becomes so important.

The written record is capable of becoming a continuing witness.

---

# 20.21 Graphē and the Written Witness

We can now bring the evidence together.

The lexical evidence establishes that:

> **γραφή belongs to the field of writing and what is written.**

The biblical usage establishes that:

> **γραφή can designate a particular written passage.**

The broader biblical usage establishes that:

> **γραφή can designate the sacred Scriptures as authoritative written testimony.**

The biblical writers further treat these Scriptures as:

- authoritative;
- searchable;
- understandable;
- fulfillable;
- prophetic;
- enduring;
- and capable of functioning as testimony.

The result is not merely a dictionary definition.

It is a demonstrated biblical category.

---

# 20.22 The Argument From Lexical Meaning to Biblical Usage

The argument can now be stated explicitly.

### Premise 1

The lexical field of **γραφή** concerns writing and what is written.

### Premise 2

The New Testament uses **γραφή** for written statements and particular written passages.

### Premise 3

The New Testament also uses **γραφή** as a designation for the sacred Scriptures.

### Premise 4

In these Scriptural contexts, the written material is treated as authoritative testimony.

### Conclusion

> **γραφή (*graphē*) is a biblical designation whose fundamental lexical field concerns writing or what is written and whose New Testament usage extends to the authoritative written Scriptures—Holy Writ.**

This conclusion does not require us to redefine the word as though its lexical meaning were “Bible.”

It follows from the combination of:

> **lexical meaning + contextual usage + repeated biblical function.**

---

# 20.23 What Has Now Been Established?

At this point several things have become clear.

### First

**γραφή is fundamentally connected with writing and what is written.**

### Second

The term can refer to a particular written statement or passage.

### Third

The term is repeatedly used for sacred Scripture.

### Fourth

Scripture is treated within the biblical record as authoritative written testimony.

### Fifth

Jesus and the apostles reason from Scripture and appeal to it as authoritative.

### Sixth

Scripture is presented as something that can speak, be fulfilled, be searched, be understood, and serve as decisive testimony.

### Seventh

The biblical usage therefore gives *graphē* a significance beyond generic writing.

It is the written Scripture under investigation.

---

> **to establish the biblical meaning and usage of γραφή as writing and, in its distinctive biblical usage, Scripture/Holy Writ.**

That foundation is necessary for everything that follows.

# 20.25 Why This Matters for the Doctrine of the Graphē

The title of this work is not built around an arbitrary Greek word.

The investigation has now reached the biblical category that describes the written Scripture itself.

The *graphē* is significant because it stands at the point where communication becomes preserved written testimony.

The Bible contains:

- divine communication;
- prophetic proclamation;
- apostolic testimony;
- historical testimony;
- written records;
- sacred writings;
- and Scripture.

*Graphē* identifies the written dimension of this complex biblical reality.

This gives the present investigation a precise object.

We are not investigating “religion” in the abstract.

We are not merely investigating theological opinions.

We are not merely investigating what later Christians have said about a book.

We are investigating the **written Scripture identified within the biblical record as *graphē*.**

# 20.27 Chapter Conclusion

The investigation began with a simple lexical question:

> **What is γραφή?**

The lexical evidence places the word within the field of writing and what is written. Its relationship to **γράφω** confirms that basic orientation.

The biblical evidence then takes the investigation further.

The New Testament uses **γραφή** for written statements and particular passages, but it also uses the term in a distinctive way for the sacred Scriptures. Jesus and the apostles repeatedly appeal to these writings as authoritative testimony. Scripture is searched, understood, fulfilled, cited, believed, and treated as decisive in theological and historical argument.

The conclusion is therefore warranted:

> **γραφή (*graphē*) is fundamentally a term for writing or what is written, and the biblical writers use it in a distinctive and authoritative sense for Scripture—Holy Writ.**

This distinction is essential.

The word does not mean “the Bible” mechanically in every occurrence. Its lexical field is broader than that. But within its biblical usage, *graphē* becomes a principal designation for the sacred written Scriptures.

The written record has therefore come into focus.

The next stage is to investigate the relationship between **writing, Holy Writ, and Scripture** and to examine how the biblical record identifies authoritative written material.

That is the task of Chapter 21.

# Lexical and Source Notes

## 1. γράφω (*graphō*)

The lexical relationship between **γράφω** and **γραφή** establishes the basic association with writing. LSJ gives γράφω senses including “to scratch, draw, write,” demonstrating the broader historical lexical field from which the noun develops.

## 2. γραφή (*graphē*)

LSJ gives the noun γραφή senses including “drawing, writing,” while New Testament-oriented lexical material identifies the term as a writing or thing written and, in biblical usage, Scripture/Holy Writ.

## 3. Strong's G1124

Strong's G1124 identifies **γραφή** as deriving from γράφω and defines its biblical sense in relation to a document or Holy Writ. The same lexical material distinguishes writing, Scripture, and a particular portion of Holy Scripture.

## 4. Lexical guardrail

The lexical evidence does **not** justify reducing every occurrence of γραφή to the English concept “Bible.” The term's lexical field concerns writing/what is written, while context determines whether a particular occurrence refers to a specific written statement, a portion of Scripture, or Scripture as a recognized sacred body of writings.

This distinction is required by the Source Integration & Evidence Protocol and is foundational for Chapters 20–22.
