## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 13 — Knowing God: Revelation Rather Than Speculation

> **Movement II — God, Truth, and Revelation**

---

## Chapter Assignment

### Locked TOC description

> **Distinguish knowledge supplied by revelation from human speculation about God. The Bible's own account must govern the investigation rather than later philosophical or theological constructions.**

This chapter follows the cumulative argument of Chapters 9–12 and prepares for Chapter 14.

The movement is:

> **What grounds truth?**
>
> ↓
>
> **God as the ground of truth**
>
> ↓
>
> **God's truthfulness, faithfulness, and immutability**
>
> ↓
>
> **God cannot lie**
>
> ↓
>
> **How, then, can God be known?**
>
> ↓
>
> **Through revelation rather than speculation as the ultimate source of knowledge.**

The chapter does **not** prematurely establish the complete authenticity, canon, inspiration, or infallibility of the Bible. Those questions belong to later movements.

Its task is to establish the necessity and biblical reality of revelation, distinguish revelation from human construction, and prepare the reader to examine human accounts about God.

---

# 1. The Question Has Changed

The preceding chapters established a foundation for a question that cannot be answered merely by describing human opinions about God.

Truth is not created by belief.

Reality is not created by assertion.

An ultimate authority cannot be established merely by declaring that it is authoritative.

And if God is the ultimate ground of truth, then the next question is unavoidable:

> **How can human beings know God?**

This is different from asking:

> *What do human beings think about God?*

It is different from asking:

> *What have philosophers concluded about God?*

And it is different from asking:

> *What have theologians, commentators, institutions, traditions, or organizations said about God?*

The central question is:

> **What has God made known concerning Himself, and how can that knowledge be distinguished from human speculation about Him?**

If God has made Himself known, then the task is not to invent an account of God.

It is to **receive, examine, understand, reason through, and faithfully live according to what God has made known**.

---

# 2. Knowledge of God Is Not the Same as Speculation About God

*Speculation* is important to distinguish from knowledge supplied by revelation.

Speculation begins with what the human investigator possesses and attempts to reason beyond what has actually been established.

It may involve:

- conjecture;
- philosophical construction;
- analogy;
- inference from limited observations;
- cultural assumptions;
- inherited concepts;
- metaphysical systems;
- theological models;
- or attempts to explain God without first establishing what God Himself has made known.

Such reasoning may raise legitimate questions. It may also expose contradictions in competing claims.

But it cannot simply be assumed to constitute authoritative knowledge of God.

There is a fundamental difference between:

> **reasoning about God**

and:

> **receiving knowledge from God.**

Reason remains necessary in both cases.

The difference concerns the **source of the premises**.

If a person begins with an unestablished conception of God and constructs a system from it, the resulting system remains dependent upon that human starting point.

If God has disclosed something concerning Himself, however, the investigator has received information whose ultimate source is claimed to be God rather than human conjecture.

The proper intellectual response is therefore not to abandon reason.

It is to **reason through what has been revealed**.

---

# 3. Revelation: The Unveiling of What Is Made Known

Biblical revelation is not merely a religious feeling or private impression.

The New Testament uses the Greek term **ἀποκάλυψις (*apokalypsis*)**, associated with unveiling, disclosure, or manifestation.

The conceptual movement is:

> something concealed
>
> ↓
>
> covering removed
>
> ↓
>
> what was hidden is made manifest
>
> ↓
>
> knowledge becomes available to the recipient.

Revelation is therefore not fundamentally a human achievement.

It is an act of disclosure.

The recipient does not create the revealed content by thinking about it.

The recipient receives what has been made known.

This is why revelation is indispensable to a serious account of knowledge concerning God.

---

# 4. God Is Not a Human Hypothesis Waiting for Human Invention

The preceding chapters established that the question of God cannot simply be settled by importing an external philosophical system and allowing that system to define the object of investigation.

God is not whatever a philosophical school decides God must be.

Nor is God defined by whatever an institution, commentator, theologian, or tradition says about Him.

The order must be reversed.

If God has made Himself known, then:

> **God's revelation must determine what is claimed about God.**

Human reasoning remains indispensable.

Its proper role is to:

- receive the claim;
- identify its meaning;
- examine its evidence;
- distinguish it from alternatives;
- trace its implications;
- test the inference;
- identify contradictions;
- and reach conclusions warranted by what has actually been established.

This is the difference between **reason as an instrument of investigation** and **speculation as a substitute for revelation**.

---

# 5. The Biblical Record Presents God as Communicating

The biblical record does not portray God as permanently silent and inaccessible.

It repeatedly presents God as:

- speaking;
- appearing;
- revealing;
- commanding;
- declaring;
- showing;
- teaching;
- sending;
- and making Himself known.

The modes vary.

The claimed source remains God.

Among the modes presented in the biblical record are:

1. direct divine speech;
2. angelic communication;
3. prophetic communication;
4. visions;
5. dreams;
6. divine manifestations;
7. Jesus Christ;
8. apostolic testimony;
9. written Scripture.

The full treatment of these modes belongs to the later discussion of **how the Bible was given**.

For this chapter, their significance is foundational:

> **The biblical record presents divine knowledge as something God can communicate to human beings.**

---

# 6. God Spoke

The Bible repeatedly records God speaking.

At Sinai, the record describes the voice of God being heard:

> **“And Moses spake, and God answered him by a voice...”**  
> — Exodus 19:19

Deuteronomy records:

> **“Ye heard the voice of the words, but saw no similitude; only ye heard a voice.”**  
> — Deuteronomy 4:12

The significance is straightforward.

The biblical account does not restrict divine knowledge to abstract reflection upon nature.

It presents God as an active communicator.

The New Testament likewise records divine speech.

At the baptism of Jesus:

> **“This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.”**  
> — Matthew 3:17

And in John 12:28, Jesus says:

> **“Father, glorify thy name.”**

The record answers:

> **“Then came there a voice from heaven, saying, I have both glorified it, and will glorify it again.”**

These passages do not yet constitute the complete authentication argument for the Bible.

They establish the narrower point required here:

> **The biblical record presents God as communicating knowledge rather than leaving humanity to construct knowledge of Him unaided.**

---

# 7. God Made Himself Known Through Prophets

Hebrews states:

> **“God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets...”**  
> — Hebrews 1:1

Two things are immediately established.

God **spoke**.

And He spoke:

> **“at sundry times and in divers manners.”**

The biblical account therefore does not reduce revelation to one mechanism.

God could communicate through different means while remaining the source of the communication.

The prophets were consequently not presented merely as philosophers constructing theories about God.

The biblical claim is that God spoke through them.

That claim creates a later evidential question:

> **How can genuine prophetic communication be distinguished from a human claim falsely attributed to God?**

That question belongs to the authentication material later in the book.

For the present chapter, the conclusion is narrower:

> **If God genuinely communicates through a prophet, the knowledge supplied by that communication originates with God rather than with the prophet's independent speculation.**

---

# 8. Revelation Through Jesus Christ

Hebrews continues:

> **“Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son...”**  
> — Hebrews 1:2

The progression is significant.

God spoke by the prophets.

God has spoken by His Son.

The New Testament therefore presents Jesus Christ not merely as another human thinker speaking about God.

It presents Him as the decisive manifestation of God's revelation.

John begins:

> **“In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.”**  
> — John 1:1

And:

> **“And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us...”**  
> — John 1:14

This chapter does not attempt to exhaust Christology.

Its point is that revelation reaches a uniquely central expression in Jesus Christ.

If God has spoken through Christ, then knowledge of God cannot be reduced to philosophical speculation about what God might be like.

There is an actual claimed revelation to investigate.

---

# 9. Revelation Is Not the Same as Interpretation

A crucial distinction must be maintained.

There is a difference between:

> **what God revealed**

and:

> **what a human being thinks the revelation means.**

The first concerns revelation.

The second concerns interpretation.

A divine communication may be perfectly truthful while a human interpreter misunderstands it.

Therefore:

> **The truthfulness of God does not automatically make every human interpretation of God's communication true.**

This is consistent with Chapter 12.

God cannot lie.

Therefore genuine divine communication is truthful.

But the human recipient may:

- misunderstand;
- misremember;
- mistransmit;
- misinterpret;
- selectively report;
- or falsely attribute his own thoughts to God.

Consequently, revelation does not eliminate epistemology.

It makes epistemology more precise.

We must ask:

> What was revealed?

> Who received it?

> How was it communicated?

> What evidence establishes that communication?

> Was it faithfully transmitted?

> What exactly was written?

> How should the written record be understood?

These questions form the architecture of the later chapters.

---

# 10. Revelation Does Not Eliminate Reason

A false alternative is often presented:

> Either human beings reason about God, or God reveals Himself.

The biblical model does not require that choice.

The proper relationship is:

> **God reveals.**
>
> **Human beings receive.**
>
> **Human beings reason through what has been revealed.**

Reason does not become the source of revelation.

Nor does revelation make reason unnecessary.

Instead, reason operates upon the revealed material.

This is why the earlier development of:

- truth;
- evidence;
- rational warrant;
- inference;
- ratiocination;
- dianoetic thinking;
- and sound judgment

was necessary before arriving here.

The investigator must distinguish:

> **a revealed proposition**

from:

> **an inference drawn from that proposition.**

And:

> **a biblical statement**

from:

> **an interpretation of that statement.**

And:

> **what Scripture records**

from:

> **what later writers say Scripture means.**

Without these distinctions, revelation can quickly become indistinguishable from speculation.

---

# 11. The Bible's Own Account Must Govern the Investigation

This is the central methodological principle of the chapter.

When the question concerns what God has revealed, the first responsibility is to investigate the **biblical record itself**.

This does not mean every external historical, linguistic, archaeological, philosophical, or manuscript source is worthless.

It means such sources cannot silently replace the primary object under investigation.

If a commentator says:

> “The Bible teaches X,”

the question is:

> **Where does the Bible teach X?**

If a theologian says:

> “God must be understood according to Y,”

the question is:

> **What establishes Y?**

If a philosophical system says:

> “God cannot be known except through Z,”

the question is:

> **Why should Z govern the investigation?**

The authority of a secondary interpreter cannot simply be presumed.

The interpreter must provide an argument.

And the argument must ultimately be measured against the evidence.

---

# 12. Human Accounts About God Must Remain Distinct From God's Revelation

A distinction must therefore be maintained among:

### God's revelation

What God has actually made known.

### The biblical record

The written record claiming to preserve and communicate that revelation.

### Interpretation

The process by which human beings understand the written record.

### Commentary

Human explanation, analysis, or exposition concerning the record.

### Theology

A systematic attempt to organize claims concerning God and divine things.

### Philosophy

Reasoned inquiry that may address questions concerning God, reality, knowledge, causation, morality, and existence.

These categories must not be collapsed.

A philosophical argument about God is not automatically revelation.

A theological system is not automatically Scripture.

A commentary is not Scripture.

An institution is not Scripture.

A tradition is not Scripture.

A scholar's conclusion is not Scripture merely because the scholar is learned.

The reverse is equally important:

> **The existence of philosophical or theological reasoning does not invalidate revelation.**

The proper question remains:

> **What is actually established, and by what evidence?**

The next chapter will examine this distinction more directly.

---

# 13. Why General Philosophical Arguments Must Not Be Confused With Revelation

Arguments from:

- causation;
- design;
- morality;
- contingency;
- consciousness;
- order;
- or human experience

may raise serious questions concerning God.

Their logical merits can and should be examined.

But their existence does not make them the biblical account.

Nor does their existence displace revelation.

A philosophical argument may reason toward a first cause.

The biblical record may identify the Creator, describe His character, record His acts, preserve His declarations, and report His promises.

These are different levels of knowledge.

Therefore:

> **philosophical reasoning can investigate questions concerning God; revelation supplies knowledge that is claimed to originate with God.**

The two should neither be confused nor falsely opposed.

Reason is not the enemy of revelation.

Speculation is not revelation.

And revelation does not require irrationality.

---

# 14. Revelation Answers Questions Human Speculation Cannot Authoritatively Settle

Suppose human beings reason from the created order and conclude that an intelligent or necessary source of reality exists.

That conclusion would still leave many questions unanswered.

For example:

- What is God's character?
- What has God said?
- What has God done in history?
- What does God intend?
- What has God promised?
- What does God require of human beings?
- What has God revealed about salvation?
- What has God revealed about judgment?
- What has God revealed about His relationship to humanity?

Human speculation cannot simply manufacture authoritative answers to these questions.

If God has answered them, the answers must come through revelation.

That is why revelation is not an optional supplement to human philosophy.

It is the means by which knowledge that originates with God can become available to human beings.

---

# 15. Revelation Creates an Evidential Responsibility

Once revelation is claimed, another mistake must be avoided.

It is not enough to say:

> **“God revealed this.”**

The claim itself requires examination.

Human beings can falsely attribute statements to God.

Therefore the following propositions must remain distinct:

> **God can reveal.**

> **God has revealed.**

> **God revealed this particular message.**

> **This person genuinely received it.**

> **This record faithfully preserves it.**

> **This writing belongs to the authoritative written record.**

Each proposition requires its own justification.

This is one of the book's principal anti-circularity safeguards.

The truthfulness of God establishes the reliability of genuine divine communication.

It does not establish the genuineness of every communication claimed to be divine.

That is why the book will later investigate:

- the modes of revelation;
- witnesses;
- prophetic mediation;
- Christ;
- apostles;
- written testimony;
- inspiration;
- canon;
- manuscript evidence;
- historical evidence;
- and authentication.

---

# 16. Revelation and Historical Knowledge

The biblical model also means that knowledge of God can enter history.

If God speaks to Moses, if prophets receive messages, if Jesus speaks and acts publicly, and if apostles witness events, then revelation is not merely a private psychological experience.

It can involve:

- people;
- places;
- times;
- words;
- actions;
- witnesses;
- records;
- and historical consequences.

This becomes especially significant concerning Jesus Christ.

The New Testament does not merely say that Jesus represents an inspiring idea.

It records claims about an actual person acting in history.

Peter states:

> **“For we have not followed cunningly devised fables, when we made known unto you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but were eyewitnesses of his majesty.”**  
> — 2 Peter 1:16

The word **eyewitnesses** matters.

It places testimony inside the evidential structure.

The claim is not merely:

> “We reasoned that Jesus must have been glorious.”

It is:

> **“We saw.”**

The question of how such testimony is authenticated will be treated later.

But the distinction is already important:

> **revelation can involve public historical events and testimony, not merely speculation.**

---

# 17. Jesus Christ as the Faithful Witness

The biblical account goes further.

Revelation 1:5 identifies Jesus Christ as:

> **“the faithful witness.”**

This matters for the doctrine of revelation.

Jesus is not merely someone who heard a message and repeated it.

The biblical record presents Him as the faithful witness Himself.

His witness therefore becomes central to the investigation of God's self-disclosure.

And the significance of witness extends beyond the small group who physically observed particular events.

Jesus' historical existence, ministry, crucifixion, death, and the testimony concerning His resurrection have generated consequences reaching across:

- individuals;
- families;
- communities;
- kingdoms;
- empires;
- nations;
- institutions;
- cultures;
- and generations.

The point here is not to establish every later Christological conclusion.

It is to recognize why the historical witness concerning Jesus deserves direct examination rather than reduction to abstract speculation.

---

# 18. Revelation, Transmission, Record, and Interpretation Are Different

Another distinction is essential.

Revelation can occur at the moment God makes something known.

But preserving that revelation for later generations involves additional processes.

The sequence may be represented as:

> **God communicates**
>
> ↓
>
> **a recipient receives**
>
> ↓
>
> **the recipient hears, sees, or witnesses**
>
> ↓
>
> **the communication is transmitted**
>
> ↓
>
> **the communication is recorded**
>
> ↓
>
> **the record is preserved**
>
> ↓
>
> **later readers receive the written testimony**
>
> ↓
>
> **the text is interpreted**

These are not identical events.

Therefore:

> **revelation is not identical to inspiration;**

> **inspiration is not identical to canon;**

> **canon is not identical to manuscript transmission;**

> **transmission is not identical to interpretation.**

The later movements of the book will examine these distinctions individually.

This chapter establishes why they must be distinguished.

---

# 19. The Bible Is Not a Blank Philosophical Slate

Once revelation is recognized as a biblical category, the student's approach to the written record must change.

The question is no longer:

> “What theory can I invent about God?”

It becomes:

> **“What does the written record actually say that God has made known?”**

Then:

> **“What establishes that this record genuinely represents that revelation?”**

Then:

> **“What does the text mean?”**

And finally:

> **“What conclusions follow from it?”**

This sequence is critical.

It prevents the reader from jumping directly from:

> “I accept the Bible”

to:

> “therefore my interpretation is correct.”

It also prevents the opposite error:

> “I have a philosophical theory”

from becoming:

> “therefore Scripture must mean what my theory requires.”

The proper movement is:

> **revelation → record → authentication → interpretation → inference → application.**

---

# 20. The Authority of Revelation Derives From Its Source

The preceding chapters established that God is the ultimate ground of truth and that falsehood is incompatible with Him.

This permits an important inference.

If God genuinely communicates a proposition, its truth does not depend upon human approval.

Its truth derives from its divine source.

Likewise, its authority does not derive from:

- popularity;
- institutional recognition;
- majority vote;
- academic prestige;
- political power;
- cultural tradition;
- or personal preference.

Its authority derives from **God as the source of the communication**.

But this again creates the authentication question.

A human being cannot simply declare:

> **“This is from God.”**

and thereby transfer God's authority to his own statement.

The source must actually be God.

That is why the later evidential investigation is necessary.

---

# 21. What Revelation Does to Epistemology

The doctrine of revelation therefore modifies—not destroys—the epistemological problem.

Before revelation, the question is:

> **How can anything concerning God be known?**

Once a claimed revelation appears, the questions become more precise:

> **What was revealed?**

> **Who revealed it?**

> **How was it communicated?**

> **Who received it?**

> **What evidence establishes the communication?**

> **What witnesses exist?**

> **How was it transmitted?**

> **What record preserves it?**

> **What makes that record authentic?**

> **What does the record mean?**

This is a much stronger intellectual position than either blind acceptance or endless speculation.

It moves the investigation from:

> **possibility of knowledge**

to:

> **claimed revelation**

to:

> **evidence**

to:

> **authentication**

to:

> **interpretation.**

---

# 22. What This Chapter Has Established

We can now state the conclusions carefully.

### First

> **God is not merely an object of human speculation.**

The biblical record presents God as a communicating reality who makes Himself known.

### Second

> **Revelation is disclosure from God to human beings.**

It concerns what God makes known rather than what human beings merely invent.

### Third

> **The biblical record presents multiple modes of divine communication.**

These include direct speech, prophetic communication, visions, dreams, Christ, apostolic witness, and other forms that the later chapters will examine individually.

### Fourth

> **Revelation and interpretation are not identical.**

God's truthful communication does not make every human interpretation of it infallible.

### Fifth

> **Reason remains necessary.**

The proper response to revelation is disciplined reception, examination, reasoning, understanding, and obedience to what has actually been established.

### Sixth

> **Human commentary cannot replace the primary record.**

Philosophers, theologians, commentators, institutions, and traditions may offer arguments or interpretations, but their claims remain claims to be examined.

### Seventh

> **A genuine divine communication would be truthful because its source is truthful.**

Chapter 12 established why this follows from the nature of God.

### Eighth

> **The claim that a particular communication is genuinely from God still requires authentication.**

This prevents circular reasoning and prepares the book for its later evidential investigation.

---

# 23. The Central Principle

The chapter can therefore be compressed into one principle:

> **We do not know God authoritatively by inventing what God must be like; we know what God has made known by receiving and rightly reasoning through His revelation.**

This principle does not make human reasoning unnecessary.

It gives human reasoning its proper object.

Nor does it make every claimed revelation true.

It gives us a criterion for asking whether a claimed revelation is genuinely from God.

Nor does it make every interpretation correct.

It requires us to distinguish what was revealed from what an interpreter concludes.

And it does not yet settle the complete question of the Bible.

It establishes why that question must now be investigated.

---

# 24. Transition to Chapter 14

If revelation is the means by which knowledge of God can be supplied from God Himself, another danger immediately appears.

Human beings do not merely receive revelation.

They also **speak about revelation**.

They summarize it.

They interpret it.

They systematize it.

They institutionalize it.

They debate it.

They sometimes distort it.

And they can place their own conclusions beside the revelation itself until the distinction disappears.

The next question is therefore unavoidable:

> **What happens when human accounts about God are mistaken for God's own revelation?**

That is the problem of Chapter 14.
