# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT I — TRUTH AND THE POSSIBILITY OF KNOWLEDGE

# Chapter 1 — What Is Truth?

> **Central question:** What is truth?

## Chapter Purpose

This chapter establishes the first foundation of the entire investigation: **truth must be distinguished from assertion, belief, opinion, convention, authority, popularity, and interpretation because truth concerns what is actually the case.**

The question is deliberately placed before the questions of God, revelation, Scripture, Graphē, authentication, and authority. Those later conclusions depend upon a standard by which truth claims can be examined.

The governing movement is:

> **Truth → Reality → Knowledge → Evidence → Warrant**

The chapter therefore does not begin by asking the reader to accept an institutional or theological conclusion. It begins with the nature of truth itself.

---

## 1. The Question Comes Before the Conclusion

A person may say:

> “This is true.”

That statement is a **truth claim**. It is not yet an explanation of why the claim is true.

A second person may say:

> “I believe this is true.”

That statement tells us something about the person's belief. It does not, by itself, establish the truth of what is believed.

A third person may say:

> “Everyone in our institution believes this.”

That tells us something about institutional agreement. It does not convert agreement into truth.

A fourth may say:

> “This has been taught for hundreds of years.”

That establishes antiquity of transmission, not necessarily truth.

A fifth may say:

> “A respected scholar says it.”

That establishes that a scholar has made the claim, not that the claim corresponds to reality.

These distinctions are not attacks upon belief, institutions, scholarship, tradition, or testimony. They are distinctions between **different kinds of propositions and different kinds of support**.

The question of this chapter is more fundamental:

> **When we call something true, what are we saying about it?**

---

# 2. Truth Concerns What Is Actually the Case

At its most basic level, truth concerns **conformity to reality or actuality**.

If the proposition

> “There is a book on the table”

is true, then there really is a book on the table.

The proposition is not made true because somebody believes it. It is not made true because ten people agree with it. It is not made true because an institution has printed it. It is not made true because it has been repeated.

Its truth depends upon the state of affairs to which the proposition refers.

This gives us a foundational distinction:

> **Truth concerns reality; belief concerns a person's acceptance of a proposition about reality.**

The distinction is simple, but its consequences are extensive.

A person can believe what is true.

A person can believe what is false.

A person can reject what is true.

A person can accept what is false.

Therefore:

> **Belief does not create truth.**

The investigator's task is not to manufacture truth but to determine, understand, and faithfully represent what is true.

---

# 3. Truth Is Not the Same as Assertion

An assertion is an expressed claim.

Truth is a property of a claim insofar as the claim corresponds to reality.

This distinction prevents a common intellectual error: treating the presence of a statement as if it were proof of the statement.

For example:

> “God exists.”

is a proposition.

> “God does not exist.”

is another proposition.

The existence of either sentence on a page proves neither proposition.

The sentences have to be examined according to the question they raise.

Likewise:

> “The Bible is the Word of God.”

is a proposition of enormous importance.

But the mere presence of the proposition does not, by itself, answer the question of why the proposition should be accepted.

That does **not** mean the proposition is false.

It means that the investigation must distinguish:

1. **the claim;**
2. **the reality to which the claim refers;**
3. **the evidence bearing upon the claim;**
4. **the reasoning connecting the evidence to the conclusion.**

This distinction will become indispensable when the investigation later asks what the Bible is and why its claim to divine revelation should be regarded as authentic.

---

# 4. Fact, Truth, and Reality

The terms **fact**, **truth**, and **reality** are related but should not simply be treated as interchangeable.

### Reality

Reality concerns what actually exists or what actually occurs.

### Fact

A fact is an actual state of affairs or an occurrence that is the case.

### Truth

Truth concerns the correspondence between what is asserted and what is actually the case.

Thus:

> **Reality is what is.**

> **A fact is something that is actually the case.**

> **A true proposition accurately represents what is actually the case.**

This distinction gives the investigation a standard.

When a claim is made, the question is not merely:

> “Who said it?”

The prior question is:

> **“Is it actually the case?”**

The question of who said it may become important later because testimony can be evidence. But testimony is evidence **because it bears upon reality**, not because the mere act of speaking creates reality.

---

# 5. Truth Is Not Created by Agreement

Agreement can be valuable evidence in certain circumstances, especially when independent witnesses converge. But agreement itself does not create truth.

Suppose ten people agree that an event occurred. Their agreement does not cause the event to have occurred.

The evidential question is instead:

> **Why do these ten people agree, and does their testimony reliably correspond to what actually happened?**

This distinction becomes especially important when examining historical testimony.

The Bible contains witnesses, testimony, reported events, prophetic claims, and apostolic testimony. These are not to be dismissed merely because they are testimony. Nor are they to be accepted merely because they are called testimony.

They must be examined as evidence.

Thus:

> **Testimony may warrant a conclusion; testimony is not itself the definition of truth.**

This is one reason the later chapters on eyewitnesses, apostles, miracles, prophecy, historical correspondence, manuscripts, preservation, and cumulative authentication are necessary.

---

# 6. Truth Is Not Created by Institutions

An institution can establish rules for its members.

It can publish a doctrine.

It can authorize a teacher.

It can recognize a credential.

It can adopt a statement.

It can declare an interpretation orthodox.

None of those actions, by themselves, creates truth.

An institutional declaration may be **correct**. It may also be **incorrect**.

Therefore institutional status cannot be used as a substitute for examination.

This distinction is particularly important for the later investigation of Scripture.

A body of people does not make Scripture authoritative by deciding that it is authoritative. If Scripture possesses divine authority, that authority must arise from what Scripture **is** and from the reality of its divine origin—not from the institutional status of those who recognize it.

Conversely, an institution does not cease to exist merely because its claim is questioned. The question is whether its claim is true.

The governing principle is:

> **Authority does not manufacture truth; genuine authority derives from what is true.**

The question of ultimate authority will therefore arise only after the nature of truth and the grounds for recognizing truth have been developed.

---

# 7. Truth and Interpretation

Another distinction must be maintained:

> **Truth is not identical with interpretation.**

An interpretation is an account of what something means.

An interpretation may be correct or incorrect.

If a written statement has an actual meaning intended by its author, a reader does not create that meaning simply by assigning a preferred interpretation to it.

This becomes especially important once the investigation reaches the Graphē.

The authority of Scripture and the correctness of an interpreter are two different questions.

A person may possess an authoritative text and misunderstand it.

A teacher may quote Scripture and draw an invalid conclusion.

An institution may possess Scripture and develop a doctrine that does not follow from Scripture.

Therefore the investigation must ultimately distinguish:

> **the authority of the text**

from

> **the fallibility of the interpreter.**

That is why hermeneutics belongs later in the architecture. First the authority of the text must be established; then the discipline required to understand and rightly handle that text can be developed.

---

# 8. Truth Is Not Popularity, Antiquity, or Repetition

Three further substitutes for truth must be rejected.

## Popularity

A proposition does not become true because many people believe it.

Majority agreement can sometimes constitute evidence about what people perceive or testify to, but majority agreement is not the definition of truth.

## Antiquity

A claim does not become true because it is old.

An ancient falsehood remains false.

An ancient truth remains true.

Age is therefore not the standard by which truth is determined.

## Repetition

Repetition can make a proposition familiar without making it true.

A proposition repeated one thousand times remains the same proposition. If it was false before the repetition, repetition does not change its relation to reality.

This distinction is especially important for biblical study.

Repeated quotation can preserve genuine knowledge, but repetition without understanding can also preserve misunderstanding.

The later question is therefore not whether quotation is valuable. It is:

> **What is being quoted, what does it mean, what does it establish, and how does the conclusion follow?**

That is why quotation, meaning, and knowledge are treated separately later in the book.

---

# 9. Truth and Error

If truth concerns conformity to reality, then error is a failure of conformity to reality.

Error can arise in different ways.

A person may:

- perceive incorrectly;
- remember incorrectly;
- misunderstand language;
- infer incorrectly;
- confuse one category with another;
- omit relevant evidence;
- accept an unsupported premise;
- draw a conclusion stronger than the evidence warrants;
- misread a text;
- or simply assert what is not the case.

This gives us an important distinction:

> **Error is not necessarily deception.**

A person can be completely sincere and still be wrong.

That observation does not make sincerity worthless. It means sincerity and truth answer different questions.

Sincerity concerns the person's genuineness.

Truth concerns whether the claim corresponds to reality.

Therefore the proper intellectual response to error is not merely to ask whether the speaker is sincere. It is to ask:

> **Is the proposition true, and why?**

This distinction will later become important in examining doctrinal turning aside, accepted error, aridity, and unskillfulness.

---

# 10. What Makes a Truth Claim Rationally Investigable?

A truth claim becomes intellectually investigable when the proposition can be examined in relation to reality and the grounds offered for accepting it.

The basic discipline is:

> **Claim → Definition → Evidence → Analysis → Inference → Conclusion**

This formula does not mean every argument must be presented mechanically. It means that conclusions should not float free of their premises.

Consider the claim:

> “This historical event occurred.”

The investigator can ask:

- What exactly is being claimed?
- When did it allegedly occur?
- Who reports it?
- What evidence bears upon it?
- Are the witnesses in a position to know?
- Are their accounts consistent?
- Is there independent corroboration?
- Are there competing explanations?
- Does the conclusion follow from the evidence?

The same intellectual discipline will later be applied to the claim that the Bible is divine revelation.

The subject may be sacred.

The reasoning must nevertheless be serious.

---

# 11. Truth and Rational Warrant

Truth and warrant must also be distinguished.

A proposition may be true even when a particular person does not possess sufficient evidence to know that it is true.

Conversely, a person may have reasons for believing a proposition that later prove inadequate.

Therefore:

> **Truth concerns whether the proposition corresponds to reality.**

> **Warrant concerns whether the grounds for accepting the proposition adequately support the conclusion.**

This distinction is essential to the whole architecture of *One Source of Truth*.

The book is not merely asking:

> “What is true?”

It is also asking:

> **“How can we know what is true, and what warrants that knowledge?”**

Those are the epistemological and evidential questions that follow the ontological question.

---

# 12. Why This Matters for the Bible

At this point, nothing has yet been assumed about the Bible's divine origin.

That restraint is deliberate.

The book will eventually examine claims such as:

> **“Thy word is truth.”**  
> — John 17:17

and:

> **“the scripture cannot be broken.”**  
> — John 10:35

But these statements cannot be placed at the beginning as unexplained premises if the purpose is to establish why Scripture should function as the primary written authority.

The investigation must first understand what truth means.

It must then examine reality, knowledge, evidence, reasoning, and warrant.

It must ask what can ground truth.

It must examine the relation of God to truth.

It must then ask what revelation is.

Only after that can it properly ask:

> **What is the Bible?**

Then:

> **How was its content given?**

Then:

> **What is γραφή (*graphē*)?**

Then:

> **Why is it Holy Writ?**

Then:

> **What evidence authenticates the written record?**

Then:

> **Why should Scripture be regarded as divine revelation?**

And ultimately:

> **Why does Jesus say that “the scripture cannot be broken”?**

The order is not accidental.

The later conclusions depend upon the earlier foundations.

---

# 13. A Biblical Dimension of Truth

The Bible itself does not treat truth as an inconsequential abstraction.

Jesus says:

> **“Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth.”**  
> — John 17:17

The statement places **truth** and **the Word** in direct relation.

But the full significance of that relation cannot be adequately developed in this first chapter. John 17:17 will be treated later after the investigation has established the nature and authentication of the written record.

Similarly, the biblical account repeatedly associates God with truthfulness.

The later investigation will therefore examine texts concerning God's truthfulness, faithfulness, immutability, and inability to lie, including:

- Numbers 23:19
- 1 Samuel 15:29
- Psalm 119:160
- John 14:6
- John 17:17
- Romans 3:4
- Titus 1:2
- Hebrews 6:17–18
- James 1:17
- 2 Corinthians 1:18

These passages will not be used as isolated proof texts. Their role will be to establish the biblical relationship between **God, reality, truth, and revelation**.

---

# 14. The First Governing Principle

The investigation can now state its first governing principle:

> **A claim is not true because it is asserted; it is true because it corresponds to reality.**

From that follows a second:

> **The fact that a person believes a proposition does not determine whether the proposition is true.**

From that follows a third:

> **The fact that an institution teaches a proposition does not determine whether the proposition is true.**

From that follows a fourth:

> **The fact that a proposition is ancient, popular, repeated, or sincerely held does not by itself establish its truth.**

And from these follows the methodological requirement:

> **Truth claims must be examined according to reality, evidence, reasoning, and warrant.**

This does not place human reason above truth.

It places reasoning in the service of discovering and accurately representing truth.

---

# 15. Why the Investigation Begins Here

The entire doctrine of the Graphē depends upon truth.

If truth were merely whatever an individual preferred, no written authority could objectively govern knowledge.

If truth were merely institutional agreement, the strongest institution would determine reality.

If truth were merely repetition, whoever repeated a proposition most effectively would determine what is true.

If truth were merely personal experience, incompatible experiences could each manufacture their own realities.

But if truth concerns what actually is, then the investigator has an objective question before him:

> **What is actually the case?**

That question makes evidence meaningful.

It makes testimony meaningful.

It makes revelation meaningful.

It makes Scripture meaningful.

It makes authentication meaningful.

It makes interpretation meaningful.

And it makes error possible to identify as error.

The investigation therefore begins not with the Bible as an unexplained premise, but with the concept of truth that makes the question of a truthful written revelation intelligible in the first place.

---

# Chapter Conclusion

Truth is not created by belief, popularity, institutional declaration, antiquity, repetition, or personal preference.

Truth concerns **what is actually the case**.

A true proposition corresponds to reality; an erroneous proposition fails to do so. The task of the investigator is therefore not to manufacture truth but to determine what is true, understand it accurately, and represent it faithfully.

This distinction creates the foundation for everything that follows.

If truth concerns reality, then the next question is unavoidable:

> **What is the relationship between truth and reality itself?**

That is the subject of Chapter 2.

### Conceptual distinctions established in this chapter

- Truth ≠ belief.
- Truth ≠ assertion.
- Truth ≠ institutional acceptance.
- Truth ≠ popularity.
- Truth ≠ antiquity.
- Truth ≠ repetition.
- Truth ≠ interpretation.
- Sincerity ≠ truth.
- Evidence ≠ truth.
- Warrant ≠ truth.
- Testimony ≠ truth, although testimony can be evidence for truth.
- Interpretation ≠ authority.
- Human recognition of authority ≠ the source of genuine authority.

### Source integration

This chapter follows the locked architecture's requirement that the investigation begin with **truth, reality, knowledge, evidence, reasoning, and warrant**, before moving to ultimate authority, God, revelation, the Bible, Graphē, authentication, Christ, and Scripture's authority.

It also follows the master authoring discipline:

> **Claim → Definition → Evidence → Analysis → Inference → Conclusion → Consequence**

The chapter deliberately does not attempt to prove the entire doctrine of the Graphē prematurely. Its function is to establish the conceptual standard required by the chapters that follow.

---

## Author's Guardrail for Chapter 1

> **Do not merely tell the reader that truth is important. Establish what truth means, distinguish it from the things commonly confused with it, and show why those distinctions are necessary for the investigation that follows.**

> **Do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture. Teach the reader to reason toward truth and, when the biblical record is subsequently examined, to reason through Scripture.**
