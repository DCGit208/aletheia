# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT I — TRUTH AND THE POSSIBILITY OF KNOWLEDGE

# Chapter 2 — Truth and Reality

> **Central question:** What is the relationship between truth and what actually is?

## Chapter Purpose

Chapter 1 established truth as conformity to what is actually the case. This chapter now moves one step deeper: **what does it mean for something to be real, and why is reality the reference point by which a truth claim succeeds or fails?**

The purpose is not to repeat the definition of truth from Chapter 1, but to examine the relation between **truth and reality**, and to distinguish reality from the several things human beings may substitute for it: assertion, perception, opinion, interpretation, convention, appearance, and representation.

The governing movement is:

> **Reality → apprehension → proposition → correspondence → truth or error**

The question is therefore not merely whether a person has a perception or a proposition, but whether what is perceived, asserted, interpreted, or represented corresponds to what actually is.

---

# 1. Reality Is Not Created by Our Description of It

Reality does not begin when someone describes it.

A mountain exists whether a person calls it a mountain, a hill, a sacred place, a geological formation, or something else.

An event either occurred or did not occur.

A person either lived at a particular time or did not.

A statement either corresponds to what happened or it does not.

Our descriptions can therefore be **about reality without being reality itself**.

This is the first distinction required by the chapter:

> **The thing described is not identical with the description of the thing.**

The distinction may appear elementary, but it is indispensable whenever the investigation concerns historical records, testimony, Scripture, prophecy, revelation, or interpretation.

A written sentence can refer to a real event.

A witness can report a real event.

A reader can interpret the report.

The event, the testimony, and the interpretation are not three versions of the same ontological object. They occupy different levels in the investigation.

The event concerns what happened.

The testimony concerns what someone reports about what happened.

The interpretation concerns what someone understands the testimony to mean.

Truth enters the relation between the representation and the reality represented.

---

# 2. Reality and the Proposition About Reality

Consider the proposition:

> **“Rain fell yesterday.”**

There are at least two different things here:

1. the proposition that rain fell; and
2. the actual state of affairs to which the proposition refers.

If rain actually fell, the proposition corresponds to reality.

If rain did not fall, the proposition does not correspond to reality.

The proposition does not become true merely because it is grammatically correct, confidently stated, widely repeated, or sincerely believed.

Its truth depends upon the reality to which it refers.

This gives us a more precise formulation:

> **A proposition is true when what it asserts corresponds to what is actually the case.**

The proposition is therefore capable of being evaluated.

That evaluability is essential.

Without an independent reality to which propositions refer, there would be no meaningful distinction between:

- what happened and what someone claims happened;
- what exists and what someone imagines exists;
- what is true and what is merely asserted;
- what God has actually revealed and what someone claims God revealed.

The existence of reality is therefore not a peripheral philosophical concern. It is what makes truth and error distinguishable in the first place.

---

# 3. Reality Is Not Perception

Human beings encounter reality through perception.

But perception and reality must not be collapsed into one another.

A person can see something accurately.

A person can see something inaccurately.

A person can hear correctly.

A person can mishear.

A person can remember correctly.

A person can remember incorrectly.

A person can understand what he has perceived.

A person can misunderstand it.

The fact that perception is fallible does not imply that reality is unknowable.

It means that the process by which a knower apprehends reality must itself be examined.

This distinction is especially important in the study of historical testimony.

Suppose a witness reports:

> “I saw this event.”

The report presents a claim about an experience.

The investigator must then ask questions such as:

- Was the witness actually present?
- Was the witness in a position to observe?
- Were the conditions suitable for observation?
- Did the witness understand what was observed?
- Was the account recorded accurately?
- Is the testimony internally coherent?
- Is there corroborating evidence?
- Are there reasons to regard the testimony as unreliable?

The existence of these questions does not make eyewitness testimony worthless.

Quite the opposite.

It shows why testimony can function as evidence **precisely because it purports to connect us with events that occurred in reality**.

---

# 4. Reality Is Not Opinion

An opinion is a person's judgment, assessment, or view.

Opinions can concern reality, but an opinion does not create the reality about which it speaks.

Two people may hold opposite opinions concerning a historical event.

Both opinions cannot make mutually contradictory accounts of the same event simultaneously true in the same respect.

For example:

> “The event occurred.”

and:

> “The event did not occur.”

cannot both correspond to the same state of affairs in the same sense at the same time.

This is not a statement about the value of the people making the claims.

It is a statement about contradiction.

If two propositions directly contradict one another, at least one of them must fail to correspond to reality.

This is why intellectual investigation requires more than cataloguing opinions.

The task is to determine which proposition, if either, corresponds to reality.

---

# 5. Reality Is Not Convention

A convention is an agreed way of treating something.

Conventions are useful.

Human societies establish conventions concerning:

- language;
- measurement;
- legal procedures;
- institutional titles;
- monetary systems;
- calendars;
- classifications;
- social practices.

But a convention should not be confused with an ontological fact.

People may agree to use a particular word for an object. Their agreement does not create the object.

People may establish a particular unit of measurement. Their convention does not create the quantity being measured.

People may establish a rule for an institution. Their rule does not alter every fact about the world outside that institution.

This distinction becomes decisive when truth itself is discussed.

If truth were merely convention, then a proposition could become true by sufficient agreement.

But if truth is correspondence to reality, convention cannot determine whether reality is what the convention says it is.

A convention can determine **how we describe something**.

It cannot, merely by agreement, determine **what actually exists**.

---

# 6. Reality Is Not Interpretation

Interpretation is necessary whenever human beings encounter language, testimony, symbols, events, or written records.

But interpretation is not identical with the thing interpreted.

A text exists.

A proposition exists within the text.

A reader interprets the proposition.

The reader's interpretation can correspond to the meaning of the text, or it can fail to do so.

Therefore:

> **The existence of interpretation does not eliminate the possibility of correct interpretation.**

Nor does the existence of disagreement mean that all interpretations are equally valid.

If two interpretations are mutually contradictory, the question remains:

> **Which interpretation better corresponds to what the text actually means?**

That question presupposes that meaning is not simply manufactured by the reader.

This distinction will become crucial much later in the book when we reach the hermeneutical question:

> **Once we have an authoritative truthful text, how do we correctly understand it?**

That question cannot be answered yet in full.

But its foundation must be laid here:

> **An authoritative text can be misunderstood by an unauthorized or unskilled interpretation.**

The authority of the text and the accuracy of the interpreter are therefore distinct questions.

---

# 7. Appearance Is Not the Whole of Reality

Reality is also not reducible to immediate appearance.

Something may appear one way while further investigation establishes something more precise.

The sun appears to move across the sky, but appearance alone does not settle the astronomical explanation.

A distant object may appear small while being physically enormous.

A historical document may appear authentic while requiring further examination.

A statement may appear plausible while its premises are false.

This does not mean perception is useless.

It means:

> **Appearance is data for investigation, not an automatic substitute for investigation.**

Reasoning begins with what is apprehended and proceeds toward judgment.

That movement requires attention to evidence, inference, comparison, and correction.

The objective is not to distrust perception indiscriminately.

The objective is to avoid confusing **what something appears to be** with **what it actually is**.

---

# 8. Reality, Representation, and Testimony

This distinction becomes especially important for the Bible.

A historical record is a representation of events, declarations, acts, testimony, and revelation.

The existence of a written record does not mean the physical pages themselves are identical with every event recorded in them.

Rather, the written record **testifies to** what it presents as having occurred.

This allows us to distinguish:

> **event → witness → testimony → written record → reader**

Each stage raises a different question.

### Event

What actually happened?

### Witness

Who encountered or received knowledge concerning it?

### Testimony

What did the witness report?

### Written record

How was the testimony or revelation committed to writing?

### Reader

Does the reader correctly understand what has been written?

This layered structure will become central when the book examines the Bible's historical and revelatory character.

It also explains why the later treatment of eyewitness testimony must not be reduced to the statement that “someone wrote it down.”

The real question is whether the record is a trustworthy representation of reality.

---

# 9. Reality and Historical Knowledge

Historical knowledge necessarily concerns realities that are no longer directly before the observer.

We do not presently stand at the Red Sea.

We do not presently stand at Sinai.

We do not presently stand beside the tomb of Jesus.

We do not presently stand among the first witnesses of His resurrection.

Yet it does not follow that these events are therefore inaccessible to rational investigation.

Historical knowledge works through evidence.

That evidence may include:

- eyewitness testimony;
- written records;
- multiple independent witnesses;
- documentary transmission;
- material evidence;
- external corroboration;
- chronological relationships;
- linguistic evidence;
- fulfilled predictions;
- continuity between earlier and later records;
- and the consequences an event produced in history.

The important point is methodological:

> **Absence of direct present perception is not equivalent to absence of knowledge.**

If it were, virtually all historical knowledge would collapse.

The rational question is instead:

> **What evidence connects us to the reality of the past, and how strong is the inference from that evidence?**

That is the question the later chapters on the Bible's authenticity will develop in greater depth.

---

# 10. Reality and Divine Revelation

The question of revelation introduces another important distinction.

If God has revealed something that is actually the case, then the content of that revelation concerns reality.

The question is therefore not:

> “Is revelation merely someone's religious opinion?”

It is:

> **“Did God actually reveal this, and does the resulting record accurately communicate what He revealed?”**

Those are evidential questions.

They cannot be answered simply by saying:

> “The Bible says so.”

That would assume the very point being investigated.

Nor can they be dismissed simply because the claim concerns divine revelation.

The proper investigation must examine the evidence and the reasoning that connects the evidence to the conclusion.

This is why the book's knowledge flow proceeds from:

> **Truth → Reality → Knowledge → Evidence → Reason and rational warrant → Ultimate authority → God and the ground of truth → Divine revelation → What the Bible is → Graphē/Holy Writ.**

The order prevents circular reasoning.

---

# 11. The Reality of God Is Not the Same Question as the Truth of a Proposition About God

A proposition about God is a statement.

God, if God exists, is not merely a statement.

This distinction is necessary because the investigation will later ask:

> **What grounds truth?**

and:

> **Why is God the ground of truth?**

A proposition such as:

> “God is true.”

must therefore not be treated as though the sentence itself were God.

Likewise:

> “God exists.”

is not identical with the existence of God.

The proposition is a representation.

The reality, if the proposition is true, is what makes the proposition true.

This distinction will protect the later chapters from a category error: confusing **a claim concerning ultimate reality** with **the reality itself**.

It also explains why the book must eventually investigate God's attributes rather than merely accumulate quotations about Him.

---

# 12. The Principle of Correspondence

The relationship developed in this chapter can now be stated formally:

> **Reality provides what is actually the case.**

> **A proposition represents or asserts something about what is the case.**

> **Truth obtains when the proposition corresponds to reality.**

> **Error occurs when the proposition fails to correspond to reality.**

This is the basic correspondence structure underlying the investigation.

It does not solve every philosophical question about truth.

It does, however, provide the minimum intellectual framework required to distinguish a true account from a false one.

It also prevents a destructive relativism in which contradictory claims can all be called “true” simply because different people hold them.

If two claims contradict one another, reality cannot be altered merely to accommodate both.

---

# 13. Why This Matters for the Investigation of Scripture

The Bible makes claims about reality.

It speaks about:

- God;
- creation;
- human beings;
- historical events;
- nations;
- covenants;
- prophecy;
- moral realities;
- the life of Jesus Christ;
- His death;
- His resurrection;
- the actions of apostles;
- and the divine communication recorded in Scripture.

These claims are not merely statements about what someone happens to believe.

They are presented as claims concerning what actually occurred, what actually exists, and what God has actually said or revealed.

Therefore the question of biblical authenticity is inseparable from the question of reality.

If a claimed biblical event did not occur, a historical proposition concerning that event would be false.

If a reported divine communication was not actually given by God, the claim concerning its divine origin would be false.

If a prophetic claim was genuinely made and later fulfilled in the manner asserted, that fulfillment becomes evidence relevant to the claim.

If eyewitness testimony reliably reports an event actually witnessed, it provides evidence connecting the reader with a historical reality.

This is why the Bible cannot responsibly be reduced either to:

- private religious experience,
- institutional tradition,
- philosophical speculation,
- or a collection of quotations detached from their evidential and historical claims.

The investigation must ask what actually happened and what the record establishes.

---

# 14. A Necessary Distinction: Reality Does Not Mean “Whatever I Can Personally Verify”

One further correction is necessary.

To say that truth corresponds to reality does **not** mean that only personally observable facts are real.

That would reduce reality to the limits of one individual's experience.

A person can know that a historical event occurred without having personally witnessed it, provided sufficient evidence connects the person rationally to that event.

Likewise, a person can know something about a distant place through reliable testimony.

Knowledge can therefore extend beyond immediate perception.

The relevant question is not:

> “Did I personally see it?”

but:

> **“What grounds do I have for concluding that it occurred?”**

That distinction is crucial for a book concerned with revelation and historical testimony.

The Bible's claims will not be evaluated by the impossible requirement that every reader personally witness every biblical event.

They will be evaluated through the evidence available to establish the reliability and authenticity of the record.

---

# 15. The Ontological Question Must Precede the Evidential Question

The order now becomes clearer.

Before asking:

> “What evidence proves this?”

we must know:

> “What is the claim about?”

Before asking whether evidence establishes a proposition, we must understand the proposition.

Before asking whether a written record is trustworthy, we must distinguish the record from the reality to which it testifies.

Before asking whether an interpretation is correct, we must distinguish interpretation from the text interpreted.

And before asking whether Scripture is an authoritative record of divine revelation, we must understand what reality, truth, evidence, and rational warrant mean.

Thus the four governing questions remain distinct:

### Ontological

> **What is true/reality?**

### Epistemological

> **How can truth be known?**

### Evidential

> **What warrants a truth claim?**

### Hermeneutical

> **Once we have an authoritative truthful text, how do we correctly understand it?**

Chapter 2 is principally ontological, but it prepares the epistemological and evidential questions that follow.

---

# 16. The Consequence for the Study of Truth

The result is a disciplined intellectual posture.

When confronted with a claim, do not ask only:

> “Who believes this?”

Ask:

> **“What reality does this claim describe?”**

Do not ask only:

> “How strongly is it asserted?”

Ask:

> **“What is actually the case?”**

Do not ask only:

> “How many people agree?”

Ask:

> **“What evidence connects the claim to reality?”**

Do not ask only:

> “What does this interpretation say?”

Ask:

> **“What does the text actually mean, and why does that interpretation follow?”**

Do not ask only:

> “Has this doctrine been taught for a long time?”

Ask:

> **“Is the doctrine true?”**

These questions do not weaken faith.

They protect truth from being confused with the human processes by which people encounter, transmit, interpret, or defend claims about truth.

---

# 17. Chapter Conclusion

Truth and reality cannot be separated.

Reality concerns **what actually is**.

Truth concerns the accurate correspondence of a proposition to what actually is.

Perception may apprehend reality, but perception is not reality.

Opinion may describe reality, but opinion is not reality.

Interpretation may explain a text, but interpretation is not the text.

Testimony may report an event, but testimony is not the event itself.

A written record may preserve testimony, but the record must still be examined as a representation of what it claims to record.

These distinctions create the necessary bridge from ontology to epistemology.

The next question is therefore unavoidable:

> **If reality exists independently of our opinions about it, how can human beings distinguish truth from error and know that their conclusions correspond to reality?**

That is the subject of **Chapter 3 — Truth and Error**.

# Authoring / Guardrail Notes

### 1. Do not collapse ontology into epistemology.

The question **“What is real?”** is not identical to the question **“How do I know what is real?”**

### 2. Do not collapse evidence into reality.

Evidence is something that bears upon a conclusion concerning reality. Evidence is not itself the reality being investigated.

### 3. Do not collapse testimony into the event testified about.

Eyewitness testimony is important precisely because it purports to connect the investigator with an event outside the testimony itself.

### 4. Do not collapse interpretation into the authoritative text.

A reader's interpretation may be correct or incorrect. The authority of a text does not transfer automatically to every interpretation of it.

### 5. Do not introduce the Bible's final authority prematurely.

This chapter prepares the conceptual conditions under which the later authentication of the Bible can be rationally examined. It does not assume the conclusion it is later required to establish.

### 6. Preserve the master formula.

> **Claim → Definition → Evidence → Analysis → Inference → Conclusion → Consequence**

### 7. Preserve the master standard.

> **Do not merely tell the reader what to conclude.**

> **Give the reader the definitions, evidence, biblical record, reasoning, distinctions, and inferential steps necessary to see why the conclusion follows.**

### 8. Preserve the central intellectual discipline.

> **Do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture.**

> **Teach the reader to reason through Scripture.**
