# 60.3 Define the Question: The Actual Citation and Psalm 82 Context

**The actual citation and its Psalm 82 context**, in this chapter, is the written wording Christ quotes — “I said, Ye are gods” — examined as Psalm 82:6 inside the argument of Psalm 82 as a whole, together with the reason He uses that wording as written rather than a paraphrase, not as an isolated shock-phrase, not as a later metaphysical system, and not as the whole of Movement VIII.

The phrase is easily abused. In ordinary speech “Ye are gods” may mean a slogan of deification, a curiosity about Hebrew vocabulary, a weapon in later debates about identity, or a license to treat every later Graphē-doctrine as already settled because a psalm exists. None of those senses is this chapter’s object.

Four elements belong to the definition.

First, **the actual citation**. The wording He cites is not a theme, not a mood, and not a later summary. It is the sentence already standing in the psalm: “I have said, Ye are gods.” Citation is quotation of what is written. A paraphrase that reports the psalm’s “gist” is not the citation. A later expansion that adds what the psalm does not say is not the citation.

Second, **in its Psalm 82 context**. Verse 6 is not a detached proverb. It stands after God has taken His place as judge among those called gods, after the charge of unjust judgment, after the command to defend the poor, and before the sentence that they shall die like men. Context is the psalm’s argument. A fragment is not the psalm.

Third, **why He uses the written wording**. The assignment is not merely to display the psalm. It is to reason through why the case in John 10 requires this wording as written. The reason will be that the charge concerns the vocabulary of God, that the writing already uses that vocabulary of those to whom the word of God came, and that a paraphrase which avoided or inflated the words would weaken the court He has opened.

Fourth, **not those other claims**. The definition excludes, as not this object: the dedicated logic from the wording to the Son-claim as Chapter 61’s finished file; the finished sense of “broken”; the dedicated why; John 17:17 as finished Graphē-doctrine; the Graphē-gate; a retry of Chapter 59’s Dedication-file; a blank check for later speculation that every use of “gods” means what a later system wishes. Those exclusions are part of the definition. They are not a later apology.

Thus:

> **The question of this chapter is not whether John 10:34–36 is an argument from writing, whether the scripture cannot be broken, or how the a fortiori to the Son is drawn in full. Those questions have been examined or remain later. The question is what Psalm 82:6 is in Psalm 82, to whom the address is directed, and why the written wording is the material Christ uses.**

# 60.7 The Actual Citation Defined

**The actual citation**, in this chapter, is the written wording Christ quotes as already standing in their law, identified as Psalm 82:6, given in the words the writing uses rather than in a later summary of what the writing is “about.”

John 10:34:

> **“Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?”**

Psalm 82:6:

> **“I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.”**

The citation is the first clause of the psalm-verse, in the form He quotes. The remainder of the verse belongs to the psalm’s context and will be examined with it. The citation is not less than the quoted words. It is not more than the quoted words plus the psalm that contains them.

Three false substitutes must be refused.

**A theme.** “The psalm is about judgment” may be true as a summary and still not be the citation. He does not ask whether a theme is in their law. He asks whether this wording is written.

**A later doctrinal expansion.** “Ye are gods” does not, by being cited, become a completed metaphysics of deification, a completed anthropology of image, or a completed Christology of the Son. Those expansions, if they come, must come by later argument. They do not come by the mere fact of quotation.

**A reconstructed original setting offered as the citation.** A later reconstruction of a court-scene, a festival, or a heavenly council may be offered as history. It is not the wording. The wording is what is written. Unverifiable reconstruction is refused as a substitute for the text.

The Greek of John 10:34, ἐγὼ εἶπα θεοί ἐστε, corresponds to the wording of the Greek Psalter at this verse. The correspondence is named as correspondence of written wording, not as an invented account of which physical copy He held. The argument depends upon what is written, not upon a reconstructed book-object in the porch.

Thus:

> **The actual citation is the written wording “I said, Ye are gods,” as Psalm 82:6 already stands. A theme, a later expansion, and an invented setting are not the citation.**

# 60.8 Psalm 82 Context Defined

**Psalm 82 context**, in this chapter, is the argument of the psalm as a whole: speaker, addressees, charge, command, address of verse 6, mortality-sentence, and closing appeal — the frame in which verse 6 is an address and not a proverb torn out of a book.

A psalm-context is not a caption. It is the sequence of sentences that makes verse 6 do the work it does. Remove verses 1–5, and “Ye are gods” has no judicial charge behind it. Remove verse 7, and the address has no mortality to qualify it. Remove verse 8, and the psalm has no appeal from failed human judgment to God’s judgment of the earth. The relevant text is therefore the psalm, not the fragment.

Context so defined is not a historical novel of Asaph’s career. The superscription names “A Psalm of Asaph.” The name locates the writing among the psalms so ascribed. It does not license a biography beyond the record. The investigation follows the sentences.

Two false substitutes must be refused.

**Immediate verbal neighbourhood without the argument.** One may quote verse 6 and call verses 5 and 7 “context” while still treating verse 6 as a slogan. Neighbourhood of words is not yet argumentative context. Argumentative context is the psalm’s case: God judging those who judge.

**A later controversy offered as context.** Later readers have disputed whether the “gods” of verse 1 are heavenly beings or human judges. That dispute may be noted as a later reading. It is not the psalm’s context. The psalm’s context is the psalm’s own verbs, addressees, and outcome. Christ’s characterization in John 10:35 is then the governing identification of those addressees for the citation’s use: those to whom the word of God came.

Thus:

> **Psalm 82 context is the psalm’s judicial argument as a whole. It is not a slogan’s caption, not invented colour, and not a later quarrel substituted for the text.**

# 60.9 Why Written Wording Defined

**Why Christ uses the written wording**, in this chapter, is the inspectable reason the case requires the words as they stand written, rather than a paraphrase, a theme, or a later restatement that would weaken or relocate the argument.

The definition has four elements.

First, **as written**. γεγραμμένον has already been established in Chapter 59 as the category of the appeal. The present file asks what follows for this sentence: the force is the words, not a speaker’s later equivalent.

Second, **rather than a paraphrase**. A paraphrase might say, “Scripture honours judges,” or “men have a high office,” or “God once spoke to Israel.” Each of those sentences might be related to the psalm. Fourth, **not the dedicated a fortiori**. Why this wording is used is not yet how the inference from this wording to “I am the Son of God” is drawn in full. The first is this file. The second is Chapter 61. The two are neighbours. They are not the same assignment.

Thus:

> **Why He uses the written wording is why the case requires the words as written rather than a paraphrase that would weaken the court. It is not yet Chapter 61’s dedicated logic.**

# 60.10 Lexical Control: אֱלֹהִים, εἶπα, θεοί

The psalm’s and the citation’s God-terms must be controlled, not inflated.

**אֱלֹהִים** (*elohim*, H430) is a plural form whose biblical usage includes the true God, false gods, and, in specified judicial contexts, those who sit in judgment. Lexical range is not contextual meaning. The word does not, by itself, prove deification, polytheism, or a later metaphysical system. Context must decide which use is in force in Psalm 82. The psalm itself uses the word of God who stands to judge (verse 1a) and of those among whom He judges (verse 1b; verse 6). The same form therefore appears in more than one role inside one psalm. That fact is not a license to collapse the Most High into the addressees, or the addressees into the Most High.

**אָמַרְתִּי** is the first-person perfect of אָמַר (*amar*, H559): I have said. Verse 6 presents the address as already spoken. The English of John 10:34, “I said,” and of Psalm 82:6, “I have said,” names the same completed speech. The citation is a citation of a saying already in force as said.

**θεοί** is the plural of θεός (*theos*, G2316): gods. John 10:34 quotes θεοί ἐστε — “ye are gods.” The Greek corresponds to the psalm’s אֱלֹהִים as rendered in the Greek Psalter. The term in the citation is the psalm’s term in Greek dress. It is not a new vocabulary invented for the porch.

Three refusals follow.

The terms do not, by themselves, finish Chapter 61. A named address may be located. How the Son’s argument uses that address remains later.

The terms do not, by themselves, finish “cannot be broken.” A named writing may be cited. Why it cannot be broken remains later.

The terms do not license a claim that every biblical use of אֱלֹהִים means the same thing. Context controls. Here the context is a psalm in which God judges those He has called gods, who judge unjustly, and who shall die like men.

Thus:

> **אֱלֹהִים and θεοί name, in this psalm and this citation, an address whose sense is controlled by the psalm’s judicial argument and by Christ’s characterization, not by an isolated lexicon-entry.**

# 60.11 Lexical Control of Neighbouring Terms Named, Not Exhausted

Other terms in the psalm must be named accurately and left, where the TOC requires, to later files or to their proper force here.

**אֵל** (*El*, H410) appears in verse 1 in the phrase commonly rendered “congregation of the mighty”: עֲדַת־אֵל, the congregation of God / El. The phrase locates the assembly as God’s assembly. It does not, by itself, settle a later quarrel about whether the assembly is heavenly or earthly. The verbs that follow will.

**עֶלְיוֹן** (*Elyon*, H5945), Most High, appears in verse 6: “children of the most High.” The title distinguishes the addressees from the One whose children they are called. Children of the Most High are not the Most High. The distinction belongs to the verse and will govern later refusals of blank-check speculation.

**שָׁפַט** (*shaphat*, H8199), to judge, is the psalm’s governing verb: He judgeth (verse 1); how long will ye judge unjustly (verse 2); do justice (verse 3); arise, O God, judge the earth (verse 8). Judicial action is not ornament. It is the psalm’s work.

**מוּת** (*muth*, H4191) underlies verse 7: “ye shall die like men.” Mortality is the psalm’s own qualification of the address in verse 6. Those called gods shall die like men. The verb forbids treating verse 6 as an ontological promotion that has no death on the other side of it.

**λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ** in John 10:35: “the word of God” came to those who were called gods. Chapter 59 named the phrase as part of the unit and reserved its mapping onto the psalm. This chapter performs that mapping so far as the psalm’s “I have said” and the addressees’ judicial commission require. It does not convert the phrase into a second inspiration-file.

**λυθῆναι** remains reserved. The unit contains “cannot be broken.” This chapter records that the writing now examined is the writing of which that clause is said. It does not define “broken.” It does not answer why.

Thus:

> **Neighbouring terms are named so the psalm is not mistranslated. Judge, Most High, die like men, and the word of God locate the frame. “Broken” remains quoted as belonging to the unit and reserved as a dedicated object.**

# 60.12 The Psalm Given in Full

The relevant text must be given as a complete argument, not as a proof-text fragment.

> **A Psalm of Asaph.**
>
> **God standeth in the congregation of the mighty; he judgeth among the gods.**
>
> **How long will ye judge unjustly, and accept the persons of the wicked? Selah.**

>

The eight verses are one psalm. The first sentence locates God as judge among those called gods. The next sentences charge, command, and diagnose. Verse 6 is the address already spoken. Verse 7 qualifies that address with mortality. Verse 8 appeals from the failed court to God’s judgment of the earth.

The argumentative force of the whole, at this stage, can be stated without stealing later files.

God stands. He does not leave judgment unattended.

He judges among gods. The addressees are already in a judicial frame before verse 6 is spoken.

He charges unjust judgment. The “gods” are not being flattered. They are being indicted.

He commands defense of the poor. Office is measured by justice to the weak, not by a title.

He diagnoses ignorance and darkness. The foundations are out of course. The crisis is judicial and moral, not a seminar about vocabulary.

He recalls what He has said: Ye are gods; children of the Most High. The address is prior speech, now cited inside the indictment.

He declares they shall die like men. The title does not confer immunity.

He appeals to God to judge the earth. Failed human judgment is not the last judgment.

That is the psalm in full argumentative force. A later preacher may quote only “Ye are gods.” A later controversy may quote only a theory of gods. Neither quotation is the argument. The argument is the psalm as given.

Thus:

> **The relevant text is Psalm 82 as a whole: God judging those called gods, charging unjust judgment, addressing them as gods and children of the Most High, declaring their death like men, and appealing to God to judge the earth. Fragmentation is not exposition.**

# 60.13 Superscription and Speaker: A Psalm of Asaph

The writing is superscribed “A Psalm of Asaph.”

The superscription locates the psalm among the Asaph psalms. It does not, in this chapter, become a biography of Asaph, a reconstructed liturgy, or an unverifiable account of first performance. The investigation takes the psalm as a writing already standing in the received Psalter, which Chapter 48 has already shown Christ to receive among the Scriptures, and which Chapter 59 has already shown Him to cite as written in their law.

The speaker of verse 1 is the psalm’s narrator presenting God as standing and judging. The speaker of verses 2–4 is God addressing the addressees. Verse 5 diagnoses their condition. Verse 6 is God’s prior address recalled: “I have said.” Verse 7 continues the divine sentence. Verse 8 is the psalm’s appeal to God.

“I have said” in verse 6 is therefore not a later commentator’s gloss. It is the psalm’s own presentation of a saying of God already in force. When Christ quotes “I said, Ye are gods,” He quotes that saying as written. He does not invent a speaker. He does not transfer the saying to a private voice of His own as if the psalm had never spoken.

The Bible Introduction’s larger argument that the received writings were already treated as Scripture in Christ’s scene is used here only as already-established correspondence: a psalm is cited as law. Correspondence is not a second confirmation-trial, and not a second canon-file.

Thus:

> **The psalm stands as an Asaph psalm in the received Psalter. “I have said” is God’s prior address as the psalm presents it. Location of the writing is not a reconstructed career of Asaph.**

# 60.14 Verse 1: God Standing in the Congregation, Judging Among the Gods

Verse 1 opens the frame.

> **“God standeth in the congregation of the mighty; he judgeth among the gods.”**

Two acts are named.

**He stands.** God takes His place in the assembly. The congregation is not unsupervised. Judgment is not left to those who happen to sit.

**He judges among the gods.** The addressees of the psalm are already called gods before verse 6 recalls the saying. The title, whatever its force, is already in the frame as the designation of those among whom God judges.

The verse therefore forbids two opposite errors.

**Error of flattening.** Flattening says that “gods” in verse 1a and “gods” in verse 1b must mean the same referent in the same role, so that God is judging Himself, or so that there is no Most High distinct from the assembly. The verse distinguishes the One who stands and judges from those among whom He judges.

**Error of detaching verse 6.** Detaching says that “Ye are gods” is a sudden metaphysical announcement. Verse 1 has already placed “gods” in a judicial assembly. Verse 6 will recall what has been said of those who sit there.

Later readers have asked whether the assembly is heavenly or earthly. The question may be named. It is not this chapter’s center. The verse’s own contribution is already full: God judges in an assembly of those called gods. Verses 2–7 will show what those addressees do, what they fail to do, and how they die. Christ’s own characterization will then identify them, for the citation’s use, as those to whom the word of God came.

Thus:

> **Verse 1 locates God as judge in an assembly of those called gods. The frame is judicial before verse 6 is recalled. A later quarrel about the assembly’s metaphysics is not the verse’s assignment.**

---

# 60.15 Verses 2–4: The Judicial Charge

Verses 2–4 are the indictment and the command.

> **“How long will ye judge unjustly, and accept the persons of the wicked? Selah.”**
> — Psalm 82:2

> **“Defend the poor and fatherless: do justice to the afflicted and needy. Deliver the poor and needy: rid them out of the hand of the wicked.”**
> — Psalm 82:3–4

The second-person verbs are judicial verbs. “How long will ye judge unjustly” names their office as judging and their failure as injustice. “Accept the persons of the wicked” names partiality. The commands that follow — defend, do justice, deliver, rid — are the measures of that office. The poor, the fatherless, the afflicted, and the needy are the persons by whom the court is tested.

These verses establish, with full force, that the addressees function as judges. Whatever else a later system may wish to say about “gods,” the psalm’s own sentences address them as those who judge, who can judge unjustly, and who are commanded to do justice to the weak. A reading that cannot account for unjust judgment, partiality, and the defense of the fatherless has not yet read the psalm.

The charge also establishes that the title in verse 6 is not a compliment that ends the case. The saying “Ye are gods” will be recalled inside an indictment, not as an escape from it. Office under God is compatible with failure before God. The psalm is about that compatibility, and about its consequence in verse 7.

Thus:

> **Verses 2–4 address the “gods” as judges: they judge unjustly, they are commanded to defend the poor. The judicial frame is the psalm’s own, not a later imposition.**

---

# 60.16 Verse 5: Ignorance, Darkness, Foundations Out of Course

Verse 5 diagnoses the condition of those so addressed.

> **“They know not, neither will they understand; they walk on in darkness: all the foundations of the earth are out of course.”**

Not knowing and not understanding are not compliments. Walking in darkness is not a mystical ascent. The foundations of the earth out of course is the psalm’s own measure of how serious unjust judgment is. The crisis is not a private mood. It is a disorder of the foundations.

The verse matters for the citation because it forbids treating “Ye are gods” as a statement of present competence. Those called gods, in this psalm, do not know, do not understand, and walk in darkness. The address in verse 6 is therefore not the psalm’s last word about them. It is a prior saying now set beside their failure.

Christ’s later characterization — those to whom the word of God came — is compatible with this diagnosis. The word may come, and the hearers may still not know. Receiving the word as office is not the same act as walking in the light of it. The psalm records both the coming of the address and the darkness of those addressed. Chapter 61 will use that structure so far as the dedicated reasoning requires. This chapter needs only the diagnosis: verse 6 is not a certificate of present enlightenment.

Thus:

> **Verse 5 diagnoses those called gods as not knowing, not understanding, walking in darkness. The address of verse 6 is recalled inside failure, not as a slogan of present competence.**

---

# 60.17 Verse 6: The Address — “I Have Said, Ye Are Gods”

Verse 6 is the cited wording in its place.

> **“I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.”**

Four features of the sentence must be seen.

**It is prior speech.** “I have said” presents the address as already spoken. The psalm does not now invent the title. It recalls it. Christ’s “I said” is that recall quoted as written.

**It is second-person address.** “Ye are gods” is spoken to the addressees of verses 2–4. It is not a general proverb about the universe. It is not an abstract definition of deity. It is “ye.”

**It is the vocabulary of gods.** The word is the word. The investigation does not replace it with a weaker English substitute in order to spare later embarrassment, and it does not inflate it into a later system in order to satisfy later appetite. The writing says gods. The writing must be allowed to say it.

**It is paired with sonship-language.** “Children of the most High” stands in the same verse. The addressees are called gods and called children of Elyon. The pairing will be examined in the next section. It already shows that the verse itself supplies a relation to the Most High rather than an identity with the Most High.

At this stage, the verse’s contribution to the citation is already full. This is the wording. This is its place: after indictment, before mortality. This is its form: prior divine speech to those who judge. Chapter 59 named the verse. This section locates it inside the psalm. Location inside the psalm is the difference between a lemma and a citation in context.

Thus:

> **Verse 6 is God’s prior address to the psalm’s addressees: Ye are gods; children of the Most High. The citation is this wording in this place, not a detached shock-phrase.**

---

# 60.18 “And All of You Are Children of the Most High”

The second clause of verse 6 must not be dropped, and it must not be allowed to steal the file.

> **“…and all of you are children of the most High.”**

**What the clause is.** It is a parallel address. Those called gods are also called children of Elyon. “All of you” distributes the address across the assembly, not to one isolated figure.

**What the clause does.** It relates the addressees to the Most High as children to Father, not as rivals who have become the Most High. Sonship-language in Scripture can name Israel, can name kings, can name those who belong to God. The present verse uses it of the same persons who judge unjustly and who shall die like men. The clause therefore belongs to office and relation under God, not to an escape from God.

**What the clause does not do here.** It does not finish a doctrine of adoption. It does not finish a doctrine of the image of God. It does not finish the Son’s unique relation to the Father, already examined as identity and claim in Chapters 44–45 and applied in John 10:36. It does not become a later identity-lesson that every hearer is already “gods” in a sense that would make the blasphemy-charge unintelligible.

Genesis 1:26–27 records that God made man in His image, after His likeness. That record stands. A later leaflet-argument that places Psalm 82:6 beside Genesis 1:26 as language of man’s true nature may be reconstructed as a proposed correspondence: children of the Most High and image-language both speak of relation to God. Correspondence is not identity of assignment. Genesis 1 is the making of man. Psalm 82 is the judging of those who judge. The image does not evacuate the indictment. The indictment does not deny that man was made in the image. The two writings remain two. This chapter’s object is the psalm’s address in its judicial frame.

Thus:

> **“Children of the most High” relates the addressees to God without identifying them as the Most High. Image-language may be named as correspondence. It may not convert Psalm 82 into a blank check.**

---

# 60.19 Verse 7: Mortality and Fall — They Shall Die Like Men

Verse 7 is the psalm’s own qualification of verse 6.

> **“But ye shall die like men, and fall like one of the princes.”**

The adversative is the psalm’s. “I have said, Ye are gods” is not left without a “but.” Those so addressed shall die like men. They shall fall like a prince. The title does not confer deathlessness. The office does not confer immunity.

This verse is decisive for the addressees. Whatever later quarrel may be raised about verse 1, verse 7 names their end in the language of human death and princely fall. The psalm itself therefore places the “gods” of verse 6 on the side of those who die as men die. A reading that cannot hear “die like men” as the psalm’s own check upon “Ye are gods” has not yet read the psalm.

The verse also matters for Christ’s use. He cites verse 6, not verse 7. He does not thereby cancel verse 7. He cites the address as written. The address, in the psalm, is the address of those who remain mortal. The persons to whom the word of God came were called gods, and they remain those who die. That fact belongs to the written case. It will belong to Chapter 61 so far as the dedicated reasoning compares their calling with the Father’s sanctifying and sending of the Son. This chapter needs the qualification as the psalm’s own: the wording “Ye are gods” is not, in Psalm 82, a sentence of immortal deity.

Thus:

> **Verse 7 qualifies verse 6: those called gods shall die like men. The citation of the address does not cancel the psalm’s mortality-sentence, and the mortality-sentence forbids treating the address as a grant of deathless deity.**

---

# 60.20 Verse 8: Appeal to God to Judge the Earth

Verse 8 closes the psalm.

> **“Arise, O God, judge the earth: for thou shalt inherit all nations.”**

Failed judgment in the assembly is not the last judgment. The psalm appeals to God to arise and judge the earth. The inheritance of the nations is assigned to Him. The Most High remains the judge of the whole, not merely the supervisor of a local court.

The closing matters for context because it shows what the psalm is for. It is not a hymn to the addressees. It is a cry from their failure to God’s judgment. “Ye are gods” is not the psalm’s destination. God’s judging the earth is the psalm’s destination.

Christ’s citation of verse 6 therefore takes a line from a psalm that ends by appealing past those who were called gods. He does not cite a psalm whose point is to make men into the Most High. He cites a psalm whose point is that God judges those who judge, and that God must yet judge the earth. The later application in John 10:36 to the One the Father sanctified and sent will have this psalm, not a rival psalm, as its written material. The dedicated application remains Chapter 61. The psalm’s destination is already this file’s to state.

Thus:

> **Verse 8 appeals from the failed court to God’s judgment of the earth. The psalm’s destination is God’s judging, not the addressees’ deification. That destination is part of the citation’s context.**

# 60.21 The Psalm’s Argument as a Whole

The verses now stand together as one argument.

God takes His place in the assembly and judges among those called gods (v. 1). He charges them with unjust judgment and partiality (v. 2). He commands justice for the poor, the fatherless, the afflicted, and the needy (vv. 3–4). He diagnoses their ignorance, darkness, and the disorder of the foundations (v. 5). He recalls His prior address: Ye are gods; children of the Most High (v. 6). He declares their death like men and their fall like princes (v. 7). He appeals to God to judge the earth and inherit the nations (v. 8).

The argument is therefore this:

> **Those who judge under God have been addressed as gods and as children of the Most High; they have judged unjustly; they remain mortal; God Himself must judge.**

That is the psalm’s case. Verse 6 is a member of the case. It is the prior address. It is not the whole case. A reading that treats verse 6 as the whole psalm has not read the psalm. A reading that treats the judicial verbs as optional colour around a metaphysical slogan has not read the psalm.

The argument as a whole is what Chapter 59 reserved when it named the psalm and refused to exposit it. The reservation is now performed. The psalm is a judicial psalm. The cited wording is the address inside that judicial psalm.

Thus:

> **Psalm 82’s argument is God’s judgment of those He has addressed as gods, who judge unjustly and shall die like men, with appeal to God to judge the earth. Verse 6 belongs to that argument and does not replace it.**

---

# 60.22 To Whom the Address Is Directed

The question of addressees must now be answered from the psalm’s own frame, not from a later appetite.

**In the psalm’s own sentences.** The addressees are those among whom God judges (v. 1); those who judge unjustly and accept the wicked (v. 2); those commanded to defend the poor and fatherless (vv. 3–4); those who do not know and walk in darkness (v. 5); those told they shall die like men and fall like princes (v. 7). The only description that fits all of those sentences without remainder is: those who exercise judgment under God and are accountable to Him for it.

**Judges**, in this argument, names that function. It does not require a reconstructed organigram of a particular court. It does not require an invented roster of names. It names what the verbs name: they judge. The poor and fatherless are the persons of their office. Death like men is their end. Princes are the comparison of their fall.

**Not the Most High.** Verse 6 calls them children of the Most High. Verse 8 calls God to arise and judge the earth. The addressees are not the One who inherits the nations.

**Not a blank “everyone.”** “All of you” in verse 6 is all of the assembly addressed, not all later readers of the psalm in every sense a later teacher may wish. The address has a “ye.” The “ye” is the court that has failed.

The chapter does not invent a modern controversy as the center of this identification. Later readers who prefer a heavenly council as verse 1’s assembly must still account for unjust judging of the poor, for dying like men, and for Christ’s own words. The psalm’s verbs and the citation’s characterization govern. A later preference does not.

Thus:

> **The address “Ye are gods” is directed, in the psalm’s own frame, to those who judge under God — the assembly God judges, who fail the poor, who shall die like men — not to the Most High, and not to an unbounded later “everyone.”**

# 60.23 Christ’s Identification: Those to Whom the Word of God Came

John 10:35 supplies the governing identification of the cited persons for the argument in which the citation is used.

> **“If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came…”**

Chapter 59 established that this clause treats the citation as a written case: persons to whom the word of God came were called gods. This chapter maps that characterization onto the psalm.

**The mapping is inspectable.** Psalm 82:6 is God’s saying: “I have said.” A saying of God is the word of God coming to those addressed. The addressees of verses 2–4 are the “ye” of that saying. Christ names them as those to whom the word of God came. He does not name a different cast. He names the psalm’s addressees by the psalm’s own act: the word came; they were called gods.

**The mapping is not a second psalm.** He does not replace Psalm 82 with a new narrative. He reads the psalm as a case of the word of God coming. “Came” is the arrival of that word to them. The perfect of “I have said” is that arrival as already accomplished speech.

**The mapping governs later speculation.** If a later system wishes to make the “gods” of verse 6 a class to whom no word of God came, it has already left Christ’s identification. If a later system wishes to make them a class for whom “gods” means the Most High Himself, it has already left both the psalm’s “children of the most High” and Christ’s distinction between those called gods and the One the Father sanctified and sent.

The identification is therefore the psalm and the citation together, not the psalm against the citation, and not the citation against the psalm. Those to whom the word of God came are the judges of the psalm’s frame, addressed as gods by God’s prior speech.

Thus:

> **Christ identifies the cited persons as those to whom the word of God came. That identification maps onto Psalm 82’s “I have said” addressed to those who judge. The mapping is the citation’s own reading of the psalm, not a rival psalm.**

# 60.24 Judges in the Biblical Record: Elohim in Judicial Function

The psalm does not stand as a unique linguistic accident. Other writings already use אֱלֹהִים in judicial settings. Those writings are correspondence, not a second object, and not a reconstructed history of a particular court.

Exodus 21:6 and 22:8–9 bring parties “unto the judges,” where the Hebrew uses אֱלֹהִים. Exodus 22:28 joins “the gods” and “the ruler of thy people” in a prohibition of reviling. Deuteronomy 1:16–17 charges judges to hear causes and not to respect persons, “for the judgment is God’s.” 2 Chronicles 19:6–7 charges judges to take heed, “for ye judge not for man, but for the LORD, who is with you in the judgment.”

The correspondence is this: in the received writings, those who sit in judgment may be named with the God-word because judgment is God’s, and they judge under Him. The name is office under God, not identity as the Most High. The charge in Deuteronomy and Chronicles is the same species of charge as Psalm 82:2–4: judge righteously; do not respect persons.

This correspondence does not prove, by itself, every later theory of the psalm. It does establish that reading Psalm 82’s addressees as judges is not an imposition from outside the corpus. It is a reading the corpus already makes available by its own usage. Christ’s “word of God came” then names the commission: the word that constitutes them in that office has come.

The correspondence is used at proportional force. It is not a catalogue of every court in Israel. It is not an invented reconstruction of the Sanhedrin as if John 10 required that organigram. It is biblical usage illuminating biblical usage.

Thus:

> **Biblical usage already names judges with אֱלֹהִים because judgment is God’s. Psalm 82’s judicial addressees stand inside that usage. Correspondence is not a reconstructed court-history, and it is not the Most High flattened into the bench.**

# 60.25 The Word of God Coming: Commission, Not Deification

“The word of God came” must be given a precise sense, neither too thin nor too thick.

Too thin: the phrase means only that a copy of a psalm existed. Existence of a writing is already γεγραμμένον. “Came” names arrival to persons. The word reached them as address.

Too thick: the phrase means that those persons became the Most High, or became ontologically what the Son is. The psalm forbids this by “children of the most High” and by “die like men.” John 10 forbids this by distinguishing those so called from the One the Father sanctified and sent.

The warranted sense at this level is **commissioning address**. God has spoken to them. He has called them gods. He has called them children of the Most High. That speech locates them in office under Him: they judge; they are accountable; they remain men who die. The word coming is the arrival of that address, not a transformation into deathless deity.

The Aletheia leaflet’s placement of Psalm 82:6 beside Genesis 1:26 as language of man’s true nature may be reconstructed without being adopted as this chapter’s center. The reconstruction is: if man is made in God’s image, sonship-language is not foreign to the corpus. The limit is: Psalm 82 is not Genesis 1, and John 10 is not a later identity-lesson. Commission is not deification. Image is not a grant to evacuate the indictment. The word coming is the psalm’s “I have said,” not a later slogan that the hearer is already what the Most High is.

Thus:

> **“The word of God came” names commissioning address to those who judge under God. It is not the mere existence of a book, and it is not deification. Commission remains commission.**

# 60.27 Why the Written Wording Matters: Citation, Not Paraphrase

The assignment’s second sentence can now be performed with full force.

Christ uses the written wording because the case is a case about words already standing, not a case about a speaker’s later equivalent.

The blasphemy-charge has named God. “Thou, being a man, makest thyself God.” The dispute’s vocabulary is already the God-vocabulary. He does not change the subject. He asks whether it is written, “I said, Ye are gods.” The writing already uses that vocabulary of those to whom the word of God came. The court therefore already contains the word the charge has used.

If He had paraphrased, He would have left the court without the exhibit. A paraphrase might be true as far as it went and still not be what is written. The appeal’s first move is γεγραμμένον. What is written is the wording. The wording is “Ye are gods.” That is why the wording matters: not because the chapter is about gods as a topic, but because the writing’s words are the material of a writing-argument.

Chapter 48 already established that He treats wording as having force, and that “it is written again” may judge a use of Scripture by Scripture. This scene is that practice under a capital charge. The present file’s contribution is not a second confirmation-trial. It is the reason this psalm’s words, as words, are the words He uses: they are the standing address the charge has made relevant, and they are written.

Thus:

> **He cites what is written, not a paraphrase, because a writing-argument requires the words as the exhibit. The God-vocabulary of the charge is already in the psalm as address to those who received God’s word.**

# 60.28 What a Paraphrase Would Weaken

The reason can be shown by contrast. The contrast is not a historical claim that He considered and rejected drafts. It is a rational account of why the wording as written is the wording the case needs.

**A paraphrase that avoided “gods.”** “Scripture honours those who judge.” The sentence might be related to the psalm. It would not place the word “gods” in the court. The charge is about making Himself God. A court without the word is a weaker court for that charge.

**A paraphrase that inflated “gods.”** “Scripture teaches that men are the Most High.” The psalm does not teach that.
