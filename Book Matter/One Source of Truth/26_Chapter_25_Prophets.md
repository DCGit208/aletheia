# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 25 — Prophets

> **Movement IV — How the Written Record Was Given**

---

## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 23 established that **God speaks**.

Chapter 24 established that God communicates not only directly but also through appointed mediators, including angels.

The investigation now turns specifically to the **prophetic mediator**.

The Bible does not present the prophet merely as a religious personality, poet, moral teacher, or predictor of future events. The prophetic office belongs to the biblical account of how God communicates with human beings in history.

The central question is therefore:

> **What does the biblical record establish concerning prophets as recipients, proclaimers, witnesses, and transmitters of divine communication?**

This chapter must establish that architecture without prematurely consuming the later chapters concerning Christ, apostles, visions and dreams, revelation, inspiration, authentication, or canon.

---

# Central Objective

> **To establish the biblical nature and function of the prophet as a human agent through whom God communicates, and to trace the prophetic communication from divine calling and reception through proclamation, testimony, and, where applicable, written preservation.**

The chapter will establish:

1. what the biblical concept of a prophet entails;
2. the relationship between God and the prophet;
3. prophetic calling and commission;
4. prophetic reception of divine communication;
5. prophetic proclamation;
6. the distinction between prophecy and prediction;
7. the relationship between prophetic speech and prophetic writing;
8. the historical breadth of the prophetic witness;
9. the biblical distinction between true and false prophets;
10. and why prophetic mediation is an important part of the path from divine Source to written testimony.

---

# 25.1 The Prophet in the Architecture of Divine Communication

The preceding chapter established:

> **God → mediator → recipient**

Chapter 25 now specifies the principal human mediator within this pattern:

> **God → prophet → people**

The prophet stands within a communicative chain.

God is the Source.

The prophet receives and declares.

The people hear.

The message enters history.

Where the communication is committed to writing, it becomes part of the preserved written testimony.

The prophet therefore belongs to the history of revelation, not merely to the history of religion.

---

# 25.2 What Is a Prophet?

The English word **prophet** comes through the Greek *prophētēs* (προφήτης), the principal New Testament term for a prophet.

Its biblical use must not be reduced to the popular modern definition:

> **“Someone who predicts the future.”**

Prediction can be prophetic.

But prophecy is broader.

A prophet may:

- announce God's will;
- rebuke sin;
- call people to repentance;
- explain covenant obligations;
- warn of judgment;
- proclaim God's purposes;
- encourage God's people;
- interpret historical circumstances;
- declare what God has revealed;
- and, where God gives such revelation, announce future events.

The defining feature is therefore not prediction.

It is **divinely commissioned proclamation**.

---

# 25.3 The Prophet Is a Spokesman

The prophet's function can be understood through the recurring biblical pattern:

> **God speaks → the prophet receives → the prophet speaks to the people.**

This explains why prophetic language so frequently includes formulas such as:

> **“Thus saith the LORD.”**

The prophet is identifying the source of the message.

The claim is not:

> “This is merely my opinion about God.”

The claim is:

> **“The LORD has spoken, and I am declaring what He has given me to declare.”**

The prophetic office is therefore fundamentally representative.

The prophet represents the divine message before the human audience.

---

# 25.4 Moses and the Prophetic Pattern

Moses provides one of the foundational examples.

God calls Moses.

God communicates with Moses.

Moses receives God's commands.

Moses speaks to Israel.

Moses records divine instruction.

The Pentateuch therefore provides an early and extensive example of divine communication entering human history through a commissioned human agent.

The significance is not that Moses ceased to be human.

He remained a historical person with a language, personality, circumstances, memory, and physical existence.

Rather:

> **God used a genuine human agent to communicate His revealed will to other human beings.**

This is precisely the divine-human communicative pattern established in the preceding chapters.

---

# 25.5 Jeremiah — “Whatsoever I Command Thee”

Jeremiah 1 presents the prophetic calling with exceptional clarity.

The LORD tells Jeremiah that He knew him before his formation in the womb, sanctified him, and appointed him as a prophet to the nations.

Jeremiah responds to the commission by identifying his youth and inadequacy.

The LORD then answers by commanding him to go where He sends him and speak what He commands.

The structure is unmistakable:

> **Divine appointment**
>
> ↓
>
> **Divine command**
>
> ↓
>
> **Human prophet**
>
> ↓
>
> **Human audience**

The prophet is therefore not self-appointed.

The prophetic office originates in divine commission.

---

# 25.6 “Behold, I Have Put My Words in Thy Mouth”

Jeremiah 1:9 contains a particularly important formulation:

> **“Behold, I have put my words in thy mouth.”**

The statement directly joins:

- God;
- words;
- the prophet;
- and proclamation.

The prophet's mouth is human.

The words are represented as coming from God.

This is one of the clearest biblical expressions of the divine Source / human messenger distinction.

The conclusion is not that Jeremiah became a mechanical instrument without personality.

The conclusion is:

> **The prophetic message is presented as originating in God's communicative initiative.**

---

# 25.7 Ezekiel — Receiving and Speaking

Ezekiel provides another major example.

His prophetic ministry includes visions, divine speech, symbolic actions, commands, and proclamations.

Again the sequence is:

> **God communicates**
>
> → **Ezekiel receives**
>
> → **Ezekiel is commanded to speak**
>
> → **the people receive the message**

The prophetic office therefore includes both **reception** and **communication**.

The prophet is not merely a passive recipient.

He becomes an active historical participant in delivering what he has received.

---

# 25.8 Isaiah — “Whom Shall I Send?”

Isaiah 6 provides another dimension of prophetic calling.

Isaiah encounters the holiness of the LORD.

He recognizes his own uncleanness.

He is cleansed.

Then comes the divine question:

> **“Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?”**

Isaiah responds:

> **“Here am I; send me.”**

The prophetic office therefore includes:

> **commission and obedience.**

The prophet is sent.

The prophetic message is not self-generated religious commentary.

It is connected to the One who sends.

---

# 25.9 The Prophet Is a Historical Witness

The prophetic office also has an important historical dimension.

Prophets lived at particular times.

They addressed identifiable peoples.

They confronted actual kings, nations, priests, social conditions, wars, injustices, covenant violations, and impending events.

Their messages therefore entered concrete history.

This matters because divine communication, once mediated through a prophet, is no longer merely an abstract proposition.

It becomes a historical claim:

> **God said this through this person at this time to these people.**

Such claims are capable of historical examination.

---

# 25.10 Prophecy Is Not Merely Prediction

A major conceptual correction is necessary.

If prophecy is defined only as prediction, much of the prophetic corpus becomes inexplicable.

The prophets:

- preached righteousness;
- condemned oppression;
- called Israel back to covenant faithfulness;
- warned of consequences;
- declared God's judgment;
- announced restoration;
- exposed false worship;
- addressed kings and nations;
- and revealed God's purposes.

Prediction is one prophetic function.

Proclamation is the broader category.

Therefore:

> **Prophecy concerns divinely commissioned speech, not prediction alone.**

---

# 25.11 Predictive Prophecy Within the Prophetic Office

This clarification does not weaken predictive prophecy.

It places it correctly.

When God reveals a future event to a prophet and the prophet announces it, the communication has predictive content.

The evidential question can later be asked:

> **Did the announced event correspond to what subsequently occurred?**

That question belongs to the broader cumulative authentication of the biblical record.

The prophetic office therefore provides both:

> **proclamation**

and, where given:

> **predictive revelation.**

---

# 25.12 “Thus Saith the LORD”

The recurring formula:

> **“Thus saith the LORD”**

deserves particular attention.

It performs an attribution.

The prophet identifies the speaker behind the message.

The formula therefore creates a distinction between:

> **prophet**

and:

> **Source.**

This is exactly why the biblical record also has to address false prophets.

If anyone could simply claim:

> “Thus saith the LORD”

and thereby establish divine authority, authentication would be unnecessary.

The Bible does not adopt that principle.

It gives criteria for distinguishing genuine from false claims.

---

# 25.13 False Prophets Confirm the Need for Authentication

The biblical existence of warnings against false prophets is significant.

It demonstrates that the biblical framework itself recognizes:

> **a difference between genuine divine communication and a false claim to divine communication.**

The category of false prophecy does not eliminate genuine prophecy.

It establishes the need for discernment.

This is analogous to the distinction between genuine testimony and false testimony.

The existence of false testimony makes testimony a category requiring evaluation; it does not make all testimony meaningless.

---

# 25.14 Deuteronomy and the Testing of Prophetic Claims

Deuteronomy provides explicit instruction concerning prophets who claim divine authority.

The biblical framework therefore does not say:

> **“Believe every person who claims to speak for God.”**

It requires testing.

This is intellectually important.

The prophetic claim contains at least two propositions:

1. **A person claims to have received a message from God.**
2. **The message itself claims divine origin.**

The second does not automatically prove the first.

The prophetic office therefore belongs within the larger authentication architecture of the book.

---

# 25.15 The Prophet's Authority Is Derivative

A genuine prophet does not possess autonomous divine authority.

The prophet's authority is derived from the One who commissions him.

The distinction is:

> **God — Source**
>
> **Prophet — commissioned spokesman**
>
> **People — recipients**

Therefore the prophet's authority does not originate in personality, social position, eloquence, education, or popularity.

It rests on divine commission.

---

# 25.16 Prophetic Authority and Obedience

The prophets frequently speak with authority precisely because they are under authority.

The paradox is important:

> **The prophet speaks boldly because the prophet does not speak independently.**

The prophet is accountable to the divine message.

This explains the severity of prophetic warnings against altering, suppressing, or falsifying God's word.

The messenger is not the owner of the message.

He is its commissioned bearer.

---

# 25.17 The Prophets Were Not a Single Generation

The prophetic witness spans centuries.

The biblical record contains prophetic figures associated with different periods of Israel's history.

The major prophetic witnesses include figures such as:

- Isaiah;
- Jeremiah;
- Ezekiel;
- Daniel;

alongside the prophets whose writings are traditionally grouped as the Minor Prophets:

- Hosea;
- Joel;
- Amos;
- Obadiah;
- Jonah;
- Micah;
- Nahum;
- Habakkuk;
- Zephaniah;
- Haggai;
- Zechariah;
- Malachi.

This historical distribution is significant.

The prophetic testimony is not the product of one isolated moment.

It develops across generations.

---

# 25.18 The Major and Minor Prophets

The terms **Major Prophets** and **Minor Prophets** are organizational terms.

“Major” does not mean more inspired.

“Minor” does not mean less authoritative.

The traditional distinction primarily reflects the relative size of the prophetic books.

This matters because terminology can otherwise create a false hierarchy.

The biblical prophetic corpus is a collection of prophetic witnesses distributed across history.

---

# 25.19 Prophets and Historical Circumstances

The prophetic books are deeply connected to historical circumstances.

Isaiah addresses Judah and Jerusalem within the political realities of his period.

Jeremiah ministers amid the final crisis of Judah and the Babylonian threat.

Ezekiel ministers among the exiles.

Daniel's prophetic ministry unfolds within the context of imperial kingdoms.

Haggai and Zechariah address the restoration period.

Malachi addresses a later post-exilic community.

The prophetic record therefore repeatedly intersects with recognizable historical settings.

That historical embeddedness is important for the cumulative evidential case.

---

# 25.20 Prophets and Kings

The prophetic ministry frequently intersects with kings.

Prophets confront:

- David;
- Ahab;
- Hezekiah;
- Josiah;
- and other rulers.

This demonstrates that prophetic authority was not simply institutional political authority.

A prophet could stand before a king and announce divine judgment.

The prophetic message therefore operates according to a higher claimed authority:

> **God's word over the ruler.**

---

# 25.21 Prophets and Nations

The prophetic corpus is not restricted to Israel's internal life.

Prophets speak concerning:

- Judah;
- Israel;
- surrounding nations;
- great empires;
- cities;
- rulers;
- and international events.

This broadens the historical scope of the prophetic record.

The prophetic claim is therefore not merely:

> **“God has something to say to my private religious community.”**

It often concerns nations and public history.

---

# 25.22 The Prophetic Message Enters Public History

A prophetic announcement made publicly becomes a public claim.

That means it can have:

- hearers;
- opponents;
- supporters;
- consequences;
- historical circumstances;
- transmission;
- and, in many cases, written preservation.

The prophetic ministry therefore contributes directly to the Bible's character as a historical record.

The message is not presented as secret information available only to the prophet.

It is proclaimed.

---

# 25.23 From Speech to Writing

The prophetic process does not end with spoken proclamation.

Some prophetic messages were committed to writing.

Habakkuk 2:2 provides a particularly clear example:

> **“Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables.”**

The sequence is:

> **revelation**
>
> → **prophetic reception**
>
> → **prophetic proclamation or recording**
>
> → **written preservation**

This distinction matters.

The original revelation and the later written record are related but conceptually distinguishable.

---

# 25.24 Jeremiah and the Written Scroll

Jeremiah 36 gives an especially valuable historical example.

Jeremiah receives the word of the LORD.

Baruch writes the words dictated or communicated through Jeremiah.

The scroll is then read publicly.

The king hears the contents.

The scroll is cut and burned.

Jeremiah then receives further communication and another scroll is produced.

This narrative is extraordinarily important for understanding the movement from:

> **divine communication**

to:

> **prophetic speech**

to:

> **writing**

to:

> **public reading**

to:

> **preservation and renewed writing.**

It demonstrates that the written record has a history.

---

# 25.25 Prophetic Writing Is Historical Activity

Jeremiah 36 also demonstrates that writing involves real human activity.

There is:

- a prophet;
- a scribe;
- writing materials;
- a scroll;
- dictation;
- reading;
- a royal audience;
- destruction;
- and renewed production.

The divine Source does not eliminate the historical process.

Rather:

> **divine communication enters history through genuine human activity.**

This is consistent with the divine-human character of the written testimony already established in Chapter 19.

---

# 25.26 The Scribe and the Prophet

The presence of Baruch in Jeremiah 36 introduces an important distinction.

The prophet may receive the message.

Another human agent may write it.

A further person may read it.

Others may hear it.

Therefore:

> **receiver, spokesman, writer, reader, and hearer can be distinct roles.**

This is essential when reconstructing how divine communication becomes written Scripture.

It prevents the simplistic assumption that every biblical writing must have been produced through one identical mechanism.

---

# 25.27 Prophetic Testimony and Memory

A prophetic message can also be remembered and transmitted.

Historical communication normally involves human memory, speech, repetition, writing, and preservation.

The prophetic corpus therefore has a transmission history.

This does not make it unreliable.

It makes it historical.

The relevant question becomes:

> **What evidence exists concerning the preservation and transmission of the prophetic testimony?**

That question belongs to the later treatment of textual transmission and authentication.

---

# 25.28 Prophets and Eyewitnesses

Prophets also demonstrate why the categories of **witness** and **prophet** should not be collapsed.

A prophet can witness an event.

A prophet can receive revelation.

A prophet can proclaim God's interpretation of an event.

A prophet can record what he has received.

These are related but distinct functions.

Therefore:

> **not every prophetic statement is eyewitness testimony, and not every prophetic witness is revelation of future events.**

The categories must remain precise.

---

# 25.29 The Prophet's Human Personality Remains Real

The prophets were not abstract voices detached from humanity.

They experienced:

- fear;
- grief;
- exhaustion;
- rejection;
- joy;
- frustration;
- compassion;
- courage;
- obedience.

Jeremiah's lamentations and personal struggles, for example, demonstrate a real human personality.

Yet the same prophet can declare God's word.

This is important because divine communication does not require the destruction of human agency.

---

# 25.30 Divine Message and Human Language

The prophet communicates through human language.

That language includes:

- grammar;
- vocabulary;
- imagery;
- metaphor;
- poetry;
- narrative;
- rhetorical questions;
- historical references;
- names;
- places;
- and cultural expressions.

The fact that the communication is expressed through human language does not make the source merely human.

It demonstrates the historical form in which the communication was delivered.

---

# 25.31 Prophetic Literature Is Therefore Literary

The prophetic books are not collections of disconnected divine noises.

They are written literature.

They contain:

- speeches;
- narratives;
- songs;
- visions;
- symbolic actions;
- poetry;
- judgments;
- promises;
- historical references;
- and theological declarations.

The human literary form belongs to the historical transmission of the prophetic message.

This observation prepares the later discussion of inspiration without prematurely defining the entire doctrine.

---

# 25.32 Prophetic Symbolism

Prophets sometimes communicate through symbolic acts and imagery.

Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Hosea, and other prophets contain examples.

A symbolic act is not identical to the interpretation of the act.

A vision is not identical to its written description.

An event is not identical to the prophetic explanation of the event.

These distinctions become important when later chapters examine visions, dreams, and revelation.

---

# 25.33 Prophetic Fulfillment

Some prophetic declarations concern events that occur later.

When an announced event corresponds with a later historical event, that correspondence becomes evidentially significant.

But the logic must be precise.

The existence of a prediction does not establish its divine origin merely because it sounds impressive.

The proper investigation considers:

- what was actually predicted;
- when it was predicted;
- what the historical context was;
- whether the text is securely dated;
- what event occurred;
- whether the correspondence is specific;
- and whether alternative explanations account for the correspondence.

Prophecy therefore becomes one component of cumulative authentication.

---

# 25.34 The Prophetic Corpus as a Long Historical Witness

The prophets contribute to the extraordinary temporal breadth of the biblical record.

The prophetic books extend across different centuries and historical crises.

They address changing political conditions while maintaining recurring themes concerning:

- God;
- covenant;
- righteousness;
- judgment;
- repentance;
- restoration;
- holiness;
- kingship;
- and God's purposes.

This creates a substantial historical and literary corpus.

Its significance is not merely numerical.

It is the cumulative witness of many historical agents across generations.

---

# 25.35 One Source, Many Prophetic Witnesses

The architecture can now be stated:

> **One divine Source**
>
> ↓
>
> **many commissioned prophets**
>
> ↓
>
> **many historical audiences**
>
> ↓
>
> **many prophetic messages**
>
> ↓
>
> **many written testimonies**
>
> ↓
>
> **one developing scriptural corpus**

The plurality of prophets does not imply plurality of ultimate divine sources.

It represents plurality of human instruments within the biblical claim of one divine communicative Source.

---

# 25.36 The Prophets and the Unity of Scripture

The prophetic corpus also contributes to the Bible's larger unity.

Different prophets speak in different historical circumstances.

Yet later biblical writers refer back to earlier prophetic writings.

Prophetic themes recur.

Later events are interpreted in relation to earlier revelation.

The result is not a series of unrelated religious documents.

The writings participate in a developing canonical history.

The full implications of this unity will be examined later.

---

# 25.37 Prophets and the Coming of Christ

The prophetic corpus also contains extensive material concerning the coming Messiah and the purposes associated with Him.

This is important but must be placed correctly.

Chapter 25 establishes the prophetic mechanism.

Chapter 26 will examine Christ as the climactic revelation identified by Hebrews 1:2.

The relationship is therefore:

> **prophetic witness → Christ**

rather than:

> **prophet = Christ.**

Christ's unique place must remain distinct.

---

# 25.38 Prophets Do Not Replace Christ

The prophet is a mediator.

Christ is not merely another prophet.

The distinction is already anticipated in Hebrews 1:1–2:

> God spoke in former times by the prophets.

> In these last days He has spoken by His Son.

The prophetic ministry therefore prepares the conceptual ground for Christ without exhausting His identity.

Chapter 26 will take up that decisive transition.

---

# 25.39 Prophets and Apostles Must Also Remain Distinct

Likewise, the prophets should not simply be merged with the apostles.

Prophets belong predominantly to the earlier covenantal and revelatory history.

The apostles are commissioned witnesses of Christ and His resurrection.

Some overlap in function can exist.

But the historical offices are not identical.

Chapter 27 will therefore examine apostolic testimony separately.

---

# 25.40 The Prophet Is a Link in the Evidential Chain

The prophetic office contributes to the broader evidential architecture:

> **God communicates**
>
> ↓
>
> **prophet receives**
>
> ↓
>
> **prophet proclaims**
>
> ↓
>
> **people hear**
>
> ↓
>
> **events occur**
>
> ↓
>
> **testimony is preserved**
>
> ↓
>
> **writing records the testimony**
>
> ↓
>
> **later generations examine the record**

The prophetic ministry therefore links revelation to history.

---

# 25.41 Why Prophetic Mediation Matters

The existence of prophetic mediation demonstrates that divine communication is capable of entering ordinary human history without becoming merely ordinary human invention.

God communicates.

A human being receives.

The human being speaks.

Other human beings hear.

The message can be written.

The record can be preserved.

The resulting Scripture therefore comes to us through an identifiable historical chain.

This is not an accidental feature of the Bible.

It is part of the form in which the biblical record claims to have been given.

---

# 25.42 The Necessary Distinctions

At this point several distinctions must remain firmly in place.

### Revelation

> **What God makes known.**

### Communication

> **How the revelation is conveyed.**

### Prophetic reception

> **The prophet receives the divine communication.**

### Prophetic proclamation

> **The prophet declares the message.**

### Testimony

> **The message or event is reported and transmitted.**

### Writing

> **The communication is committed to written form.**

### Inspiration

> **The divine operation concerning Scripture.**

These concepts are connected.

They are not interchangeable.

---

# 25.43 The Prophet and the Written Record

The prophetic office therefore helps explain how a divine communication can become a written record without requiring that the prophet cease to be a genuine human author.

The prophet can:

- hear;
- see;
- receive;
- remember;
- speak;
- dictate;
- write;
- or supervise the preservation of a message.

The precise mechanism varies by passage.

The governing principle remains:

> **God communicates through a genuine human agent, and the communication can enter the written testimony.**

---

# 25.44 The Prophetic Record Is Publicly Addressed

An important characteristic of prophecy is public proclamation.

The prophet often speaks:

> **to Israel;**
>
> **to Judah;**
>
> **to kings;**
>
> **to priests;**
>
> **to nations;**
>
> **to cities;**
>
> **to particular historical communities.**

The message therefore enters public history.

It can be opposed, accepted, remembered, recorded, transmitted, and later evaluated.

This public character contributes to its historical evidential value.

---

# 25.45 The Prophet's Message Is Not Validated by Popularity

The prophets often speak precisely when their message is unpopular.

Therefore:

> **acceptance is not the source of prophetic authority.**

A prophet may be rejected.

A prophet may be persecuted.

A prophet may be ignored.

The question remains:

> **Did the prophet genuinely speak the word of God?**

That is a question of divine commission and authentication, not popularity.

---

# 25.46 Prophetic Faithfulness

The prophetic model also requires faithfulness.

A prophet must declare what has been received rather than replacing it with a preferred message.

This is why false prophecy is treated so seriously.

The prophetic office is therefore simultaneously:

> **privileged**

and:

> **accountable.**

The prophet carries a message but does not own its authority.

---

# 25.47 The Historical Significance of Multiple Prophets

Multiple prophetic witnesses provide a distinctive feature of the biblical record.

The corpus contains:

- different individuals;
- different generations;
- different geographical settings;
- different political circumstances;
- different audiences;
- different literary forms.

Yet the prophetic testimony repeatedly contributes to one developing account of God's dealings with humanity.

This breadth is relevant to the cumulative case for the coherence and historical depth of Scripture.

---

# 25.48 The Prophets as Part of the Bible's Longitudinal Testimony

The Bible is unusual in the extent to which its narrative and prophetic record crosses generations.

The prophetic writings participate in this longitudinal structure.

A message delivered in one generation can become the interpretive framework for another.

Later writers can quote earlier prophets.

Historical events can be interpreted through prior prophecy.

Promises can be remembered.

Warnings can be fulfilled.

The written corpus therefore possesses a temporal depth that must be considered when assessing it as a historical record.

---

# 25.49 Necessary Conclusion

The biblical evidence establishes the prophet as a genuine human mediator within God's communicative activity.

> **God calls.**

> **God communicates.**

> **The prophet receives.**

> **The prophet proclaims.**

> **The message enters history.**

> **The communication may be written and preserved.**

The prophet therefore occupies a crucial place between:

> **Divine Source**

and:

> **Written Testimony.**

The prophetic office is not reducible to prediction, personality, religious institution, or private opinion.

It is presented as a divinely commissioned human ministry of receiving and declaring God's message.

---

# 25.50 What This Chapter Establishes

This chapter has established:

1. **The prophet is a human agent within divine communication.**
2. **Prophetic authority derives from divine commission.**
3. **Prophecy is broader than prediction; it is divinely commissioned proclamation.**
4. **The biblical prophetic formula identifies God as the Source of the message.**
5. **Prophetic communication enters concrete historical circumstances.**
6. **The Bible distinguishes genuine prophets from false claimants to prophetic authority.**
7. **Prophetic speech can become written testimony.**
8. **Jeremiah 36 demonstrates a concrete historical movement from divine communication to writing, reading, destruction, and renewed preservation.**
9. **The plurality of prophets does not imply plurality of ultimate divine Sources.**
10. **The prophetic ministry is an essential link in tracing the path from divine communication to the written record.**

---

# 25.51 What This Chapter Does Not Yet Establish

The chapter does not yet exhaustively establish:

- the complete doctrine of revelation;
- the complete doctrine of inspiration;
- the full scope of inspired Scripture;
- the canonical corpus;
- textual preservation;
- the complete authentication of prophetic claims;
- Christ's unique revelation;
- apostolic authority;
- visions and dreams as distinct revelation modes.

Those subjects have their proper places later in the architecture.

The purpose here has been to establish the prophetic mediator and his role in the communicative history of Scripture.

---

# 25.52 Transition to Chapter 26

The investigation has now moved from:

> **God speaks**

to:

> **God speaks through angels**

and then:

> **God speaks through prophets.**

But Hebrews 1 does not end with the prophets.

It continues:

> **“Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son.”**

The architecture therefore moves toward its decisive center.

# Chapter 26 — Christ

The next question is no longer merely:

> **Who mediated God's word?**

It is:

> **Who is the Son through whom God has spoken in these last days?**

That question requires a different level of investigation because Christ is not simply another messenger within the prophetic sequence.

The next chapter therefore examines **Christ's unique place in divine revelation and in the authentication of the written testimony**.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **The prophet is a genuine human agent through whom God communicates.**
- **Prophetic authority rests upon divine commission, not personality or popularity.**
- **Prophecy is divinely commissioned proclamation, not merely prediction.**
- **The prophetic formula “Thus saith the LORD” identifies God as the Source.**
- **Moses, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, and the prophetic writers provide a multi-generational historical witness.**
- **Jeremiah 1 demonstrates divine calling and commissioning.**
- **Jeremiah 1:9 directly connects God's words with the prophet's proclamation.**
- **Jeremiah 36 demonstrates the movement from divine communication to writing and public reading.**
- **Prophetic communication can therefore enter the written record through genuine human activity.**
- **The Bible distinguishes true prophets from false claimants to divine authority.**
- **The plurality of prophets forms a broad historical witness while remaining within the biblical claim of one divine Source.**
- **The prophetic office prepares the investigation for Christ without equating Christ with the prophets.**

# Methodological Guardrails

### 1. Do not reduce prophecy to prediction

Prediction is one form of prophetic revelation, not the definition of prophecy.

### 2. Do not confuse prophet with Source

> **God → prophet → audience**

The prophet is the commissioned human mediator.

### 3. Do not confuse prophetic reception with writing

A prophet can receive a message before that message is spoken or written.

### 4. Do not confuse prophecy with inspiration

The existence of prophetic revelation does not by itself provide the complete doctrine of inspiration.

### 5. Do not assume every prophetic utterance became Scripture

Prophetic ministry is broader than the surviving written prophetic corpus.

### 6. Do not collapse prophets, apostles, and Christ into one category

Their relationships to divine communication and authority differ and will be treated separately.

### 7. Do not use prophetic claims circularly as their own complete authentication

The biblical record contains its own authentication criteria, while the broader evidential case will be developed cumulatively.

### 8. Preserve genuine human agency

Prophets were historical persons who genuinely spoke and wrote.

### 9. Preserve divine agency

The biblical claim remains that God communicated through them.

### 10. Distinguish communication from preservation

The historical path from revelation to written Scripture involves reception, proclamation, writing, transmission, and preservation.

---

# Final Doctrinal Statement

> **The biblical record presents prophets as genuine human agents commissioned by God to receive and proclaim divine communication within history. Their ministry includes proclamation, warning, instruction, covenant enforcement, and, where given, predictive revelation. The prophetic message is attributed to God as its ultimate Source, while the prophet remains a genuine historical speaker and, in some cases, writer or participant in the writing process. Through prophetic reception, proclamation, testimony, writing, and preservation, divine communication enters the historical written record. The prophetic office therefore forms a major link between the divine Source and the written testimony and prepares the investigation for the unique revelation of God through His Son.**
