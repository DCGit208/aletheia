# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 15 — What Is the Bible?

> **Movement III — What Is the Bible?**

---

## Chapter Assignment

> **Define the Bible before making sweeping claims about it. Establish its nature as the body of writings under investigation and ask what the Bible itself claims concerning its origin, contents, authority, and truth.**

Chapters 1–8 established the problem of truth, reality, knowledge, evidence, reason, judgment, and ultimate authority. Chapters 9–14 then examined the ground of truth, God in relation to truth, His truthfulness and immutability, His inability to lie, revelation, and the danger of confusing God's revelation with human accounts about God.

The next question must therefore be precise:

> **What is the Bible?**

Not merely: What does an institution say about it? What does a theologian say about it? What does a commentator say it means?

The first task is to identify the object.

Only after the object has been identified can its claims, origin, authenticity, authority, and infallibility be examined without ambiguity or circular reasoning.

---

# 1. Why Definition Must Come First

A conclusion cannot be evaluated intelligently if its object has not been identified.

If someone says, “The Bible is false,” ask:

> **What precisely is being called “the Bible”?**

If someone says, “The Bible is God's Word,” ask:

> **What body of writings is being designated, and what warrants that designation?**

If someone says, “The Bible contradicts itself,” ask:

> **Which writings, passages, textual readings, contexts, and kind of contradiction?**

The same discipline applies to favorable claims.

A statement does not become true merely because it is favorable toward Scripture. It does not become false merely because it is critical of Scripture.

The claim must be defined and examined.

> **Do not evaluate a claim about an undefined object.**

---

# 2. “Bible” Means “Books”

The supplied *Bible Introduction* begins with the name itself. The English word **Bible** is not the word used in the English text of Scripture; it corresponds to the Greek **biblia**, meaning **books**. The designation developed from references to the books and holy books until the sacred writings came to be called **The Books**, and eventually **The Book — The Bible**. Jerome used the designation **The Divine Library**.

The immediate significance is simple:

> **The Bible is a collection of books.**

The word identifies a corpus rather than a single ordinary literary composition produced at one moment.

The Bible is therefore a **library**.

But it is not thereby a random library. The writings are treated as a connected body of sacred Scripture, and the investigation must determine why.

---

# 3. The Bible Is a Body of Writings

The *Bible Introduction* describes the Bible as a **divine library of sixty-six books**: thirty-nine in the Old Testament and twenty-seven in the New Testament in the arrangement under consideration.

Its writings include:

- history;
- biography;
- poetry;
- proverbial sayings;
- hymns;
- laws;
- letters;
- directions concerning worship;
- parables;
- riddles;
- allegories;
- prophecy;
- and other forms of human expression.

This diversity is part of the object being investigated.

A historical narrative must be recognized as historical narrative. A proverb must be read as a proverb. Poetry must be read as poetry. Prophecy and visions must be treated according to their forms. A letter must be read as a letter.

Literary form does not determine whether the Bible is true or false. It affects how a statement is made and therefore how it should be understood.

---

# 4. The Bible Is Not One Continuous Human Composition

The *Bible Introduction* expressly rejects the idea of the Bible as an uninterrupted series of divine utterances. It describes the revelation as given “here a little and there a little” and through many men across a long historical period.

That creates a significant question:

> **How can a collection possessing such diversity exhibit the unity claimed for it?**

There are different writers, historical circumstances, literary forms, languages, audiences, and periods of divine dealing.

Yet the Bible is presented as a unified written testimony.

The answer cannot simply be asserted here. Its unity is an evidential question and is treated later as such.

> **Do not use an evidential conclusion as though it were part of the definition.**

---

# 5. What the Bible Is Not

The supplied *Bible Introduction* gives useful negative descriptions.

The Bible is not:

- an amulet, charm, or fetish;
- a physical object whose mere presence automatically performs wonders;
- a chronological diary of every event;
- an uninterrupted series of divine utterances;
- a book written in supernatural heavenly language;
- a document in which ordinary passages secretly mean something unrelated to what they say;
- a specimen of God's literary technique in which human authors have no genuine expression;
- or a systematic discourse on one subject from beginning to end.

These distinctions prevent category errors.

The Bible is not less divine because it contains ordinary human language.

It is not less authoritative because it contains different literary forms.

It is not less unified because different writers express themselves differently.

And it is not less truthful because it records statements made by people whose statements are false.

The identity of a recorded speaker must be distinguished from the truthfulness of the written record.

---

# 6. The Bible Uses Human Language

The *Bible Introduction* states that the Bible is God's revelation expressed in human language.

The Old Testament is principally Hebrew, with specified portions in Aramaic. The New Testament is written in common Hellenistic Greek.

That matters because written communication operates through actual language.

Words matter.

Grammar matters.

Syntax matters.

Context matters.

Idioms matter.

Historical setting matters.

Literary form matters.

Lexical meaning matters.

Translation matters.

The doctrine of the Graphē therefore cannot be developed while ignoring what writing actually is.

> **The Bible is written communication and must be treated as written communication.**

---

# 7. The Bible Is More Than a Book About God

Calling the Bible merely “a book about God” is inadequate.

It certainly speaks about God, but it also records God's actions and communications.

It records creation, promises, judgments, covenants, prophecy, historical events, rulers, nations, journeys, conflicts, births, deaths, conversations, manifestations, the ministry of Jesus Christ, His death, testimony concerning His resurrection, and apostolic preaching.

These are claims about reality.

They are therefore claims capable of historical and evidential examination.

The Bible presents itself not merely as philosophical reflection about a proposed deity, but as a record concerning reality and God's dealings within history.

---

# 8. The Bible Claims a Divine Dimension

The Bible's own record presents divine communication as entering history.

The supplied *Bible Introduction* identifies several ways in which biblical material was given:

1. **The audible voice of God** — Exodus 19:19; Deuteronomy 5; Matthew 3:16–17; John 12:28.
2. **Angels** — Acts 7:38; Hebrews 2:2.
3. **Prophets** — Acts 3:21; Hebrews 1:1.
4. **Jesus Christ** — Hebrews 1:1; Revelation 1.
5. **Apostles** — Acts 1:2; Ephesians 4.
6. **Visions** — Isaiah 6; Daniel 7–8; Ezekiel 1.
7. **Dreams** — Daniel 2; Matthew 1:20; Matthew 2:12.
8. **Revelation** — the biblical revelation passages.
9. **Inspiration** — 2 Timothy 3:15–17.

These categories overlap in places; they are not nine mechanically isolated processes.

Their significance is that the Bible's own account of its origin is multi-dimensional.

There is communication, reception, witnessing, prophetic transmission, apostolic testimony, recording, and inspiration.

---

# 9. Revelation Is Not the Same Thing as Inspiration

**Revelation** concerns what is made known.

The supplied lexical material on **ἀποκάλυψις (*apokalypsis*, G602)** centers on unveiling: what was hidden is made known.

**Inspiration** concerns the divine superintendence of the communication and recording of Scripture.

Thus:

> **Revelation asks: What has God made known?**

> **Inspiration asks: What is the status of the written Scripture produced under divine superintendence?**

This distinction matters because not everything recorded in Scripture is a newly revealed proposition.

A historical event may be witnessed. A king may speak. An apostle may investigate testimony. A prophet may record an event.

Yet Paul writes:

> **“All scripture is given by inspiration of God.”**  
> — 2 Timothy 3:16

Therefore:

> **All Scripture is inspired, even though not every passage is itself a newly revealed proposition.**

This distinction will be developed fully in the later inspiration chapters.

---

# 10. Inspiration Does Not Make Every Recorded Speaker Truthful

The *Bible Introduction* makes another important distinction.

Inspiration can faithfully record the words of God, angels, human beings, Satan, and demons. But faithful recording is not the same thing as affirming every statement spoken by those characters.

If Scripture records Satan telling a lie, inspiration guarantees that the lie was accurately recorded. It does not turn the lie into truth.

Therefore:

> **The truthfulness of the written record is not identical to the truthfulness of every statement uttered by every character recorded within the record.**

The record can truthfully say:

> “This person said X.”

while X itself is false.

This safeguard will be essential when the book later develops the doctrine of infallibility.

---

# 11. The Bible Contains Testimony

The Bible is also a testimonial record.

Peter writes:

> **“For we have not followed cunningly devised fables, when we made known unto you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but were eyewitnesses of his majesty.”**  
> — 2 Peter 1:16

Peter identifies a historical category of knowledge: witnessing.

This does not mean a person becomes truthful merely by calling himself an eyewitness. Testimony remains evidence that must be assessed.

But the biblical record itself invites historical examination.

The questions are therefore:

- Who witnessed what?
- What did they claim to see or hear?
- Were they positioned to know?
- What did they subsequently testify?
- How does their testimony relate to other testimony?
- What corroboration exists?
- What conclusion does the testimony warrant?

That is evidential reasoning, not mere quotation.

---

# 12. Jesus Christ Is Presented as the Faithful Witness

The testimonial dimension is not exhausted by the term “eyewitness.”

Revelation 1:5 identifies Jesus Christ as:

> **“the faithful witness.”**

Christ is therefore not merely an object about whom others testify. He is presented as a faithful witness.

Hebrews 1:1–2 likewise presents a progression:

> God spoke in time past by the prophets, and in these last days spoke by His Son.

The biblical record therefore places Christ centrally within its account of divine communication.

The investigation must later examine what Christ's life, words, works, death, resurrection, and testimony establish.

For this chapter, the structural conclusion is sufficient:

> **Christ is central to the Bible's own account of divine revelation and faithful witness.**

---

# 13. The Bible Records History

The Bible names people, places, rulers, nations, journeys, events, and actions. It records national histories and public events. It records the life and ministry of Jesus Christ and the actions and testimony of His apostles.

These are claims about reality.

A historical claim can be examined.

A witness can be assessed.

A document can be genuine or forged.

A report can preserve an event accurately or inaccurately.

A manuscript can preserve or alter a reading.

These are distinct questions.

The Bible's historical character therefore creates an evidential dimension that cannot be dismissed merely by assigning it a broad category such as “religious.”

The relevant questions remain:

> **What happened?**

and:

> **What warrants believing that it happened?**

---

# 14. The Bible Is a Record of God's Dealings With Humanity

The *Bible Introduction* describes the Bible as the record of God's dealings with humanity in the past, present, and future.

That description captures the narrative character of the corpus.

God creates.

God communicates.

God makes promises.

God judges.

God reveals.

People respond.

Nations rise and fall.

Prophets speak.

Christ comes.

Witnesses testify.

The apostles preach.

The written record preserves testimony.

The Bible therefore presents an account of reality rather than merely a collection of disconnected propositions.

---

# 15. The Bible Is a Record of Divine Revelation

The *Bible Introduction* states that much of the Bible is revelation because human beings could not otherwise know certain matters concerning the eternal past, creation, the spiritual world, God's purposes, future events, and other matters.

The reasoning is straightforward.

If a fact is inaccessible to ordinary human observation and cannot be reached by human inference, human beings cannot discover it merely by speculation.

If God discloses that fact, revelation supplies knowledge unavailable by ordinary means.

Thus:

> **Reason can examine revelation; reason does not become the source of revelation merely by examining it.**

---

# 16. The Bible's Unity Is a Question, Not a Premise

The *Bible Introduction* emphasizes the Bible's unity despite diversity of authors, periods, circumstances, and literary forms.

That may constitute significant evidence.

But evidence must not be inserted into the definition.

Therefore this chapter does not argue:

> **“The Bible is divine because it is unified.”**

It establishes instead:

> **The Bible is a diverse collection that claims remarkable unity; the nature and evidential significance of that unity must be investigated.**

That investigation belongs later.

---

# 17. The Bible Is Not Defined by an Institution

A Bible can be possessed by an individual, library, school, church, government, museum, or private collection.

None of these creates its authority.

Likewise, an institution does not make a writing Scripture merely by declaring it Scripture.

> **Institutional recognition and textual authority are distinct questions.**

An institution may preserve, teach, interpret, or dispute Scripture.

It may also misunderstand it.

The written record therefore remains the object of investigation.

---

# 18. The Bible Is Not Defined by a Commentary

A commentary may explain a passage, compare passages, provide historical information, identify lexical possibilities, trace themes, or propose an interpretation.

That can be valuable.

But commentary remains commentary.

The reader must distinguish:

> **what the biblical text says**

from:

> **what an interpreter says the biblical text means.**

A secondary source may assist the investigation.

It cannot silently become the object being investigated.

The proper direction is:

> **commentary → text → context → evidence → conclusion**

not:

> **commentary → assumed meaning → text forced into conformity.**

---

# 19. The Bible Is Not Defined by Philosophy

Philosophy can ask important questions about existence, causality, necessity, contingency, knowledge, morality, reason, inference, and evidence.

But a philosophical description of God is not automatically identical with the biblical revelation concerning God.

The distinction established in Chapter 14 therefore remains operative.

A philosophical argument may provide evidence relevant to the investigation.

It may not automatically become:

> **“What Scripture says God is.”**

The two may be compared.

They must not be conflated.

---

# 20. What the Bible Says It Is

The biblical record and the supplied *Bible Introduction* present the Bible as:

1. **a divine library of sixty-six books;**
2. **a written record of God's dealings with humanity;**
3. **God's inspired revelation concerning the origin and destiny of all things;**
4. **a record containing historical events and testimony;**
5. **a collection containing many literary forms;**
6. **a written body containing revelation and other records under inspiration;**
7. **a corpus containing prophetic and apostolic testimony;**
8. **a corpus centered on the person and work of Jesus Christ;**
9. **a written body whose contents claim divine authority;**
10. **the Scripture that later biblical writers and Christ treat as authoritative.**

These propositions must not be flattened into one.

Some describe the corpus.

Some report what it claims.

Some describe its literary nature.

Some concern its origin.

Some concern authority.

The investigation must keep the categories distinct.

---

# 21. Definition, Claim, Evidence, and Conclusion

Four categories must now be separated.

### Definition

> **The Bible is the recognized body of writings constituting the Old and New Testaments.**

This identifies the object.

### Claim

> **The Bible claims divine inspiration.**

This reports what the object claims.

### Evidence

> **The writings contain prophetic claims, eyewitness testimony, historical material, unity, and other evidential features.**

This identifies material bearing upon authentication.

### Conclusion

> **The evidence warrants the conclusion that the Bible is divinely inspired.**

This is a conclusion that must be established by argument.

If these categories are collapsed, circular reasoning becomes almost unavoidable.

---

# 22. Why This Chapter Does Not Yet Prove Everything

The book must not argue:

> **“The Bible is inspired because it is the inspired Word of God.”**

That merely repeats the conclusion.

The stronger order is:

1. identify the corpus;
2. examine its claims;
3. examine its historical and testimonial character;
4. examine its claimed modes of divine communication;
5. investigate genuineness;
6. investigate authenticity;
7. examine the evidence;
8. establish the role of Christ;
9. examine apostolic authority;
10. examine canon;
11. examine inspiration;
12. establish infallibility;
13. examine Christ's treatment of Scripture;
14. then draw the conclusions concerning the Graphē.

That is why this chapter defines the object without pretending that the entire authentication argument has already been completed.

---

# 23. The Bible Must Be Understood Before It Is Defended

A person can defend a proposition Scripture never made.

A person can refute an interpretation Scripture never intended.

A person can defend a caricature of the Bible or attack a caricature of the Bible.

Both failures arise from the same problem:

> **The object has not been properly understood.**

Therefore this work begins by asking better questions:

- What is the Bible?
- What writings constitute it?
- What forms of writing does it contain?
- What does it claim concerning its origin?
- How was its material given?
- What is revelation?
- What is inspiration?
- Who witnessed what?
- What is the role of Christ?
- What is the role of the apostles?
- What is Scripture?
- What is Graphē?
- Why is it Holy Writ?
- What evidence authenticates it?

Those questions will be answered in their proper order.

---

# 24. Chapter Conclusion

The question **“What is the Bible?”** has produced a more precise answer than “a religious book.”

The Bible is:

> **a collection of books constituting a written body of testimony, containing multiple literary forms, historical records, divine communications, prophetic material, witness testimony, and apostolic testimony, and claiming divine revelation and inspiration.**

It is therefore an object capable of investigation at four levels:

- **Ontological:** What kind of written object is it?
- **Epistemological:** How can its claims be known?
- **Evidential:** What warrants accepting its claims?
- **Hermeneutical:** How should its writings be correctly understood?

Those questions must remain ordered.

The most important conclusion of this chapter is methodological:

> **We have identified the object without smuggling the final conclusion into its definition.**

That preserves the integrity of everything that follows.

The next chapter therefore asks:

> **If the Bible is a library of writings, what kind of library is it, and how do its diverse writings constitute a written record?**

That is the question of **The Bible as Divine Library and Written Record**.
