# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 14 — The Problem With Human Accounts About God

> **Movement II — God, Truth, and Revelation**

---

## Chapter Assignment

### Locked TOC description

> **Examine the difference between what Scripture establishes and what commentators, philosophers, theologians, institutions, or traditions say about Scripture or God. External claims must themselves be tested.**

This chapter follows Chapter 13, which established the distinction between **knowledge supplied by revelation** and **human speculation about God**.

The question now becomes unavoidable:

> **What happens when a human account about God is treated as though it were God's own revelation?**

The answer is not that every human account is false.

Nor is it that philosophy, history, scholarship, commentary, or theology has no legitimate place.

The issue is **authority, source, warrant, and category**.

A human account may be:

- accurate;
- partially accurate;
- mistaken;
- incomplete;
- speculative;
- evidentially useful;
- interpretive;
- historical;
- philosophical;
- theological;
- or directly contrary to Scripture.

It must therefore be examined according to what it actually establishes.

The controlling distinction is:

> **God's Word is not identical with every human claim made about God's Word.**

---

# 1. The Question Before Us

Chapter 13 established that if God makes Himself known, knowledge supplied by that revelation must not be confused with human speculation about God.

That distinction now has to be applied.

Human beings speak about God constantly.

They write dictionaries.

They produce commentaries.

They formulate theological systems.

They construct philosophical arguments.

They develop traditions.

They establish institutions.

They publish academic studies.

They summarize Scripture.

They debate doctrines.

They offer interpretations.

Some of these works may be extremely valuable.

But none becomes divine revelation merely because it discusses divine revelation.

This gives us a fundamental rule:

> **The subject matter of a statement does not determine the authority of the statement.**

A book about God is not therefore the Word of God.

A commentary on Scripture is not therefore Scripture.

A theological proposition about God is not therefore a divine proposition.

A philosophical argument for God's existence is not therefore God's own account of His existence.

And an institution that identifies itself with God, Christ, Scripture, or Christian teaching does not acquire divine authority merely by making that identification.

The claim must be examined.

---

# 2. Source and Statement Must Be Distinguished

Consider two statements:

> **“God said X.”**

and:

> **“A scholar says that God said X.”**

These statements are not epistemically identical.

The first is a claim concerning divine communication.

The second is a human report about that claim.

The second may be correct.

But its correctness cannot be established merely by its being stated.

The same distinction applies to Scripture.

There is a difference between:

> **“Scripture says X.”**

and:

> **“This commentator says Scripture says X.”**

And there is another difference between:

> **“Scripture says X.”**

and:

> **“I infer Y from Scripture's statement X.”**

These three levels must remain visible.

### Level 1 — The biblical statement

What the written text actually says.

### Level 2 — The human interpretation

What an interpreter says the text means.

### Level 3 — The human inference

What someone concludes from the text and its meaning.

The authority of Level 1 cannot simply be transferred to Levels 2 or 3.

This distinction is one of the most important safeguards in the entire investigation.

---

# 3. Why This Distinction Matters

If the distinction disappears, a remarkable substitution can occur.

A person begins with Scripture.

Then a commentator explains Scripture.

Then the commentator's explanation is repeated.

Eventually the explanation becomes tradition.

The tradition becomes institutional doctrine.

The institutional doctrine becomes a test of belonging.

And finally the human interpretation is treated as though Scripture itself had spoken it.

The original source has been displaced.

The process can be represented:

> **Scripture**
>
> ↓
>
> **interpretation**
>
> ↓
>
> **repetition**
>
> ↓
>
> **tradition**
>
> ↓
>
> **institutionalization**
>
> ↓
>
> **assumed authority**

At the end of the process, the reader may no longer be able to distinguish:

> **what God said**

from:

> **what people have said that God said.**

The danger is not merely academic.

It changes the authority structure by which truth is judged.

---

# 4. Easton's Statement as a Test Case

A useful example comes from the material examined for this work.

Easton's Bible Dictionary, in its discussion of God, presents the statement that the Bible takes God's existence for granted and does not formally attempt to prove God's existence. The same entry then discusses what it calls the arguments generally advanced by theologians: the *a priori* argument and *a posteriori* arguments, including cosmological, teleological, and moral reasoning.

This provides an excellent test case for the methodology of *One Source of Truth*.

The purpose is not to dismiss Easton because Easton is a commentator.

Nor is the purpose to accept Easton's statement because Easton is a respected reference work.

The question is:

> **What exactly is being claimed, what is meant by “the Bible,” what evidence supports the claim, and does the claim accurately represent the biblical record?**

That is the proper question.

---

# 5. “The Bible Takes the Existence of God for Granted”

The statement sounds simple.

But before deciding whether it is true, we must ask:

> **What does “the Bible” mean in the statement?**

Is it:

- the Hebrew Scriptures?
- the Old Testament?
- the New Testament?
- the sixty-six-book collection?
- the canonical corpus?
- the biblical record as a whole?
- a particular passage?
- a particular literary form?
- or a commentator's conceptual summary of the collection?

A sweeping statement requires a defined object.

This is not a trivial objection.

If someone says:

> **“The Bible does X,”**

the reader must know what body of writings is being described and what textual evidence warrants the description.

Otherwise the statement can become rhetorically powerful while remaining analytically undefined.

---

# 6. What Does the Biblical Record Actually Do?

The biblical record does not merely present a philosophical proposition:

> **“God exists.”**

It presents God acting, speaking, creating, judging, promising, appearing, revealing, commanding, communicating, and entering into historical dealings with human beings.

Genesis opens:

> **“In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.”**  
> — Genesis 1:1

The statement does not read like a speculative proof constructed from premises outside the text.

It is a declaration about reality.

But that does not establish Easton's statement by itself.

It establishes something more important for our investigation:

> **The biblical record contains positive assertions concerning God, His existence, His actions, His character, His purposes, and His dealings with creation.**

The record therefore cannot responsibly be reduced to the proposition that it merely “takes God for granted” without qualification.

The actual content must be examined.

---

# 7. The Bible Does Not Present God as an Abstract Conclusion

The biblical record presents God as the Creator.

> **“In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.”**  
> — Genesis 1:1

It presents Him as the One who speaks.

> **“And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.”**  
> — Genesis 1:3

It presents Him as the One who reveals.

It presents Him as the One who speaks through prophets.

It presents Him as the One who manifests Himself.

It presents Him as the One who sends His Son.

It presents Jesus Christ as the Word made flesh.

It presents God as truthful and incapable of lying.

It presents His works in history.

It presents testimony concerning those works.

It presents prophecy and fulfillment.

It presents witnesses.

It presents written records.

Therefore the biblical account is not merely a philosophical proposition from which the reader is invited to infer God's existence.

It is a **recorded account of divine reality and divine activity**.

That distinction matters.

---

# 8. The Problem Is Not That Philosophical Arguments Exist

We must be equally careful not to make an opposite error.

The existence of philosophical arguments concerning God is not itself a problem.

Human beings naturally ask:

- Why is there something rather than nothing?
- What explains contingent existence?
- Why does the universe exhibit order?
- What explains moral obligation?
- What explains rational consciousness?
- Is there a necessary reality?
- Is there an intelligent cause?

These are legitimate questions.

Reason can investigate them.

The error would be to assume that the resulting philosophical construction is automatically identical with God's revelation.

A cosmological argument may attempt to reason from effects to a cause.

A teleological argument may reason from order or design.

A moral argument may reason from moral consciousness.

These may be worthy of analysis.

But they do not thereby become:

> **“What Scripture says God is.”**

That is a category distinction.

---

# 9. Evidence Is Not Authority

This distinction must be maintained carefully.

An archaeological discovery may provide evidence concerning a biblical event.

A manuscript may provide evidence concerning the text.

An eyewitness may provide testimony concerning an event.

A philosophical argument may provide rational support for a conclusion.

A historical source may corroborate a person or event.

None of these automatically becomes the **authority** of Scripture.

Evidence can support the conclusion that a document is genuine.

Evidence can support the conclusion that an event occurred.

Evidence can support the credibility of a witness.

Evidence can support the conclusion that a claim is highly warranted.

But evidence and authority are not identical categories.

The authority of a genuinely divine communication derives from its source.

The evidence establishes whether the claimed source and communication are genuine.

This distinction will become essential in the later authentication chapters.

---

# 10. Why Theology Cannot Replace the Biblical Record

The word *theology* can describe the systematic study or organization of claims concerning God and divine things.

There is nothing inherently wrong with organizing knowledge.

Indeed, organization can be extremely useful.

But organization is not revelation.

A theological system is a human construction designed to arrange propositions.

Therefore each proposition within it must still be traced to its warrant.

The correct question is not:

> **“Is this theology?”**

The question is:

> **“What establishes this theological proposition?”**

If Scripture establishes it, show where and how.

If it is an inference from Scripture, identify the inference.

If it comes from historical evidence, identify the historical evidence.

If it comes from philosophy, identify the philosophical premises.

If it is a tradition, identify the tradition.

If it is an institutional decision, identify the institutional basis.

This makes theology accountable to evidence rather than allowing the label “theology” to function as evidence.

---

# 11. Why Commentary Cannot Become the Text

A commentary can explain a passage.

It can compare passages.

It can provide historical information.

It can identify lexical possibilities.

It can trace themes.

It can offer an interpretation.

But the commentary remains commentary.

Its authority is derivative.

This does not mean it is useless.

It means it must be correctly classified.

A good commentary can help the reader see something that is genuinely present in the text.

A poor commentary can obscure it.

A reader must therefore be capable of moving in the reverse direction:

> **commentary → text → context → evidence → conclusion**

rather than:

> **commentary → accepted doctrine → text forced to conform.**

The difference is enormous.

---

# 12. The Bible Introduction Provides a Different Kind of Material

The *Bible Introduction* used in this investigation illustrates why source integration must be more sophisticated than simply citing a reference work.

It describes the Bible as:

- God's inspired revelation;
- a record of God's dealings with humanity;
- a divine library of sixty-six books;
- a collection containing history, biography, poetry, proverbial sayings, hymns, letters, laws, parables, riddles, allegories, prophecy, and other forms of expression;
- and a written record concerning God's dealings with humanity.

It also distinguishes revelation from inspiration.

Its formulation is especially important:

> revelation concerns the discovery or disclosure of new truth;

while inspiration concerns the divine superintendence of communicating and recording that truth.

The Introduction further explains that all Scripture is inspired, while not every passage is itself a newly revealed proposition.

That distinction is directly relevant to this chapter.

The Bible can accurately record:

- God's words;
- Satan's words;
- the words of angels;
- the words of demons;
- and the words of human beings,

without thereby affirming every statement spoken by those characters as true.

The record is truthful in recording what was said.

The proposition spoken by a false speaker remains false.

This distinction prevents a major category error:

> **the truthfulness of the record is not identical to the truthfulness of every proposition recorded within the record.**

---

# 13. What the Bible Introduction Means by “Genuineness” and “Authenticity”

The *Bible Introduction* also distinguishes two concepts.

### Genuineness

It defines genuineness in terms of whether the books were actually written by the persons whose names they bear, or whether sufficient evidence establishes their authorship and the time claimed.

### Authenticity

It defines authenticity in terms of whether the Bible truthfully relates the matters with which it deals and whether its contents correspond to what was originally written.

These definitions are important because they show that the authentication problem is not exhausted by saying:

> **“The Bible says it is God's Word.”**

The source itself identifies external and internal lines of evidence.

Among the material it identifies are:

1. the Bible's own claims;
2. Christ's relation to the Old Testament;
3. divine confirmation associated with apostolic ministry;
4. New Testament testimony to the Old Testament;
5. secular historical testimony;
6. ancient manuscripts;
7. lectionaries;
8. patristic quotations;
9. ancient versions;
10. and other evidential considerations.

These are not all the same kind of evidence.

That is precisely why they must be analyzed rather than merely listed.

---

# 14. The Cumulative Case Must Be Reconstructed, Not Merely Announced

The *Bible Introduction* gives a particularly important methodological model.

It presents numerous alleged evidences for inspiration and authenticity, including:

- unity;
- superiority;
- influence;
- preservation;
- fulfilled prophecy;
- miracles;
- perfection and accuracy;
- biblical claims;
- secular historical confirmation;
- and what it calls inexhaustible proofs.

The correct response is not:

> **“The Introduction says there are twenty proofs, therefore the Bible is inspired.”**

That would violate the methodology of this book.

Instead, each proposed proof must be translated into an argument.

For example:

### Unity

What is the alleged unity?

Across how many writers?

Across what period?

Under what circumstances?

How much diversity existed?

What would we reasonably expect from independent writings produced under those conditions?

What conclusion follows?

### Fulfilled prophecy

What was predicted?

When was the prediction recorded?

How specific was it?

What constitutes fulfillment?

Could the fulfillment have been manufactured after the fact?

What historical evidence exists?

What conclusion follows?

### Miracles

What event is claimed?

What testimony exists?

What makes the witness credible?

What alternative explanations exist?

What conclusion follows?

### Manuscripts

What manuscripts exist?

How early are they?

What portions do they preserve?

How are textual differences evaluated?

What conclusion follows concerning the recoverability of the text?

The evidential argument becomes much stronger when the reader can see the reasoning.

---

# 15. Eyewitness Testimony Is Not Mere Speculation

This distinction is particularly important concerning the New Testament.

Peter writes:

> **“For we have not followed cunningly devised fables, when we made known unto you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but were eyewitnesses of his majesty.”**  
> — 2 Peter 1:16

This is an explicit evidential claim.

Peter does not describe the testimony as a philosophical deduction.

He identifies witnesses.

The argument is therefore historical and testimonial.

But even here, disciplined reasoning requires questions:

- Who witnessed the event?
- What did they claim to witness?
- Were they in a position to know?
- When did they testify?
- What did they risk?
- Is their testimony internally coherent?
- Is it consistent with other testimony?
- Is there corroborating evidence?
- What conclusion does the testimony warrant?

And Revelation 1:5 gives an additional dimension by identifying Jesus Christ as:

> **“the faithful witness.”**

Christ therefore belongs centrally within the evidential architecture.

The investigation is not limited to people who witnessed particular moments of His earthly ministry.

The biblical record presents Christ Himself as the faithful witness and presents His life, works, death, resurrection, words, and continuing consequences as historically significant.

The question remains what these facts establish.

---

# 16. “Which Bible?” Is an Epistemological Question

The Easton example exposes a broader issue.

When someone says:

> **“The Bible says...”**

the reader should not immediately respond with either belief or rejection.

The reader should ask:

> **Which writing?**

> **What does it actually say?**

> **What is its context?**

> **What is the textual evidence?**

> **What is the meaning?**

> **What does the claim establish?**

> **What is being inferred?**

This is not hostility toward Scripture.

It is respect for Scripture.

A text cannot be understood responsibly by allowing a secondary statement about it to replace the text itself.

---

# 17. External Scholarship Has a Proper Place

This book does not require intellectual isolation.

Historical research can be valuable.

Lexical research can be valuable.

Manuscript scholarship can be valuable.

Archaeology can be valuable.

Philosophy can be valuable.

Theology can be valuable.

Commentaries can be valuable.

Dictionaries can be valuable.

But each must remain within its proper category.

### Lexicon

Can establish lexical information.

### History

Can establish historical information.

### Archaeology

Can establish archaeological information.

### Manuscript study

Can establish evidence concerning textual transmission.

### Commentary

Can offer interpretation.

### Theology

Can organize theological propositions.

### Philosophy

Can analyze conceptual and logical questions.

None of these categories automatically becomes:

> **the Word of God.**

That status requires a separate argument.

---

# 18. Human Reasoning Can Expose Error Without Becoming Revelation

There is another important distinction.

Suppose a human philosophical argument contradicts Scripture.

The conclusion does not have to be:

> “Therefore philosophy is evil.”

The proper conclusion may simply be:

> **The argument is unsound, because one or more of its premises or inferences fail.**

Likewise, if a theological system contradicts Scripture, the response should not merely be:

> “That theologian is wrong.”

The stronger response is:

> **Here is the proposition; here is the biblical record; here is the interpretation being made; here is the inference; here is where the inference fails.**

That is dianoetic engagement.

It is the reasoning discipline established in Movement I.

---

# 19. The Danger of Beginning With a Human System

Suppose someone begins with a philosophical conception of God.

He then interprets Scripture through that conception.

Whenever Scripture appears to conflict with the conception, he modifies the interpretation until the conflict disappears.

The resulting system may become internally sophisticated.

But its sophistication does not prove its truth.

The problem is methodological.

The system has become the controlling premise.

The text has become the dependent object.

The proper order is reversed.

The investigator must therefore ask:

> **Did the text produce the conclusion, or did the conclusion control the reading of the text?**

This question should be asked repeatedly.

---

# 20. The Same Problem Exists in Institutions

Institutional status does not automatically create truth.

An institution may possess:

- history;
- credentials;
- buildings;
- offices;
- titles;
- publications;
- schools;
- councils;
- traditions;
- ceremonies;
- recognized leaders;
- or political influence.

None of these properties converts an institutional proposition into divine revelation.

The question remains:

> **What establishes the proposition?**

An institution may faithfully preserve and teach Scripture.

It may also misunderstand Scripture.

The institution must therefore remain distinguishable from the written Word.

This principle applies equally to:

- churches;
- denominations;
- ministries;
- schools;
- Bible colleges;
- seminaries;
- scholarly societies;
- governments;
- and other organizations.

Institutional recognition can establish institutional recognition.

It cannot, by itself, establish divine authority.

---

# 21. Tradition Must Also Be Tested

Tradition can preserve valuable information.

But antiquity is not truth.

A proposition does not become true because people have believed it for a thousand years.

Nor does it become false because it is old.

Age is a historical property.

Truth is a correspondence property.

Therefore tradition must be evaluated according to evidence.

The relevant question is:

> **What does the tradition preserve, where did it originate, what evidence supports it, and does it correspond to the written record?**

This is why the distinction between:

> **the Graphē**

and:

> **human accounts about the Graphē**

must remain permanent.

---

# 22. The Proper Use of Human Accounts

Human accounts are not to be discarded.

They are to be **subordinated and evaluated**.

A secondary source may:

- alert the reader to a passage;
- identify an historical event;
- preserve an ancient testimony;
- explain a word;
- identify a manuscript;
- reconstruct a historical setting;
- offer a logical argument;
- or expose a competing interpretation.

Its usefulness is real.

But its usefulness does not make it infallible.

The proper sequence is:

> **Receive the claim**
>
> ↓
>
> **Identify its source**
>
> ↓
>
> **Determine its category**
>
> ↓
>
> **Examine its evidence**
>
> ↓
>
> **Compare it with Scripture where Scripture is relevant**
>
> ↓
>
> **Analyze its reasoning**
>
> ↓
>
> **Determine what it actually establishes**
>
> ↓
>
> **Retain what is warranted**
>
> ↓
>
> **Reject or qualify what is not warranted**

This is neither blind acceptance nor blind rejection.

It is disciplined examination.

---

# 23. The Bible Must Not Be Reduced to a Human Interpretation of It

The distinction can now be stated more sharply.

There is:

> **the written record**

and there are:

> **human accounts of the written record.**

The second category can be extensive.

It can include thousands of books and millions of pages.

But quantity does not change category.

Ten thousand commentaries do not become Scripture by accumulation.

A million repetitions of an interpretation do not make the interpretation inspired.

Institutional consensus does not transform a proposition into revelation.

Academic prestige does not create divine authority.

Nor does disagreement with a human interpretation constitute disagreement with Scripture.

The text must be brought back into view.

---

# 24. This Also Protects Scripture From Unfair Criticism

The distinction works in both directions.

If someone attacks a commentator's interpretation, that does not necessarily constitute an attack on Scripture.

If someone exposes a contradiction in a theological system, that does not necessarily expose a contradiction in Scripture.

If someone disproves a philosophical argument for God, that does not necessarily disprove God.

If someone finds an error in a Bible commentary, that does not establish an error in the Bible.

And if someone discovers that an institution has made a false claim, that does not establish that Scripture is false.

The categories must remain distinct.

Otherwise an error in a human account can be improperly transferred to the object the account was supposed to describe.

---

# 25. The Central Epistemic Rule

We can now formulate the governing rule of this chapter:

> **Never allow a human account about God or Scripture to acquire the authority of God or Scripture without independently establishing that authority.**

This rule does not reject human knowledge.

It orders it.

It does not reject scholarship.

It disciplines scholarship.

It does not reject philosophy.

It prevents philosophy from becoming revelation by declaration.

It does not reject theology.

It prevents theology from becoming Scripture by repetition.

It does not reject institutions.

It prevents institutions from becoming divine authority merely through institutional status.

---

# 26. What This Means for the Doctrine of the Graphē

The distinction prepares the next movement.

If the Graphē is to become the primary written authority, the reader must know exactly what is being designated by that term.

That requires more than saying:

> **“The Bible is God's Word.”**

The investigation must now establish:

- what the Bible is;
- what kind of written object it is;
- how its books relate to one another;
- what it claims about its own origin;
- how revelation entered the record;
- how eyewitness testimony functions;
- how prophetic testimony functions;
- how apostolic testimony functions;
- what inspiration means;
- what Scripture means;
- what *graphē* means;
- and how the written record is authenticated.

Those questions belong to Movement III and beyond.

---

# 27. What We Have Established

This chapter has not attempted to settle every question concerning the Bible.

It has established the necessary methodological boundary.

### 1. A human account about God is not automatically divine revelation.

### 2. A commentary is not the text it explains.

### 3. A theological system is not Scripture.

### 4. A philosophical argument is not revelation merely because it concerns God.

### 5. Institutional recognition does not create divine authority.

### 6. Tradition does not become truth merely through antiquity.

### 7. Evidence and authority are distinct.

### 8. Scripture and interpretation are distinct.

### 9. Human reasoning remains necessary for examining claims about Scripture.

### 10. The Bible's own record must remain the primary object of investigation when determining what the Bible actually teaches.

And therefore:

> **Human accounts about God must be examined; they must never be silently promoted into God's own account.**

---

# 28. Transition to Movement III

We are now ready to ask the question that should have been asked before making broad claims about “the Bible”:

> **What is the Bible?**

Not:

> *What does a commentator say the Bible is?*

Not:

> *What does a theological system say it is?*

Not:

> *What does an institution declare it to be?*

But:

> **What is this body of writings, what kind of record does it constitute, what does it claim concerning its origin and contents, and what evidence surrounds those claims?**

The next movement begins there.
