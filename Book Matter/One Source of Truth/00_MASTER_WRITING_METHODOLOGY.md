# 1. PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT

This document governs the research, reasoning, organization, drafting, auditing, and final production of *One Source of Truth — The Doctrine of the Graphē*.

It exists to prevent the manuscript from becoming merely:

- a compilation of quotations;
- a Bible commentary;
- a systematic-theology textbook;
- an apologetics manual;
- a philosophy book detached from Scripture;
- a history of the Bible;
- a collection of proof texts;
- or a succession of assertions supported by citations.

The objective is a **cumulative investigation** in which the reader can follow the argument and understand **why each major conclusion follows**.

The governing progression is:

# 3. THE FOUR QUESTIONS GOVERNING THE INVESTIGATION

Every major chapter must preserve the distinction between four different kinds of inquiry.

## 3.1 Ontological Question

> **What is true? What is reality?**

This concerns being, actuality, reality, existence, and what is the case independently of someone's opinion about it.

## 3.2 Epistemological Question

> **How can truth be known?**

This concerns knowledge, means of knowing, testimony, observation, revelation, reason, inference, memory, and the reliability of the means by which knowledge is acquired.

## 3.3 Evidential Question

> **What warrants believing that something is true?**

This concerns evidence, verification, testimony, corroboration, probability, inference, explanatory power, consistency, and rational warrant.

## 3.4 Hermeneutical Question

> **Once we have an authoritative truthful text, how do we correctly understand it?**

This concerns interpretation, context, language, genre, grammar, cross-reference, authorial communication, and rightly handling the written Word.

These questions may interact, but they must not be silently conflated.

# 5. THE BIBLE INTRODUCTION

The Bible Introduction is a substantive research source and must be studied rather than merely named.

Relevant material includes, among other things:

- what the Bible is;
- the Bible as divine revelation;
- divine inspiration;
- the Bible's literary and historical character;
- its claims concerning God;
- modes of divine communication;
- eyewitness testimony;
- apostolic testimony;
- miracles;
- prophecy;
- unity;
- preservation;
- historical correspondence;
- manuscript evidence;
- canon;
- Christ's relationship to the Old Testament;
- and the cumulative evidence concerning authenticity.

The methodological rule is:

> **Extract the argument, not merely the statement.**

If a source presents unity as evidence, ask:

- What kind of unity?
- Across what diversity?
- Over what period?
- Why would that matter evidentially?
- What conclusion does the evidence actually warrant?

If prophecy is presented as evidence, ask:

- What was predicted?
- When was it recorded?
- How specific was it?
- What later event corresponds to it?
- Is the fulfillment independently identifiable?
- What does the evidence establish?

If eyewitness testimony is presented, ask:

- Who witnessed what?
- What exactly did they claim?
- When did they testify?
- Were they positioned to know?
- Is there corroborating evidence?
- What inference follows?

The manuscript must never reduce an evidential argument to:

> “The source says it is evidence.”

# 10. THE EASTON TEST

The Easton example establishes a permanent methodological safeguard.

If an external writer says:

> “The Bible takes the existence of God for granted,”

the manuscript must not simply accept or reject the sentence.

It must ask:

1. What does the writer mean by “the Bible”?
2. Has the object being characterized actually been defined?
3. What does the biblical corpus itself present concerning God?
4. What premises support the writer's statement?
5. Does the statement accurately describe the biblical record?
6. Does the writer's philosophical classification correspond to Scripture's own presentation?
7. Has the writer established or merely asserted his conclusion?
8. What evidence does the writer omit?
9. What does the biblical record actually warrant?

The source becomes an **object of investigation**, not a substitute for investigation.

# 14. WITNESS PROTOCOL

- eyewitness;
- witness;

- testimony;
- apostolic testimony;

> **Jesus Christ is the faithful witness.**

# 15. LEXICAL METHOD

Important Hebrew and Greek terms must be treated rigorously.

The preferred sequence is:

> **Original script**
>
> → **transliteration**
>
> → **lexical form**
>
> → **Strong's number where relevant**
>
> → **basic lexical meaning**
>
> → **grammatical form**
>
> → **biblical usage**
>
> → **contextual meaning**
>
> → **argumentative significance.**

Examples include:

### γραφή — *graphē* — G1124

Begin with its lexical meaning of **writing**, then establish its biblical usage in contexts where it denotes Holy Writ / Scripture.

### ἀποκάλυψις — *apokalypsis* — G602

Examine its meaning of unveiling/revelation and its different biblical applications.

### σπουδάζω — *spoudazō* — G4704

Do not reduce the word to the English translation “study.” Examine its lexical range involving earnestness, diligence, effort, promptness, endeavour, and labour, and then interpret its contextual force in 2 Timothy 2:15.

### ἄπειρος — *apeiros* — G552

Where Hebrews 5:13 is relevant, examine the lexical and contextual sense of being inexperienced or unskilled.

Lexical material must illuminate the text, not replace contextual interpretation.

# 17. JOHN 10:34–36 PROTOCOL

The statement:

> **“the scripture cannot be broken”**

must be handled through the actual argument.

The manuscript must examine:

> the accusation against Jesus  
> → Jesus' response  
> → the written wording of Psalm 82:6  
> → **“I said, Ye are gods”**  
> → the reasoning Jesus draws from the written text  
> → **“the scripture cannot be broken”**  
> → why Jesus' conclusion depends upon the authority and integrity of the written Scripture.

The lesson must answer the question:

> **Why can Scripture not be broken?**

Not merely:

> “Because Jesus says it cannot be broken.”

The deeper argument must be developed concerning the nature of truth, God's truthfulness, and the authority of what has been written.

# 18. GOD, TRUTH, AND SCRIPTURE

The manuscript must avoid circular reasoning when establishing Scripture as authoritative.

Do not begin:

> “Scripture is God's Word because Scripture says it is God's Word.”

Instead, the cumulative argument must develop the relevant premises.

The reasoning should include questions such as:

- What is truth?
- What grounds truth?
- Why is God the ground of truth?
- What is God's relationship to truth?
- Is God capable of falsehood?
- Why can God not lie?
- What follows if God communicates?
- How can divine revelation be authenticated?
- What evidence supports the Bible's claim to divine origin?
- What does inspiration guarantee?
- Why may Scripture be regarded as an infallible record?

Biblical self-testimony is important, but it must be situated within the larger evidential and logical case.

- corroboration;

# 21. THE “WHY?” LADDER

Every major conclusion must survive repeated questioning.

For example:

> **Why is Scripture authoritative?**

If the answer is:

> “Because Scripture is God's Word,”

ask:

> **Why should Scripture be regarded as God's Word?**

If the answer is:

> “Because Scripture says so,”

ask:

> **What establishes the reliability of that testimony?**

Continue until the necessary evidential, ontological, epistemological, or logical foundation has been exposed.

This prevents conclusions from being smuggled into premises.

# 23. SOURCE-INTEGRATION CLASSIFICATION

When new material is supplied, classify its function.

It may:

- **strengthen** an existing argument;
- **clarify** a definition;
- **correct** an earlier formulation;
- **complete** an incomplete argument;
- **qualify** a conclusion;
- **cross-link** separate arguments;
- **challenge** an assumption;
- or **generate deeper inquiry**.

The question is not:

> “Where can this quotation be inserted?”

The question is:

> **“What does this material establish, what question does it answer, what question does it raise, what premise does it strengthen, and where does that function properly belong in the existing architecture?”**

---

# 24. ANTI-DUPLICATION PROTOCOL

Do not repeat an argument simply because another source expresses it differently.

Before adding material, ask:

- Has this proposition already been established?
- Is this genuinely new evidence?
- Is it a clarification?
- Is it a stronger demonstration?
- Does it answer a previously unanswered question?
- Does it belong in a later chapter?

Repetition is justified when it serves a new argumentative function, not merely because the source is interesting.

---

# 25. PREMATURE-CONCLUSION PROTOCOL

A chapter must not consume conclusions that depend upon later chapters.

For example:

- do not assume Scripture's infallibility before authenticating Scripture;
- do not assume canon before examining canonical recognition;
- do not use biblical testimony as the only authentication of biblical testimony before establishing the relevant premises;
- do not perform advanced hermeneutics before establishing the nature and authority of the text;
- do not use the six First Principles to prove the foundations upon which the six principles depend.

The TOC is a **knowledge-flow architecture**, not merely a list of subjects.

---

# 31. SOURCE CLAIMS VS. BIBLICAL CLAIMS

Never write:

> “The source says…”

as a substitute for analysis.

Instead:

1. identify the source's proposition;
2. state the proposition clearly;
3. identify its supporting evidence;
4. examine its reasoning;
5. compare it with Scripture;
6. identify agreement, tension, omission, or error;
7. determine the warranted conclusion.

A source may be used because it has discovered something valuable without making the source itself the authority.

---

# 37. THE COMPLETENESS STANDARD

The manuscript is **not complete because it contains many facts**.

It is complete when:

> **the reader can follow the argument and see why each major conclusion follows.**

The manuscript should therefore continuously answer:

- What is being claimed?
- What does the term mean?
- Why is the claim important?
- What evidence supports it?
- What biblical record bears upon it?
- What premises are required?
- What reasoning connects the evidence to the conclusion?
- What alternative explanation exists?
- Why does the conclusion follow?
- What new question does that conclusion create?

---

# 38. THE FINAL MASTER FORMULA

For every major conclusion:

> **Do not merely tell the reader what to conclude.**
>
> **Give the reader the definitions, evidence, biblical record, reasoning, distinctions, and inferential steps necessary to see why the conclusion follows.**

And:

> **Do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture.**
>
> **Teach the reader to reason through Scripture.**

This is the controlling methodological principle of the entire book.
