# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 82 — Rightly Dividing the Word of Truth

> **Movement X — The Scholarly Labour of the Graphē**

> **Central objective:** Develop the requirement to handle the Word accurately and responsibly. Examine 2 Timothy 2:15, the command to “rightly divide the word of truth,” the Greek term **ὀρθοτομέω — *orthotomeō***, and the relationship between correct perception, meaning, distinction, context, reasoning, application, and faithful handling of the written Word.

---

## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 74 established that Scripture creates a scholarly impetus: the writing requires serious intellectual labour.

Chapter 75 established perception, learning, and understanding.

Chapter 76 established reasoning through the Word.

Chapter 77 established quotation, meaning, and knowledge.

Chapter 78 established **σπουδάζω — *spoudazō***: diligence, endeavour, labour, and study.

Chapter 79 established exercise through responsible use.

Chapter 80 established maturity, skill, strong meat, and trained discernment.

Chapter 81 established the condition of the babe and the unskilful.

The sequence has therefore advanced:

> **Perceive → Learn → Understand → Reason → Quote → Know → Diligence → Use → Exercise → Skill → Maturity → Discernment.**

The preceding chapter ended with a necessary question:

> **What does it mean to rightly divide the Word of Truth?**

That question is now before us.

The phrase occurs in a particular setting.

Paul writes to Timothy:

> “Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth.”

The verse contains several ideas that must not be collapsed into one.

There is diligence.

There is presentation before God.

There is approval.

There is work.

There is the possibility of shame.

There is the Word of Truth.

And there is the requirement to **rightly divide** it.

The present chapter is principally concerned with the last of these.

The next chapter will examine the approved workman more fully.

That boundary matters.

The question here is:

> **What does faithful handling of the Word require?**

Not merely:

> What does it mean to study?

Not merely:

> What does it mean to be approved?

Not merely:

> What does it mean to be a worker?

But:

> **What does it mean to handle the Word of Truth rightly?**

---

# 82.1 The Command Is Directed Toward the Word

The object of the command is not vague religious activity.

Paul identifies:

> **“the word of truth.”**

That expression establishes the field in which the labour occurs.

The worker is not being told merely to think carefully about life.

He is not merely being told to become intellectually disciplined in the abstract.

He is not merely being told to acquire theological vocabulary.

He is being directed toward the **Word of Truth**.

This continues the architecture already established in the previous chapters.

The learner does not become the source of truth by studying.

The learner does not become the authority over Scripture by understanding.

The learner does not create revelation by reasoning.

The learner does not make the Word true by accepting it.

Rather:

> **The learner's intellectual labour is brought under the authority of the Word being handled.**

This is why the phrase “rightly dividing” must not be detached from “the word of truth.”

The object governs the operation.

The writing is not raw material for the learner to reshape according to preference.

The learner is responsible for handling what has been given.

---

# 82.2 “Rightly Dividing” Is an Operation

The English expression “rightly dividing” can easily produce a misleading picture.

A modern reader may imagine cutting a document into pieces.

That image is not sufficient.

The Greek verb in 2 Timothy 2:15 is:

> **ὀρθοτομέω — *orthotomeō***

The Bible Introduction supplied for this work identifies **G3718**, gives the basic sense **“set straight; handle right,”** and treats the expression as involving correct meaning and correct application.

The lexical observation is important.

The word itself does not give us permission to impose an elaborate theory upon the verse merely because the English word “divide” can suggest one.

The first task is to understand the operation being commanded.

The basic conceptual movement is:

> **straight → correct → rightly handled.**

The question is therefore not simply:

> “What can I divide?”

It is:

> **“Am I handling the Word in the right way?”**

That is a more fundamental question.

---

# 82.3 The Word “Divide” Must Not Govern the Greek

English translations are valuable witnesses to meaning, but a translated expression must not become a substitute for examination of the underlying term.

“Rightly dividing” is traditional English wording.

The Greek verb is **ὀρθοτομέω**.

Therefore the investigation must move in the proper order:

> **English translation → Greek expression → lexical meaning → contextual force → biblical argument → warranted conclusion.**

The reverse operation is dangerous.

If someone begins with the English word “divide,” constructs a theory of division from that word alone, and then forces the Greek verb to support the theory, the process has already become circular.

The translation has become the authority over the original expression.

That is precisely the kind of reversal that the present chapter is warning against.

The learner must therefore be willing to ask:

> What does the Greek actually contribute?

Then:

> What does the context require?

Then:

> What conclusion is warranted?

This is reasoning through the Word applied to the Word itself.

---

# 82.4 Straight Handling

The lexical material supplied in the Bible Introduction gives the expression a useful conceptual direction:

> **set straight; handle right.**

That immediately places the emphasis upon correctness.

A crooked handling is one that does not preserve the intended direction of the text.

A distorted handling may retain words while changing their relationship.

A selective handling may quote genuine Scripture while omitting the qualification that controls the statement.

A careless handling may apply a statement to a person, time, class, or circumstance for which the text has not established it.

A speculative handling may move from what Scripture says to what the interpreter wishes Scripture had said.

A faithful handling therefore requires more than possession.

It requires correspondence between:

> **what is written**

and

> **what the interpreter says the writing establishes.**

The closer that correspondence is, the straighter the handling.

---

# 82.5 Right Handling Begins With What Is Written

The first discipline is deceptively simple:

> **Read what is there.**

Before dividing a passage, the learner must perceive it.

Before explaining it, he must know what it says.

Before applying it, he must understand what it means.

Before drawing an inference, he must distinguish the explicit statement from the conclusion.

This preserves the sequence established in earlier chapters:

> **Perception → Learning → Understanding → Reasoning → Handling.**

The operation of rightly dividing does not abolish the earlier stages.

It depends upon them.

A learner cannot rightly handle a proposition he has not accurately perceived.

He cannot correctly apply a meaning he has not understood.

He cannot faithfully infer a conclusion if he has misunderstood the premises.

He cannot distinguish the scope of a statement if he has not identified its qualifiers.

Therefore right handling begins before the formal act of interpretation.

It begins with disciplined attention.

---

# 82.6 Right Handling Preserves Meaning

The Bible Introduction states that the governing interpretive principle is to gather from Scripture itself the precise meaning the writers intended to convey.

That formulation is important because it prevents the interpreter from treating Scripture as a container into which any desired meaning may be poured.

The question is not:

> “What can I make this verse mean?”

The question is:

> **“What meaning does this text communicate?”**

That difference is foundational.

The interpreter is not sovereign over the text.

The interpreter is a reader of the text.

The interpreter may discover.

The interpreter may infer.

The interpreter may compare.

The interpreter may explain.

The interpreter may apply.

But these activities do not give the interpreter permission to manufacture the meaning of the writing.

Thus:

> **Right handling seeks to recover and preserve meaning rather than manufacture meaning.**

---

# 82.7 Meaning Is Not the Same as Application

This distinction must be protected.

A text may have a meaning before the reader applies it.

Application asks what the established meaning requires in a particular circumstance.

Therefore:

> **Meaning → Application**

is not identical to:

> **Application → Meaning.**

If the interpreter begins with a desired application and then adjusts the text until it supports that application, the process has been reversed.

Suppose a passage addresses a particular audience concerning a particular situation.

The first question is:

> What did the statement mean in that setting?

Only after that has been established can the learner ask:

> What principle, if any, follows for another setting?

Application may require additional reasoning.

The additional reasoning must be visible.

Otherwise an application can masquerade as though it were the direct meaning of the text.

---

# 82.8 The Difference Between Meaning and Use

A biblical statement may be correctly understood and incorrectly used.

The words may be quoted accurately.

The meaning may even be known.

Yet the statement may be transferred to a different question without sufficient warrant.

This is why Chapter 77 distinguished quotation from meaning and meaning from knowledge.

It is also why Chapter 76 distinguished textual propositions from inferences.

Right handling brings those distinctions together.

The learner asks:

1. What is written?
2. What does it mean?
3. What does it establish?
4. What does it not establish?
5. What inference follows?
6. What application follows?
7. What additional premises are required for that application?

This prevents the common collapse in which a reader moves directly from:

> **verse**

to:

> **personal conclusion**

without showing the reasoning between them.

---

# 82.9 The Context Controls the Operation

Right handling cannot be separated from context.

A word belongs to a sentence.

A sentence belongs to an argument.

An argument belongs to a passage.

A passage belongs to a book.

A book belongs to a larger corpus.

The reader therefore needs to know not merely the words but their relationships.

This does not mean every passage requires an encyclopedic reconstruction of the entire Bible.

It means the interpreter must provide enough context to prevent the statement from being detached from the factors that determine its meaning.

Relevant questions include:

> Who is speaking?

> Who is being addressed?

> What is being discussed?

> What has already been said?

> What follows?

> What problem is being answered?

> What terms are qualified?

> What conditions are stated?

> What contrasts are made?

> What conclusion is being reached?

These are not academic decorations.

They are instruments of right handling.

---

# 82.10 Speaker, Audience, and Subject

Three questions often expose an incorrect application:

> **Who said it?**

> **To whom was it said?**

> **What was being discussed?**

The same sentence can function differently depending upon these relationships.

A statement made by God is not identical in function to a statement merely reported by a narrator.

A command to a particular person is not automatically a command to every reader in precisely the same form.

A promise to a particular recipient cannot be transferred indiscriminately without examining its stated scope.

A historical statement is not automatically a universal command.

A question is not automatically a proposition.

An accusation is not automatically a divine judgment.

A quotation by an adversary is not automatically divine endorsement of the adversary's argument.

Right handling therefore preserves the communicative structure of the text.

---

# 82.11 Narrative Is Not Automatically Command

The Bible contains narrative.

Narrative records actions, words, events, failures, successes, conflicts, judgments, and experiences.

But recording an event does not automatically command the reader to reproduce it.

If a biblical character lies, the record of the lie is not itself a command to lie.

If a person acts foolishly, the narrative does not automatically commend the action.

If an individual makes a theological claim, the mere fact that the statement appears in Scripture does not necessarily mean the narrator endorses the claim.

The interpreter must distinguish:

> **reported speech**

from:

> **endorsed instruction.**

This is a basic requirement of right handling.

---

# 82.12 Quotation Is Not Endorsement

Chapter 77 established this distinction.

The Bible records statements made by many persons.

Some statements are true.

Some are false.

Some are incomplete.

Some are accusations.

Some are questions.

Some are objections.

Some are speeches whose errors are later exposed.

Therefore:

> **The presence of words in the biblical record does not by itself establish that the speaker's proposition is endorsed as doctrine.**

This is particularly important when handling long speeches or disputed statements.

The interpreter must ask:

> Who is speaking?

> Is the statement being affirmed by the biblical narrator?

> Is the statement subsequently corrected?

> Does the surrounding narrative expose it as error?

> What function does the quotation serve?

This is right division at the level of speech.

---

# 82.13 The Temptation Narrative Demonstrates the Problem

The temptation narratives provide a particularly important warning.

The adversary quotes Scripture.

The quotation is recognizable.

The words come from Scripture.

Yet the argument constructed from the quotation is not thereby legitimate.

Christ answers by Scripture.

The lesson is not that Scripture can be quoted wrongly because Scripture is unreliable.

The lesson is precisely the opposite.

The authoritative writing can be used within an erroneous argument by a person who mishandles its application.

Therefore:

> **Correct quotation does not guarantee correct handling.**

This reinforces Chapter 77:

> **An authoritative quotation is not a substitute for understanding.**

And it reinforces Chapter 76:

> **A correct premise can be placed inside an incorrect inferential structure.**

The danger is therefore not only misquotation.

It is also misapplication.

---

# 82.14 “For,” “Therefore,” and the Direction of Thought

Right handling pays attention to the connective tissue of Scripture.

Words such as:

> **for**

> **therefore**

> **because**

> **but**

> **if**

> **that**

> **so that**

> **wherefore**

often reveal relationships between propositions.

The reader who removes those relationships may preserve the vocabulary while losing the argument.

A passage may say:

> proposition A

> **therefore**

> proposition B.

If the interpreter quotes A but ignores the reason for B, he may produce a conclusion the author did not intend.

Likewise, if a statement is conditional:

> **if X, then Y**

it must not be handled as:

> **Y in every circumstance.**

If the text contains a contrast:

> **not A, but B**

the interpreter must not retain A while ignoring B.

Right handling therefore includes attention to logical direction.

---

# 82.15 Conditions Matter

Conditions are part of meaning.

A promise containing a condition cannot be treated as though the condition were absent.

A warning containing a condition cannot be generalized beyond the stated relationship without additional reasoning.

An instruction limited by circumstance cannot automatically be universalized.

This is one reason the learner must be trained to identify:

- conditions;
- exceptions;
- qualifications;
- negations;
- temporal markers;
- scope;
- and contrasts.

These details are not obstacles to faith.

They are part of what the text communicates.

---

# 82.16 “All Scripture” Does Not Mean “Every Statement Functions the Same Way”

The Bible is Scripture.

But its contents include:

- commands;
- promises;
- prophecy;
- narrative;
- wisdom;
- poetry;
- testimony;
- questions;
- parables;
- letters;
- historical reports;
- warnings;
- genealogies;
- songs;
- visions;
- symbolic language;
- direct divine speech;
- human speech reported within the narrative.

The authority of the corpus does not erase these distinctions.

Instead, right handling requires the learner to recognize them.

A command is handled as a command.

A prophecy is handled as prophecy.

A proverb is not automatically treated as an unconditional mathematical formula.

A vision is not automatically flattened into ordinary prose.

A figure of speech is not treated as though it were a literal physical description merely because the interpreter prefers literal vocabulary.

The Bible Introduction therefore rightly emphasizes a disciplined use of language and context.

The question is not:

> “Literal or figurative?”

as a slogan.

The question is:

> **“What kind of language is being used, and what truth is that language communicating?”**

---

# 82.17 Literal Language and Figurative Language

The supplied Bible Introduction states that the Bible should be taken literally wherever possible and that when language cannot be taken literally, the reader should recognize figurative expression and seek the literal truth conveyed by that figurative language.

The useful principle is not a mechanical rule that every sentence must be flattened into physical literalism.

The useful principle is:

> **Do not invent figurative meaning where ordinary language already communicates clearly, and do not force literalism upon language whose form and context clearly communicate figuratively.**

A metaphor communicates something real.

A symbol communicates something real.

A figure of speech communicates something real.

The presence of figurative language does not weaken truth.

The error lies in confusing the vehicle of communication with the thing communicated.

Thus the interpreter asks:

> What is the expression?

> What does it communicate?

> What signals its figurative force?

> Does the surrounding context explain it?

> Does the text itself interpret it?

> What literal proposition does the figurative expression communicate?

This is right handling.

---

# 82.18 “Divide” Does Not Mean Destroy Unity

There is another danger.

If rightly dividing is understood merely as separating passages from one another, the result may be fragmentation.

The Bible must not be “divided” in such a way that its parts become mutually independent authorities.

Right handling distinguishes without destroying relationships.

It separates where the text distinguishes.

It connects where the text connects.

It compares where comparison is warranted.

It refuses to merge what the text keeps distinct.

Therefore:

> **Right division requires both distinction and relationship.**

A learner who only separates can become fragmented.

A learner who only harmonizes can erase distinctions.

Skill requires both.

---

# 82.19 Distinction Is Not Contradiction

Two passages may describe different circumstances without contradicting one another.

Two commands may address different audiences.

Two promises may have different conditions.

Two narratives may emphasize different details.

Two statements may appear different because they answer different questions.

The interpreter must therefore resist the premature conclusion:

> “These statements differ, therefore Scripture contradicts itself.”

Chapter 76 already established the discipline of distinguishing contradiction from apparent tension.

Chapter 82 applies that discipline to handling the text.

Ask:

> Are the propositions actually contradictory?

> Are they speaking about the same subject?

> In the same sense?

> At the same time?

> Under the same conditions?

> Concerning the same audience?

> With the same scope?

If not, the apparent contradiction may disappear.

This is not forced harmonization.

It is logical examination.

---

# 82.20 Comparison Must Be Controlled

Scripture can illuminate Scripture.

But cross-reference is not automatically cross-proof.

A passage may contain similar vocabulary without establishing the same proposition.

A similar event may illustrate a principle without proving identity.

A parallel passage may illuminate context without erasing differences.

Therefore:

> **Similarity is not entailment.**

Right handling asks:

> Why does this passage belong here?

> What relationship does it actually have to the passage being interpreted?

> Does it establish the proposition, illuminate it, contrast with it, or merely resemble it?

This distinction protects the reader from chains of attractive but unsupported associations.

---

# 82.21 The Whole Proposition Must Be Preserved

A quotation can be technically accurate and still be materially misleading.

Consider the difference between quoting:

> **“If…”**

and quoting the conclusion while omitting the condition.

Or quoting:

> **“not…”**

while removing the negation.

Or quoting one clause while ignoring the clause that qualifies it.

The words may all be biblical.

The resulting proposition may not be.

Therefore the interpreter must preserve the **whole proposition**.

This is one of the most practical meanings of right handling.

Do not merely preserve biblical vocabulary.

Preserve biblical relationships.

---

# 82.22 Scope Must Be Preserved

Every statement has some scope.

The scope may concern:

- a person;
- a group;
- Israel;
- the nations;
- disciples;
- apostles;
- a church;
- a particular congregation;
- a historical generation;
- believers generally;
- unbelievers;
- rulers;
- teachers;
- parents;
- children;
- husbands;
- wives;
- or another defined class.

Right handling asks:

> **Who falls within the statement's stated scope?**

The answer must come from the text and warranted inference.

The interpreter must not enlarge a statement merely because enlargement makes a preferred doctrine easier to defend.

Nor should the interpreter shrink a statement merely because its full scope is uncomfortable.

Both are forms of mishandling.

---

# 82.23 Time Must Be Preserved

Biblical statements occur in time.

A command may be given before or after a major event.

A promise may concern a future event.

A prophecy may speak about a later fulfillment.

A historical instruction may belong to a particular stage in the biblical story.

Therefore the reader must ask:

> **When does this statement operate?**

Time does not automatically determine whether a statement remains relevant.

But time is part of the evidence needed to determine how it is relevant.

The interpreter must not treat every temporal marker as disposable.

Nor should a later application be confused with the original historical reference.

---

# 82.24 “Classes of People” Matter

The Bible Introduction specifically emphasizes correct application with regard to different times and classes of people.

This is a useful interpretive safeguard.

Not every biblical statement addresses the same class.

A command given to an apostle is not automatically a command to every Christian in precisely the same form.

A statement concerning kings is not automatically a statement concerning children.

An instruction concerning teachers may have responsibilities not identical to those of ordinary hearers.

Right handling therefore asks:

> **Who is the grammatical and contextual recipient?**

Then:

> **What is the justified relationship between that recipient and the present reader?**

Application may be transferable.

But transfer requires reasoning.

---

# 82.25 Subjects Must Be Distinguished

A passage may use the same word in connection with different subjects.

The word does not automatically carry the same contextual force every time.

Likewise, the same subject may be treated differently in different passages.

The learner must therefore distinguish:

> **word → sense → proposition → subject → context.**

This is why lexical study cannot replace contextual interpretation.

A dictionary may give a range of meanings.

The passage determines the contextual force.

Right handling therefore avoids two opposite errors:

> **dictionary absolutism**

and

> **contextual subjectivism.**

The first says the dictionary mechanically determines the meaning.

The second says the interpreter may invent any meaning he desires because “context” is flexible.

Neither is acceptable.

---

# 82.26 The Bible Interprets Its Own Terms Through Usage

A word's meaning is not established merely by its English translation.

Nor is it established merely by a lexical gloss.

The reader must observe how the biblical writers use the expression.

This was already required in Chapter 78 when *spoudazō* was examined.

The same principle applies here.

The interpreter should ask:

> What is the original form?

> What is its grammatical function?

> What semantic range is possible?

> What sense fits this context?

> How is the term used elsewhere?

> Does the present passage constrain its meaning?

This is not an invitation to make every interpretation depend upon Greek or Hebrew expertise.

It is a warning against pretending that English surface vocabulary settles every question.

---

# 82.27 Right Handling Is Not Greek-Word Worship

The opposite error must also be rejected.

An interpreter can misuse original-language vocabulary just as easily as English vocabulary.

A Greek word is not a magic key.

A lexicon entry is not revelation.

Strong's numbering is not interpretation.

A grammatical label is not an argument by itself.

The presence of a Greek term does not automatically prove a doctrinal conclusion.

The correct sequence remains:

> **Form → lexical range → grammar → context → proposition → reasoning → conclusion.**

The language tools serve the text.

They do not become an independent authority over it.

---

# 82.28 Right Handling Requires Distinction Between Text and Interpretation

This distinction is central to the whole book.

There is:

> **the text**

and:

> **the interpretation of the text.**

The text says something.

The interpreter explains what it means.

That explanation may be correct.

It may be mistaken.

It may be partly correct.

It may be incomplete.

Therefore:

> **The authority of Scripture does not transfer automatically to the interpreter's explanation of Scripture.**

This protects both sides of the argument.

The learner must not treat every interpretation as equally valid.

But neither may he treat his own interpretation as infallible merely because the source is infallible.

The text remains the authority.

The interpreter remains responsible.

---

# 82.29 Right Handling Requires Distinction Between Interpretation and Inference

Interpretation asks:

> What does the text mean?

Inference asks:

> What follows from what the text means?

These are related but not identical.

Suppose a text explicitly states proposition A.

An interpreter may infer proposition B from A.

If B is a necessary inference, it may be strongly warranted.

But B should not be presented as though the text explicitly said B.

This is why Chapter 76 established:

> **Explicit statement ≠ inference.**

Right handling preserves that distinction.

It does not weaken the authority of necessary inference.

It strengthens intellectual honesty.

The learner can say:

> “The text explicitly states this.”

Or:

> “This conclusion follows from the text.”

Those are different statements.

Both may be authoritative in different senses, but they must not be confused.

---

# 82.30 Right Handling Requires Limits

A disciplined interpreter must know where to stop.

This is one of the most important consequences of the previous chapters.

A passage may establish A.

A may support B.

B may make C plausible.

But if the evidence does not establish D, the interpreter must stop at C.

Right handling is therefore not the art of finding the maximum number of conclusions that can be extracted from a passage.

It is the discipline of drawing the conclusions that the passage and its warranted relationships support.

Thus:

> **Faithful interpretation includes disciplined restraint.**

The ability to stop is a form of skill.

---

# 82.31 Right Handling Does Not Mean Saying Less Than the Text

Restraint has an opposite danger.

An interpreter may become so afraid of inference that he refuses to acknowledge conclusions Scripture actually establishes.

That is also mishandling.

If the text explicitly teaches something, the interpreter should not weaken it into mere possibility.

If a necessary inference follows, the interpreter should not pretend that nothing can be known.

The proper discipline is proportionality:

> **Say what the text says.**

> **Infer what follows.**

> **Do not add what does not follow.**

> **Do not subtract what does follow.**

That is straight handling.

---

# 82.32 Right Handling Is Not Mere Literalism

The Bible Introduction's emphasis on taking the Bible literally wherever possible is useful when it means that the interpreter should not escape clear statements by inventing unnecessary spiritualized meanings.

But “literal” must itself be handled responsibly.

A literal reading recognizes ordinary communication.

If Jesus says:

> “I am the door,”

the reader does not need to conclude that Christ is made of timber.

The figure communicates something real.

The question is therefore not whether the sentence can be physically literal.

The question is:

> **What proposition does the figure communicate?**

A disciplined interpreter takes the language seriously without confusing figurative form with falsehood.

---

# 82.33 Right Handling Is Not Allegory Without Control

The opposite error is uncontrolled allegorization.

If every concrete statement can be transformed into whatever spiritual meaning the interpreter prefers, the text loses authority because the interpreter has become the source of meaning.

The result is not deeper spirituality.

It is interpretive sovereignty.

Right handling therefore requires evidence for figurative interpretation.

Ask:

> Does the text signal the figure?

> Does the genre support it?

> Does the context require it?

> Does the text explain the symbol?

> Does the surrounding argument constrain the meaning?

If the answer is no, invention has entered the process.

---

# 82.34 Right Handling Preserves the Author's Argument

A passage is more than a collection of sentences.

The writer may be:

- introducing a question;
- presenting an objection;
- answering an objection;
- establishing a premise;
- drawing a conclusion;
- giving an example;
- making a contrast;
- issuing a warning;
- giving a command;
- explaining a consequence.

Right handling therefore asks:

> **Where is the writer going?**

A verse extracted from an argument can be made to answer a question it was never designed to answer.

The interpreter must follow the movement of the passage.

This is why connectives matter.

This is why paragraphs matter.

This is why questions matter.

This is why conclusions matter.

Right handling is therefore partly the recovery of the argument's direction.

---

# 82.35 Questions Are Part of the Argument

A question in Scripture may contain an argument.

It may challenge an assumption.

It may expose ignorance.

It may introduce an objection.

It may invite the hearer to draw a conclusion.

It may be rhetorical.

It may be genuine.

The interpreter must not automatically turn every question into a doctrinal proposition.

The question:

> “Have ye not read…?”

does not function identically to a declarative proposition.

Yet it may expose a premise or demonstrate that the hearers have failed to understand something already written.

Right handling therefore asks:

> What work is the question doing?

---

# 82.36 Commands Are Not Mere Information

A command has a different function from a historical statement.

When Scripture commands, the interpreter must identify:

- the commanded action;
- the recipient;
- the conditions;
- the purpose;
- the scope;
- and the context.

The command must not be reduced to a descriptive fact.

At the same time, the interpreter must not automatically universalize every command without examining its recipient and setting.

Right handling preserves both authority and scope.

---

# 82.37 Promises Require the Same Discipline

A promise is also not merely information.

The reader must ask:

> To whom is it promised?

> Under what conditions?

> What does the promise actually contain?

> Is it temporal or future?

> Is it individual, communal, national, covenantal, or otherwise specified?

> What does the surrounding passage say?

The interpreter must not weaken a genuine promise.

But neither may he transfer a promise to a recipient or circumstance without warrant.

Right handling therefore protects promises from both unbelieving reduction and careless appropriation.

---

# 82.38 Prophecy Requires Time and Fulfilment

Prophetic language often requires attention to:

- historical setting;
- audience;
- temporal markers;
- immediate context;
- later fulfilment;
- symbolic language;
- repeated or extended fulfilment;
- and the testimony of later Scripture.

The interpreter must not treat every future-oriented statement as though its timing were obvious.

Nor may he declare a fulfilment merely because an event resembles a prophetic phrase.

Similarity is not fulfilment.

The claim of fulfilment itself requires evidence.

Thus:

> **Rightly dividing prophecy requires disciplined correspondence between prediction, context, and claimed fulfilment.**

---

# 82.39 Historical Statements Require Historical Reading

The Bible contains historical testimony.

Historical statements should be read as historical claims.

That means asking:

> Who is involved?

> What happened?

> Where?

> When?

> Who witnessed it?

> What does the text actually report?

The interpreter must not turn every historical detail into a universal rule.

Nor should he dismiss historical details as irrelevant simply because they are not commands.

Historical testimony can establish the context in which later teaching is understood.

Right handling therefore respects history as part of the text's meaning.

---

# 82.40 Right Handling and Christ

Christ provides a central model of engagement with Scripture.

He repeatedly reasons from what is written.

He asks:

> “Have ye not read…?”

He answers challenges with Scripture.

He distinguishes what Scripture says from the traditions used to override it.

He reasons through wording and implication.

He identifies errors by returning to the written record.

This does not mean every modern interpretive procedure can be mechanically attributed to Christ.

It means the Gospels demonstrate a pattern of serious engagement with the written Word.

The written text matters.

Its wording matters.

Its context matters.

Its implications matter.

And the interpreter must submit his claim to what is written.

---

# 82.41 Christ Does Not Replace the Written Word With Private Interpretation

This point is essential to the Doctrine of the Graphē.

Christ's authority does not make human interpretation unnecessary.

Rather, Christ demonstrates the authority and usefulness of the written Word.

The learner therefore does not have to choose between:

> **Christ**

and:

> **Scripture.**

The biblical record presents Christ engaging Scripture as authoritative.

This strengthens the architecture already established in the book:

> **Christ → Scripture → interpretation under Scripture.**

The learner must not use an appeal to spiritual authority as permission to disregard the writing.

---

# 82.42 Right Handling and Tradition

Tradition can preserve useful knowledge.

Tradition can also preserve error.

The existence of a long-established interpretation does not itself establish its truth.

Right handling therefore asks:

> What does the tradition claim?

> What evidence supports it?

> What does Scripture explicitly say?

> What inference does the tradition make?

> Does the inference follow?

> Has a later interpretation become primary authority over the text?

This continues the earlier chapters concerning authority and secondary sources.

The issue is not whether tradition exists.

The issue is whether tradition is being allowed to govern the Word rather than being examined under the Word.

---

# 82.43 Right Handling and Commentaries

Commentaries can be useful.

They may identify historical information.

They may explain grammatical issues.

They may compare interpretations.

They may preserve observations that would otherwise be missed.

But a commentary is not the Graphē.

The interpreter must therefore distinguish:

> **“The text says…”**

from:

> **“This commentator explains…”**

and:

> **“I infer…”**

This threefold distinction is one of the safeguards of scholarly integrity.

The more authoritative the source, the more important it becomes to preserve the distinction.

---

# 82.44 Right Handling and Institutional Authority

Institutions may teach.

Institutions may preserve traditions.

Institutions may train students.

Institutions may establish curricula.

None of those functions automatically transfers authority over the Graphē.

A learner may respect a teacher while remaining responsible for examining what the teacher teaches.

Likewise, rejecting institutional authority does not make the learner automatically correct.

The proper posture is neither:

> **blind dependence**

nor:

> **arrogant independence.**

It is:

> **responsible examination under the written Word.**

---

# 82.45 Right Handling Requires Correctability

The mature learner developed in Chapters 80 and 81 is correctable.

That quality becomes essential here.

If an interpreter cannot revise an interpretation when the evidence requires revision, his interpretation has become more authoritative to him than the text.

That is a reversal of order.

A learner may have spent years developing an interpretation.

He may have taught it.

He may have written books about it.

He may have built an institution around it.

None of these facts make the interpretation infallible.

The question remains:

> **Does the Word actually establish it?**

Right handling therefore requires intellectual humility.

---

# 82.46 Right Handling Requires Evidence Against One's Own Interpretation

The interpreter should not search only for confirming texts.

A disciplined reader asks:

> What passage appears difficult for my interpretation?

> What evidence could falsify or qualify my conclusion?

> Am I ignoring a condition?

> Am I assuming a definition?

> Am I using a quotation outside its scope?

> Does another passage actually contradict my reading?

> Have I explained the apparent difficulty or merely hidden it?

This is not unbelief.

It is intellectual responsibility.

The truth does not need protection from examination.

The interpretation may need correction.

---

# 82.47 Right Handling and Apparent Contradictions

When two passages appear to conflict, the learner should not immediately choose one and discard the other.

He should first reconstruct both.

Ask:

1. What exactly does passage A say?
2. What exactly does passage B say?
3. Are they speaking about the same proposition?
4. Are the terms being used in the same sense?
5. Are the conditions identical?
6. Are the audiences identical?
7. Are the times identical?
8. Is one statement a quotation rather than the narrator's assertion?
9. Is one passage explaining what the other assumes?
10. Is the contradiction real or only apparent?

This method protects the learner from both forced harmonization and premature rejection.

---

# 82.48 Right Handling Does Not Mean Harmonizing by Invention

There is a temptation whenever two passages appear difficult:

> “They must harmonize, so I will invent an explanation.”

That is not sufficient.

A proposed harmonization is itself an interpretation.

It requires evidence.

If the text does not establish the proposed mechanism, the interpreter should say so.

He may identify a possible explanation.

He may identify a probable explanation where the evidence supports it.

But he must distinguish:

> **possible**

from:

> **necessary**

and:

> **established.**

This is the discipline established in Chapter 76.

---

# 82.49 Right Handling Does Not Mean Fragmenting Scripture

The opposite temptation is:

> “These passages differ, so they must belong to completely unrelated systems.”

Again, the conclusion must be established.

The learner must not create divisions merely because divisions make a preferred theory easier.

The text itself must establish the relevant distinction.

Therefore:

> **Divide where Scripture distinguishes. Connect where Scripture connects.**

This concise rule captures an important aspect of right handling.

---

# 82.50 Right Handling and “All Scriptures on a Subject”

The Bible Introduction states that Scriptures on a subject should be harmonized.

The methodological value of that statement is significant if “harmonized” means that relevant passages are brought into comparison rather than one passage being allowed to erase another.

A responsible investigation therefore gathers relevant evidence.

But gathering passages is not enough.

The interpreter must ask whether each passage is actually about the same proposition.

The danger of a concordance-driven method is that a word can appear in many places while its contextual meaning differs.

Therefore:

> **Collect broadly, compare carefully, conclude narrowly.**

That is a useful discipline.

---

# 82.51 Right Handling Is Not Proof-Texting

Proof-texting occurs when a passage is used to support a conclusion without demonstrating the relevant connection between the passage and the conclusion.

A proof text may be:

- accurate;
- relevant in a broad sense;
- familiar;
- powerful;
- and still insufficient to prove the specific proposition being asserted.

Right handling therefore asks:

> What exactly does this passage prove?

Not:

> Can I make this passage sound like support?

The distinction is crucial.

---

# 82.52 The Question “What Does This Establish?” Must Become Habit

The mature learner should repeatedly ask:

> **What does this establish?**

Then:

> **What does it not establish?**

This two-question discipline prevents enormous amounts of interpretive error.

For example, a passage may establish:

> A.

It may support:

> B.

It may suggest:

> C.

But it may not establish:

> D.

The interpreter must not allow the emotional force of A to carry D across an evidential gap.

Right handling is therefore partly the discipline of measuring conclusions against grounds.

---

# 82.53 Right Handling and Application to Modern Life

Application is necessary.

But application must be constructed honestly.

A passage may establish a principle.

The present circumstance may require additional reasoning.

For example:

> **Textual principle → modern circumstance → additional premises → application.**

The interpreter should not hide the additional premises.

This is especially important because application can feel obvious while actually containing substantial assumptions.

A mature learner can say:

> “The text explicitly establishes X. From X, and given Y about the present circumstance, the appropriate application is Z.”

That is stronger than pretending:

> “The Bible explicitly says Z.”

Right handling preserves the boundary.

---

# 82.54 Principle Is Not Always a Rule

A biblical principle may have broad relevance without being a direct command in every circumstance.

A rule may specify an action.

A principle may reveal a pattern of judgment.

The interpreter must not transform every principle into a mechanical rule.

Nor should he reduce explicit commands to vague principles merely to avoid obedience.

The question remains:

> What kind of statement is this?

That classification helps determine how it should be applied.

---

# 82.55 Right Handling and Silence

Silence is difficult.

If Scripture does not address a question explicitly, the interpreter must not pretend that it does.

An inference may still be possible.

But the inference must be identified as an inference.

The absence of a statement is not automatically proof of absence.

Nor is silence permission to invent a doctrine.

Therefore:

> **Scriptural silence requires epistemic discipline.**

The learner must know when he has:

> explicit teaching,

> necessary inference,

> reasonable application,

> or unresolved uncertainty.

These categories should not be collapsed.

---

# 82.56 Right Handling and “The Bible Says”

The phrase:

> “The Bible says…”

should carry responsibility.

If the Bible explicitly says it, the phrase is appropriate.

If the interpreter has inferred it, the wording should reveal that.

Instead of:

> “The Bible says X,”

the intellectually honest form may be:

> “From these passages, it appears that X follows.”

That does not weaken the conclusion.

It tells the reader how the conclusion was reached.

The distinction between source and interpreter remains visible.

---

# 82.57 Right Handling and Doctrinal Synthesis

Doctrine often requires synthesis.

A doctrine may depend upon several passages.

That is legitimate.

But synthesis must not be disguised as quotation.

If five passages together support a doctrinal conclusion, the interpreter should say so.

He should not quote one sentence and imply that it explicitly contains the entire synthesis.

The process should be visible:

> **Passage A → proposition A**

> **Passage B → proposition B**

> **Passage C → proposition C**

> **relationship among A, B, C → conclusion D**

This is reasoning through Scripture.

---

# 82.58 Right Handling Does Not Create New Revelation

This boundary is essential.

The interpreter may discover an implication of Scripture.

He may formulate a doctrinal synthesis.

He may recognize a relationship previously overlooked.

None of those activities automatically makes the resulting statement new revelation.

The distinction is:

> **Revelation gives what was made known.**

> **Interpretation seeks to understand what was given.**

> **Inference draws warranted conclusions from what was given.**

> **Application relates what was established to a circumstance.**

These operations must not be confused.

A new conclusion is not automatically a new revelation.

---

# 82.59 Right Handling and Human Fallibility

The authority of the Graphē does not make every reader infallible.

This has already been established.

Therefore the reader should distinguish:

> **infallible source**

from:

> **fallible interpretation.**

This does not create relativism.

Interpretations can be evaluated.

Some are better supported than others.

Some contradict explicit statements.

Some depend upon unsupported premises.

Some preserve context.

Some destroy it.

The fact that human interpretation is fallible is not an argument for abandoning interpretation.

It is an argument for disciplined interpretation.

---

# 82.60 Right Handling and Scholarly Labour

The command to rightly divide confirms the argument of Movement X.

Serious engagement with Scripture requires labour.

The learner must:

- read;
- reread;
- compare;
- define;
- distinguish;
- trace arguments;
- identify context;
- examine language;
- test inferences;
- identify scope;
- recognize genre;
- examine conditions;
- and revise conclusions when necessary.

This is not academic decoration.

It is part of faithful handling.

The scholarly impetus established in Chapter 74 now has a concrete object.

The learner is not merely studying for intellectual prestige.

He is labouring so that his handling of the Word will correspond to the Word.

---

# 82.61 Right Handling and Exercise

Chapter 79 established:

> **Diligence applies the learner to the work. Exercise develops the learner through the work.**

Chapter 82 shows what the developed capacity must eventually produce.

The learner who exercises the Word should become increasingly capable of handling:

- distinctions;
- arguments;
- contexts;
- conditions;
- quotations;
- inferences;
- applications;
- apparent tensions;
- competing interpretations.

Thus the progression becomes:

> **Diligence → Exercise → Skill → Right Handling.**

The goal is not merely more information.

It is responsible capacity.

---

# 82.62 Right Handling and Maturity

Chapter 80 established that maturity is not measured merely by how much the learner possesses but by what the learner has become capable of doing with what he possesses.

That principle reaches a new stage here.

The mature learner does not merely quote Scripture.

He handles it.

He does not merely know words.

He knows relationships.

He does not merely recognize doctrines.

He can trace their premises.

He does not merely defend an interpretation.

He can examine it.

He does not merely possess confidence.

He possesses disciplined capacity.

Therefore:

> **Right handling is one of the practical demonstrations of developed biblical skill.**

---

# 82.63 Right Handling and the Unskilful

Chapter 81 described the unskilful as one who lacks developed competence in relation to the Word of righteousness.

Chapter 82 now identifies one of the competencies toward which development must move.

The unskilful learner may:

- quote without context;
- apply without scope;
- infer without premises;
- harmonize without evidence;
- divide without distinction;
- compare without relevance;
- literalize without genre;
- spiritualize without control;
- repeat tradition without testing;
- or confuse interpretation with Scripture itself.

These are not merely isolated mistakes.

They reveal undeveloped handling.

The cure is not contempt.

The cure is development through disciplined labour under the Word.

---

# 82.64 Right Handling Is Responsible Handling

The word “rightly” introduces a moral and intellectual seriousness.

The learner is not merely trying to produce an interesting interpretation.

He is responsible for what he does with the Word.

A careless interpretation can affect:

- doctrine;
- teaching;
- practice;
- conscience;
- relationships;
- worship;
- institutions;
- and the lives of others.

Therefore handling Scripture is not an intellectual game.

The more influence an interpreter possesses, the more important disciplined handling becomes.

This is why the next chapter will examine the workman and approval before God.

But the responsibility begins here:

> **The Word must be handled as the Word of Truth.**

---

# 82.65 Right Handling and Error Correction

A correct interpretation is not defined by confidence.

It is defined by correspondence to the text and its warranted meaning.

Therefore correction is part of the discipline.

When evidence exposes an error:

> return to the passage.

When context exposes a limitation:

> revise the claim.

When a word has been overextended:

> narrow the conclusion.

When a cross-reference proves irrelevant:

> remove it.

When an inference is only possible rather than necessary:

> qualify it.

When a conclusion is unsupported:

> stop.

This is not weakness.

It is scholarly honesty under the authority of the Word.

---

# 82.66 Right Handling and Intellectual Pride

The learner can become proud of his interpretation.

He may begin to defend the interpretation because it is his.

At that point the object of protection has changed.

He is no longer simply protecting the truth.

He is protecting his relationship to his own conclusion.

That is dangerous.

A doctrine should not become true because the interpreter has spent years defending it.

A tradition should not become infallible because many people have repeated it.

A commentary should not become Scripture because it has been printed for generations.

Right handling continually returns the learner to the source.

---

# 82.67 Right Handling and Intellectual Laziness

The opposite failure is laziness.

The lazy interpreter wants:

> one verse;

> one definition;

> one quotation;

> one teacher;

> one answer.

But difficult questions may require:

> comparison;

> context;

> language;

> reasoning;

> historical setting;

> multiple passages;

> and patient investigation.

The scholarly labour of the Graphē therefore rejects both extremes:

> **intellectual pride**

and:

> **intellectual laziness.**

One trusts itself too much.

The other works too little.

Both can mishandle the Word.

---

# 82.68 Right Handling and the Teacher

The teacher has a special responsibility.

A teacher can multiply error.

If the teacher presents an inference as explicit Scripture, students may repeat the error as though it were written.

If the teacher quotes a passage outside its context, the students may inherit the distortion.

If the teacher hides uncertainty, students may mistake confidence for certainty.

Therefore a responsible teacher should distinguish:

> **Scripture says…**

> **The passage means…**

> **This follows from the passage…**

> **This is an application…**

> **This is one interpretation…**

These distinctions train students to handle the Word rather than merely repeat the teacher.

---

# 82.69 Right Handling and the Student

The student has responsibility as well.

He should not ask only:

> “What does my teacher believe?”

He should ask:

> “What does the text establish?”

He should not merely collect answers.

He should learn how the answers were reached.

He should ask:

> What is the passage?

> What is the proposition?

> What is the context?

> What is explicit?

> What is inferred?

> What is the evidence?

> What remains uncertain?

This is how the student moves from dependence toward mature capacity.

---

# 82.70 Right Handling and the Word Drill

The Word drill method developed in the Aletheia teaching material is useful as a pedagogical instrument because it forces the learner to work directly with the writing.

But the method itself must never be confused with the Graphē.

The method serves the source.

It does not become the source.

Likewise, this chapter is not the Word.

The definitions and procedures presented here are instruments for helping the learner obey the requirement already present in Scripture.

This distinction protects the architecture:

> **Graphē → method**

not:

> **method → Graphē.**

The tool serves the writing.

---

# 82.71 A Practical Sequence for Right Handling

The preceding chapters now permit a disciplined working sequence.

When approaching a passage, the learner can proceed:

> **1. Locate the passage.**

> **2. Read the passage in context.**

> **3. Identify the speaker and audience.**

> **4. Identify the subject and question being addressed.**

> **5. Observe the actual words and grammatical relationships.**

> **6. Identify commands, promises, questions, assertions, contrasts, conditions, and conclusions.**

> **7. Determine the contextual meaning of important terms.**

> **8. Distinguish explicit statements from inference.**

> **9. Compare relevant passages.**

> **10. Test apparent contradictions or tensions.**

> **11. Identify the premises supporting the conclusion.**

> **12. Ask what the evidence actually establishes.**

> **13. Separate interpretation from application.**

> **14. State qualifications and limits.**

> **15. Stop where the evidence requires stopping.**

This is not presented as a new command from Scripture.

It is a disciplined method for carrying out the requirement to handle the Word rightly.

---

# 82.72 Observe Before You Explain

One of the most important habits is temporal order.

Do not begin with explanation.

Begin with observation.

Ask:

> What is actually there?

Only then:

> What does it mean?

Only then:

> What follows?

Only then:

> What should be done?

This protects the learner from importing conclusions before perceiving the text.

The sequence is:

> **Observe → Understand → Reason → Handle → Apply.**

If application comes first, the text is at risk of becoming a justification mechanism.

---

# 82.73 Define Before You Debate

When interpreters disagree, they often debate conclusions while using key terms differently.

Therefore define the relevant terms.

Ask:

> What does “faith” mean here?

> What does “world” mean here?

> What does “law” mean here?

> What does “salvation” mean here?

> What does “perfect” mean here?

> What does “flesh” mean here?

The point is not to assume that every word has one fixed meaning.

The point is to establish the sense required by the context.

A disagreement may disappear once the terms are clarified.

Or it may become more precise.

Either result is progress.

---

# 82.74 Distinguish the Proposition Before Defending It

A person may say:

> “The Bible teaches X.”

Before defending X, write X precisely.

Then ask:

> Which passage teaches X?

> Does it state X explicitly?

> If not, what premises yield X?

> Are those premises established?

> Does another passage qualify X?

> What exactly does X exclude?

> What does X not claim?

A vague doctrine can survive indefinitely because no one states it precisely enough to test.

Right handling brings the proposition into the open.

---

# 82.75 Trace the Argument Before Criticizing It

A fair interpreter should understand an argument before rejecting it.

If another person proposes an interpretation, ask:

> What text are they using?

> What meaning do they assign?

> What premises do they assume?

> What conclusion do they draw?

> Where does the disagreement actually occur?

This prevents caricature.

It also makes correction possible.

The goal is not merely to defeat another interpretation.

The goal is to know what the Word establishes.

---

# 82.76 Trace the Argument Before Accepting It

The same discipline applies to interpretations we already like.

A familiar doctrine can become invisible because we have heard it so often.

Therefore ask:

> Why do I believe this?

> What passage established it?

> What interpretation does the passage require?

> What inference connects the passage to the doctrine?

> What other passages are relevant?

> Have I inherited a conclusion without examining its premises?

This is the intellectual discipline of the Graphē.

---

# 82.77 Right Handling and Familiarity

Familiarity can create an illusion of knowledge.

A verse may be so familiar that the reader stops seeing it.

A doctrine may be so familiar that its assumptions become invisible.

A phrase may be repeated so often that repetition feels like evidence.

Right handling interrupts familiarity.

Read the passage again.

Read the surrounding verses.

Ask what you previously assumed.

Ask what the passage actually says.

The goal is not novelty.

The goal is accuracy.

---

# 82.78 Right Handling and Novelty

The opposite danger is the desire to discover something nobody has seen before.

Novelty is not evidence.

A new interpretation may be correct.

It may also be wrong.

Its newness proves nothing.

The learner must therefore resist both:

> **tradition worship**

and:

> **novelty worship.**

The question remains:

> **What does the Word establish?**

---

# 82.79 Right Handling and Majority Agreement

Many people may agree about an interpretation.

That does not itself establish the interpretation.

Likewise, a minority interpretation is not automatically more faithful merely because it is unpopular.

Popularity is evidence of reception.

It is not by itself evidence of truth.

The interpreter must return to the text.

---

# 82.80 Right Handling and Emotional Force

Some interpretations feel compelling.

Some passages are emotionally powerful.

Some doctrines are deeply comforting.

Others are deeply disturbing.

None of these reactions determines meaning.

Emotional force can accompany truth.

It can also accompany error.

The learner must therefore distinguish:

> **what is moving**

from:

> **what is established.**

Right handling does not despise emotion.

It refuses to make emotion the court of interpretation.

---

# 82.81 Right Handling and Institutional Consequences

Sometimes an interpretation supports an institution's structure.

Sometimes it protects a tradition.

Sometimes it preserves someone's reputation.

Sometimes changing an interpretation would require substantial correction.

These consequences can create pressure.

But pressure does not establish meaning.

The interpreter must remain able to say:

> “Our previous conclusion was not sufficiently established.”

That is part of scholarly integrity.

---

# 82.82 Right Handling and the Possibility of Error

Because the interpreter is fallible, every interpretation should be held with appropriate epistemic proportionality.

This does not mean every doctrine becomes uncertain.

Some propositions are explicit.

Some are necessary.

Some are strongly warranted.

Some are possible.

Some are speculative.

Right handling distinguishes these levels.

The interpreter should not use absolute language where only possibility has been established.

Nor should he use tentative language where Scripture speaks plainly.

Precision is a form of honesty.

---

# 82.83 Right Handling and Responsibility Before God

The command of 2 Timothy 2:15 is not presented as a competition for intellectual prestige.

The work is done before God.

That means the learner's ultimate concern cannot be:

> “Will people admire my interpretation?”

The question is:

> **“Have I handled the Word faithfully?”**

That question gives the scholarly labour its proper orientation.

The learner does not study merely to become impressive.

He studies because the Word is worthy of accurate handling.

This prepares the next chapter, where the workman and approval before God will be examined more directly.

---

# 82.84 What Rightly Dividing Does Not Mean

By this point several errors can be ruled out.

Rightly dividing does not mean:

> cutting Scripture into arbitrary pieces.

It does not mean:

> creating divisions merely because a theory requires them.

It does not mean:

> treating every passage as though it were written to the same audience.

It does not mean:

> ignoring the unity of Scripture.

It does not mean:

> inventing hidden meanings.

It does not mean:

> reducing Scripture to English vocabulary.

It does not mean:

> worshipping Greek and Hebrew terminology.

It does not mean:

> treating every quotation as an endorsed proposition.

It does not mean:

> confusing interpretation with revelation.

It does not mean:

> turning every application into explicit Scripture.

It does not mean:

> harmonizing passages by invention.

It does not mean:

> rejecting harmony because differences exist.

It does not mean:

> relying on tradition without examination.

It does not mean:

> rejecting teachers and scholarship.

It does not mean:

> surrendering judgment to teachers and scholarship.

It means:

> **handling the Word of Truth accurately, with the distinctions, relationships, context, reasoning, and limits required for faithful understanding and application.**

---

# 82.85 The Central Discipline

The whole chapter can now be reduced to one governing discipline:

> **Do not ask merely, “What does this verse say?” Ask what the text actually establishes, how it establishes it, what it does not establish, and how far the conclusion may legitimately be carried.**

This brings together the previous chapters.

Perception tells us what is there.

Learning receives it.

Understanding grasps its meaning.

Reasoning traces its relationships.

Quotation preserves its words.

Knowledge recognizes what is established.

Diligence applies the learner to the task.

Exercise trains the faculties.

Maturity develops capacity.

Discernment distinguishes.

Right handling directs that developed capacity toward faithful treatment of the Word.

Thus:

> **Perceive → Learn → Understand → Reason → Know → Exercise → Discern → Rightly Handle.**

---

# 82.86 Right Handling as Straight Correspondence

The phrase **rightly dividing** therefore reaches beyond the image of cutting.

Its practical force in this chapter is:

> **straight handling of the Word of Truth.**

The interpreter seeks correspondence between:

> **text**

and:

> **meaning**

between:

> **meaning**

and:

> **inference**

between:

> **inference**

and:

> **conclusion**

between:

> **conclusion**

and:

> **application.**

When those relationships are distorted, the handling becomes crooked.

When they are faithfully preserved, the handling is straight.

This is why right handling is an intellectual discipline.

It is also why it is a moral responsibility.

The interpreter is dealing with truth.

---

# 82.87 The Word Remains the Authority

The final safeguard is the most important.

The method is not the authority.

The teacher is not the authority.

The commentator is not the authority.

The institution is not the authority.

The student's interpretation is not the authority.

The Word remains the object being handled.

The entire scholarly apparatus exists to serve faithful engagement with the written record.

Therefore:

> **The interpreter must remain under the Word while interpreting the Word.**

That is the proper posture.

Not beneath human tradition.

Not beneath a commentator's reputation.

Not beneath institutional pressure.

But neither above Scripture in the posture of private sovereignty.

The learner stands as a responsible reader before the authoritative writing.

# 82.88 Right Handling and the Six First Principles

The Doctrine of the Graphē is moving toward the Six First Principles.

But Chapter 82 must not prematurely enter those principles.

The present chapter establishes a prerequisite:

> **The learner must know how to handle the authoritative written Word before the learner can responsibly advance through the foundational doctrinal material that follows.**

The book therefore remains a gate.

The Graphē is the source.

The labour is the learner's responsibility.

The method serves the source.

The skill develops through exercise.

The purpose is faithful handling.

The destination remains beyond this chapter.

This chapter has established that:

1. 2 Timothy 2:15 commands serious labour in relation to the Word of Truth.

4. Right handling concerns correct treatment of the Word rather than arbitrary division of the text.

5. The English word “divide” must not be allowed to determine the meaning independently of the Greek expression and context.

6. Right handling begins with accurate perception of what is written.

7. Right handling seeks to preserve the meaning communicated by the text.

8. Meaning must be distinguished from application.

9. Quotation must be distinguished from interpretation.

10. Interpretation must be distinguished from inference.

11. Inference must be distinguished from application.

12. Speaker, audience, subject, time, scope, conditions, genre, and argument affect responsible interpretation.

13. Narrative report must not automatically be treated as command.

14. Quoted speech must not automatically be treated as endorsed doctrine.

15. Correct quotation does not guarantee correct argument.

16. Conditions, qualifiers, negations, contrasts, and connectives must be preserved.

17. Scripture may be compared with Scripture, but similarity is not automatically proof.

18. Distinction does not require fragmentation.

19. Harmony does not justify invention.

20. Literal and figurative language must both be handled according to context.

21. Lexical tools serve interpretation; they do not replace it.

22. Commentaries and traditions are secondary resources and must not silently become primary authority.

23. Human interpretation remains fallible even when the source is authoritative.

24. Right handling requires correction, restraint, and willingness to stop where evidence stops.

25. Right handling includes responsible application rather than merely accurate quotation.

26. The developed learner should be able to distinguish what the text establishes from what the interpreter adds.

27. Right handling is a practical expression of the maturity, skill, and discernment developed through the preceding chapters.

The resulting sequence is:

> **Perceive → Learn → Understand → Reason → Know → Diligence → Exercise → Skill → Maturity → Discernment → Right Handling.**

---

# 82.90 What Chapter 82 Has Not Yet Established

This chapter has not yet exhausted 2 Timothy 2:15.

It has not fully developed:

> **“approved unto God”**

nor:

> **“a workman”**

nor:

> **“needeth not to be ashamed.”**

Those belong to the next question.

The present chapter has concentrated upon the operation of handling the Word rightly.

The next chapter will therefore ask:

> **What kind of person is required to perform this work, and what does it mean for that workman to be approved before God?**

That question must not be collapsed into the present chapter.

The distinction is deliberate.

---

# 82.91 The Workman Is Not the Word

One final distinction must be fixed before moving forward.

The workman handles the Word.

Therefore:

> **The workman is not the Word.**

His interpretation is not the Word.

His explanation is not the Word.

His curriculum is not the Word.

His method is not the Word.

His institution is not the Word.

His scholarship is not the Word.

The Word is what he is responsible to handle.

This protects the entire architecture of the book.

The learner's greatness is not established by becoming an additional source of revelation.

The learner's maturity is demonstrated by becoming increasingly faithful in handling what has been given.

---

# 82.92 From Right Handling to the Approved Workman

The movement can now proceed naturally.

Chapter 81 asked:

> **What does it mean to rightly divide the Word of Truth?**

Chapter 82 has answered:

> **It requires the straight, accurate, responsible handling of the Word—preserving its meaning, distinctions, context, relationships, scope, inferential limits, and warranted application rather than imposing the interpreter's own authority upon it.**

But this answer creates another question.

If the Word must be handled rightly, then:

> **Who is responsible for this work?**

And if Paul commands the learner to show himself approved unto God:

> **What does approval mean in relation to the workman?**

And if the workman can be ashamed:

> **What does faithful workmanship require?**

And if the command is joined to diligence:

> **How do diligence and workmanship relate?**

These questions belong to Chapter 83.

# 82.93 The Movement Continues

The scholarly labour of the Graphē has now moved from the general requirement to study into the concrete discipline of handling.

The movement has become:

> **Scripture creates scholarly impetus.**

> **The learner perceives.**

> **The learner learns.**

> **The learner understands.**

> **The learner reasons.**

> **The learner quotes accurately.**

> **The learner acquires knowledge.**

> **The learner gives diligence.**

> **The learner exercises the Word.**

> **The faculties become trained.**

> **Skill develops.**

> **Maturity develops.**

> **Discernment develops.**

> **The Word is rightly handled.**

But right handling creates accountability.

The learner is not merely an observer.

He is a worker.

And the worker is not merely working before other people.

He is working before God.

Therefore the next question is:

> **What does it mean to be the approved workman who rightly handles the Word of Truth?**

## Chapter 82 Conclusion

The command to rightly divide the Word of Truth is a command to handle the writing with intellectual and practical fidelity.

The learner must not merely possess the text.

He must not merely quote it.

He must not merely admire it.

He must not merely repeat the interpretation inherited from someone else.

He must learn to work with it.

He must perceive accurately.

He must understand carefully.

He must distinguish explicitly stated truth from inference.

He must follow the argument.

He must preserve context.

He must respect scope.

He must recognize conditions.

He must distinguish speaker from narrator, quotation from endorsement, meaning from application, interpretation from inference, and source from interpreter.

He must compare Scripture without confusing similarity with proof.

He must harmonize without inventing.

He must distinguish without fragmenting.

He must use language carefully.

He must remain correctable.

He must know when the evidence establishes a conclusion and when it does not.

He must stop where the text requires him to stop.

He must not place himself above the Word while claiming to explain the Word.

And he must not reduce the Word to the method by which he studies it.

The writing remains the writing.

The Word of Truth remains the Word of Truth.

The learner remains the learner.

The method remains the servant.

The responsibility is to handle what has been given faithfully.

Therefore the sequence stands:

> **Diligence → Use → Exercise → Skill → Maturity → Discernment → Right Handling.**

And now a further question necessarily follows:

> **What is the workman who performs this labour, and what does it mean to be approved unto God?**

That is the subject of **Chapter 83 — The Approved Workman**.
