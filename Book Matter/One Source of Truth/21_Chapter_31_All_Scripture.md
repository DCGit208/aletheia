# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# Chapter 31 — “All Scripture”

> **Movement IV — How God Communicates**

---

## Chapter Assignment

Chapter 30 established the biblical doctrine of **inspiration**, especially from 2 Timothy 3:15–17 and 2 Peter 1:20–21.

The next question is not simply whether Scripture is inspired.

It is:

> **What is the scope of that claim?**

Paul does not say merely that some Scripture is God-breathed. He says:

> **πᾶσα γραφὴ θεόπνευστος**

> **“All Scripture.”**

This chapter therefore examines the scope and force of that expression. It must not quietly weaken **ALL** into:

- some Scripture;
- selected passages;
- only passages containing direct divine speech;
- only doctrinal passages;
- only prophetic passages;
- or only passages a reader considers spiritually important.

The immediate biblical task is to determine what Paul means by **πᾶσα γραφή**, how that expression functions in its context, and what follows from it.

The supplied *Bible Introduction* makes the scope point explicitly under its treatment of inspiration:

> **“[All scripture] ALL, not a part of Scripture.”**

That observation belongs directly to this chapter. The biblical text itself remains the primary object of examination.

---

# 31.1 The Question Before Us

The previous chapter established:

> **Scripture is God-breathed.**

Chapter 31 now asks:

> **Does that description apply selectively, or does Paul assign it to Scripture as Scripture?**

This is a question of scope.

A doctrine can be true while being incorrectly limited.

Thus:

> “Scripture is inspired”

is weaker than:

> **“All Scripture is God-breathed.”**

The second statement identifies the extent of the divine-breathed description.

Therefore the central issue is:

> **What does the word “all” do in Paul's statement?**

---

# 31.2 The Governing Text: 2 Timothy 3:15–17

The argument must begin with the complete unit rather than verse 16 isolated from its context.

Paul tells Timothy:

1. Timothy has known the sacred writings from childhood.
2. Those writings are able to make him wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.
3. Scripture is God-breathed and profitable.
4. Its usefulness includes teaching, reproof, correction, and training in righteousness.
5. Its purpose is that the person of God may be complete and equipped for every good work.

The Greek text places the crucial statement in this sequence:

> **ἱερὰ γράμματα**  
> sacred writings

↓

> **πᾶσα γραφή**  
> all/every Scripture

↓

> **θεόπνευστος**  
> God-breathed

↓

> **ὠφέλιμος**  
> profitable/useful

↓

> **διδασκαλία**  
> teaching

↓

> **ἐλεγμός**  
> reproof

↓

> **ἐπανόρθωσις**  
> correction

↓

> **παιδεία**  
> training

The scope cannot therefore be separated from the sacred writings already under discussion.

---

# 31.3 The Greek Expression

The key expression is:

> **πᾶσα γραφὴ θεόπνευστος καὶ ὠφέλιμος**

The principal terms are:

### πᾶσα — *pasa*

A form of **πᾶς**, meaning all, every, each, or the whole according to context.

### γραφή — *graphē*

A writing; in the relevant New Testament usage, **Scripture** or sacred Scripture.

### θεόπνευστος — *theopneustos*

God-breathed.

### καί — *kai*

And.

### ὠφέλιμος — *ōphelimos*

Useful, profitable, beneficial.

The sentence therefore attributes two coordinated qualities to **γραφή**:

> **God-breathed**

and

> **profitable/useful.**

The scope marker is **πᾶσα**.

That word cannot simply be ignored.

---

# 31.4 What Does πᾶσα Mean?

The semantic range of **πᾶς** is broad enough to be translated:

- all;
- every;
- each;
- the whole;
- every kind of;

depending upon grammatical and contextual circumstances.

Lexical definition alone is therefore insufficient.

The question is:

> **What does πᾶσα mean with γραφή in this sentence and context?**

That is the proper lexical method.

A dictionary does not decide meaning independently of syntax and context.

The context determines whether the expression is naturally understood as:

> **all Scripture**

or:

> **every Scripture / every scriptural writing.**

These formulations are complementary rather than contradictory.

---

# 31.5 “All Scripture” and “Every Scripture”

English translations vary between:

> **All Scripture**

and

> **Every Scripture.**

The difference should not be exaggerated.

Both translations recognize the force of **πᾶσα**.

The question is whether Paul is speaking of Scripture collectively or each individual scriptural writing within the category.

The two ideas can be expressed together:

> **the whole of Scripture**

and

> **each Scripture belonging to that whole.**

The text therefore gives no warrant for reducing inspiration to a selected subset of Scripture.

---

# 31.6 The Absence of the Article

The Greek phrase is:

> **πᾶσα γραφή**

rather than:

> **πᾶσα ἡ γραφή.**

This has sometimes been used to argue for a rendering such as:

> “Every Scripture inspired by God is also useful.”

The grammatical question deserves examination, but the absence of the article does not by itself settle the matter.

Greek frequently uses anarthrous nouns in constructions that do not correspond mechanically to English indefinite nouns.

More importantly, the immediate context has already identified the sacred writings Timothy knows:

> **ἱερὰ γράμματα.**

Paul then speaks of:

> **γραφή.**

The semantic context is therefore already scriptural.

The grammar must be read with the context, not isolated from it.

---

# 31.7 The Predicate Construction

The traditional reading understands:

> **πᾶσα γραφή**

as the subject and:

> **θεόπνευστος**

as a predicate adjective:

> **All Scripture is God-breathed.**

The same construction coordinates **ὠφέλιμος** with it:

> Scripture is God-breathed **and** useful.

This is the reading represented by major English translations, though translations differ in how they render **θεόπνευστος**.

The Greek text itself is:

> **πᾶσα γραφὴ θεόπνευστος καὶ ὠφέλιμος...**

The natural force of the sentence is therefore a statement concerning the character of Scripture.

---

# 31.8 The Alternative Translation Question

Some interpreters have proposed a construction along the lines of:

> “Every Scripture inspired by God is also useful.”

This possibility has been discussed because of the absence of the article and the syntax.

It is important to understand what this does and does not establish.

It does not establish a doctrine of partly inspired Scripture.

Rather, it would make **God-breathed** a condition identifying the writings under discussion.

But the immediate context makes the traditional reading coherent with Paul's argument:

> Timothy has known the sacred writings.

↓

> Scripture is described as God-breathed.

↓

> Scripture is profitable.

↓

> Scripture equips the servant of God.

The passage reads naturally as a statement concerning Scripture.

Therefore the direct conclusion of this chapter remains:

> **Paul presents Scripture as God-breathed, not as a mixture of God-breathed and non-God-breathed Scripture.**

---

# 31.9 “All” Cannot Become “Some”

This is the mandatory safeguard of the chapter.

Once Paul has identified:

> **πᾶσα γραφή**

the reader cannot legitimately replace it with:

> “some Scripture.”

Nor can it be silently transformed into:

> “the parts of Scripture that teach doctrine.”

Nor:

> “the parts directly spoken by God.”

Nor:

> “the passages that contain prophecy.”

Nor:

> “the portions later judged to be spiritually useful.”

Such reductions change the proposition.

Paul's statement is comprehensive within the referent designated by **γραφή**.

Therefore:

> **If a writing belongs to Scripture, the divine-breathed description applies to it as Scripture.**

That is the scope this chapter must preserve.

---

# 31.10 What “All Scripture” Does Not Mean

The statement must nevertheless be understood precisely.

“All Scripture” does **not** mean:

> every sentence spoken by every person recorded in the Bible is itself a divine utterance.

The Bible records:

- human speech;
- questions;
- accusations;
- lies;
- speeches by hostile characters;
- statements by unbelievers;
- statements by Satan;
- historical reports;
- poetry;
- narrative;
- dialogue.

The inspired character belongs to the **Scripture as written record**.

Thus:

> **All Scripture is God-breathed**

does not mean:

> **Every quoted speaker is speaking God's own words.**

The written record is inspired even when it faithfully records false human speech.

---

# 31.11 Recorded Words and Divine Utterance

Suppose Scripture records:

> “There is no God.”

The statement may be the utterance of a foolish person.

The Bible is not thereby asserting:

> “There is no God.”

Instead, Scripture truthfully records what the person said, and the context tells us who said it and whether the proposition is true.

Therefore inspiration concerns the truthfulness and authority of the **Scriptural record**, not the conversion of every quotation into direct divine speech.

This distinction allows “all Scripture” to be comprehensive without producing category errors.

---

# 31.12 “All Scripture” Does Not Mean One Literary Form

Nor does “all Scripture” mean that every part of the Bible is written in the same literary form.

Scripture contains:

- narrative;
- law;
- poetry;
- wisdom;
- prophecy;
- genealogy;
- Gospel testimony;
- letters;
- apocalyptic material.

“All Scripture” means all Scripture.

It does not mean:

> all Scripture must be read as one literary genre.

The scope of inspiration is comprehensive.

The modes of expression remain diverse.

---

# 31.13 Inspiration and Literary Diversity

If:

> **all Scripture is God-breathed,**

then the divine claim extends across the whole scriptural corpus while preserving the actual forms in which those writings communicate.

Therefore:

- poetry is inspired as poetry;
- narrative is inspired as narrative;
- law is inspired as law;
- prophecy is inspired as prophecy;
- wisdom is inspired as wisdom;
- letters are inspired as letters.

Inspiration does not flatten Scripture.

It establishes its divine character.

---

# 31.14 Timothy's Sacred Writings

Paul's statement begins with Timothy's childhood knowledge of:

> **ἱερὰ γράμματα**

—sacred writings.

These writings were already known to Timothy before his Christian ministry.

They were capable of making him wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.

This is important because Paul's doctrine of inspiration is rooted in a real existing body of sacred writings.

He is not speaking abstractly about hypothetical future Scripture.

He is speaking about the sacred writings Timothy knows.

---

# 31.15 Sacred Writings and Scripture

The relationship between:

> **ἱερὰ γράμματα**

and

> **γραφή**

must therefore be taken seriously.

Paul does not move from ordinary literature to Scripture without context.

He moves from Timothy's sacred writings to the category of Scripture.

The immediate subject is therefore not:

> “religious ideas generally.”

It is:

> **the sacred written Scriptures.**

---

# 31.16 What Timothy Knew From Childhood

Timothy's knowledge of the sacred writings predates his Christian ministry.

His mother and grandmother are mentioned earlier in the letter.

The sacred writings therefore belonged to the inherited scriptural tradition of Israel.

This makes the Old Testament Scriptures the immediate historical background of 2 Timothy 3:15–16.

Paul's statement cannot simply be used as though he were standing at the end of a formally closed New Testament canon.

That later canonical question belongs elsewhere.

Here the immediate referent is the sacred Scripture already known by Timothy.

---

# 31.17 The Old Testament as Immediate Context

The immediate Scripture in view includes the Jewish Scriptures Timothy knew.

These writings include:

- the Law;
- the Prophets;
- the Psalms and other sacred writings.

Jesus and the apostles consistently treated these writings as Scripture.

Paul's doctrine of Scripture therefore arises from a pre-existing scriptural tradition.

This is important because it shows that the apostolic doctrine of inspiration is not a late invention detached from Israel's Scriptures.

---

# 31.18 Jesus and the Scope of Scripture

Jesus repeatedly appeals to Scripture as an authoritative category.

He refers to:

- the Law;
- the Prophets;
- Moses;
- David;
- Isaiah;
- Daniel;
- and the Scriptures generally.

His use of Scripture is not restricted to a tiny collection of passages.

He appeals to the written corpus as authoritative revelation.

This provides Christological continuity with Paul's doctrine.

---

# 31.19 “Scripture Cannot Be Broken”

John 10:35 contains the statement:

> **“Scripture cannot be broken.”**

The immediate context concerns Jesus' argument from Scripture.

Its significance here is the way Jesus treats Scripture.

He does not divide Scripture into a reliable inspired portion and an unreliable remainder.

He appeals to Scripture with authoritative force.

A fuller examination of Scripture's authority belongs later in the book.

Here the passage serves as corroborating evidence for the comprehensive scriptural category.

---

# 31.20 Jesus and the Written Word

Jesus repeatedly uses the formula:

> **“It is written.”**

The written form is authoritative.

The appeal is not merely:

> “Someone once received a revelation.”

It is:

> **“It is written.”**

This connects divine revelation to written Scripture.

It demonstrates why the scope of “all Scripture” matters.

The written record is not a secondary decoration attached to revelation.

It is the authoritative scriptural form through which revelation is transmitted.

---

# 31.21 Moses and the Written Law

Moses provides a foundational example.

The Pentateuch contains repeated commands and descriptions concerning writing.

Moses is commanded to write.

Moses writes.

Moses records.

Moses gives the written law to Israel.

The resulting written material becomes part of the scriptural tradition later treated as authoritative Scripture.

This is consistent with Chapter 30:

> divine agency does not eliminate genuine human authorship.

Chapter 31 now adds:

> **the divine-breathed description is not restricted to isolated prophetic sentences; the written scriptural corpus itself is included.**

---

# 31.22 The Prophets and the Written Record

The prophets likewise demonstrate that divine communication becomes written Scripture.

Isaiah records prophetic revelation.

Jeremiah records words received from the LORD.

Ezekiel records visions.

Daniel records visions and revelations.

Habakkuk is instructed to write the vision.

Zechariah records prophetic visions.

The process therefore includes:

> **revelation → prophetic reception → written record.**

When such writings belong to Scripture, the claim of 2 Timothy 3:16 applies to Scripture as Scripture.

---

# 31.23 Psalms and Wisdom

The scope also reaches beyond direct prophetic oracles.

The Psalms contain:

- prayer;
- praise;
- lament;
- confession;
- wisdom;
- historical remembrance;
- royal material;
- messianic expectation.

Proverbs contains wisdom sayings.

Ecclesiastes contains reflective discourse.

Song of Songs is poetic literature.

If Scripture were defined only as:

> “words directly dictated by God,”

large portions of the biblical corpus would become difficult to account for.

That is not the biblical model.

“All Scripture” embraces the written corpus in its actual literary diversity.

---

# 31.24 Narrative Is Scripture

Genesis through Kings, Chronicles, Ezra-Nehemiah, the Gospels, Acts, and other narrative material communicate through historical narration.

The narrator does not need to introduce every sentence with:

> “Thus says the LORD.”

Narrative itself can belong to Scripture.

Therefore:

> **inspiration is not limited to direct divine speech.**

A narrated event can be Scripture.

A genealogy can be Scripture.

A historical report can be Scripture.

A poem can be Scripture.

A letter can be Scripture.

The scope is determined by the category of Scripture, not by whether every sentence has the grammatical form of divine speech.

---

# 31.25 Genealogies and Lists

Genealogies provide a useful test.

They may appear less obviously “inspired” to a modern reader than prophetic visions.

Yet if they belong to Scripture, “all Scripture” includes them.

The doctrine therefore refuses to create a hierarchy in which:

> prophetic passages = inspired

but:

> genealogical passages = merely human.

The biblical statement is broader.

The question is not:

> “Does this passage feel inspired?”

The question is:

> **“Is this Scripture?”**

Once the writing belongs to Scripture, Paul's description applies.

---

# 31.26 Letters Are Scripture

Letters provide another test.

Paul writes to actual communities and individuals.

His letters:

- address particular situations;
- contain greetings;
- discuss practical problems;
- name people;
- address controversies;
- reason from Scripture;
- and give instruction.

Yet apostolic writings can function as Scripture.

This demonstrates again that inspiration is not restricted to mystical experiences or direct oracles.

God-breathed Scripture can take the form of an ordinary historical letter.

---

# 31.27 Luke and Historical Investigation

Luke provides a particularly important case.

His opening presents:

- previous accounts;
- eyewitnesses;
- servants of the word;
- investigation;
- careful tracing;
- orderly writing;
- certainty for the reader.

This demonstrates that inspiration does not require the absence of research.

Luke can investigate.

Luke can assess testimony.

Luke can arrange material.

Luke can write.

The resulting Gospel can nevertheless be Scripture.

Therefore:

> **human historical methodology and divine inspiration are not mutually exclusive.**

---

# 31.28 John and Testimony

John likewise presents written testimony arising from historical experience.

The Johannine writings appeal to:

- hearing;
- seeing;
- contemplation;
- touching;
- testimony;
- writing.

The result is not merely:

> “John had a spiritual feeling.”

It is:

> **John testifies to what was encountered and writes that testimony.**

Again:

> **all Scripture**

includes Scripture produced through historical testimony.

---

# 31.29 Paul and Apostolic Writing

Paul's letters demonstrate that Scripture can be produced through ordinary literary activity.

Paul reasons.

Paul argues.

Paul explains.

Paul remembers.

Paul commands.

Paul appeals to prior teaching.

Paul responds to historical circumstances.

His personality remains visible.

Yet his writings can function as authoritative apostolic Scripture.

The scope of inspiration therefore reaches into genuine human communication.

---

# 31.30 Not Merely Direct Words From God

The cumulative evidence is decisive.

Scripture contains:

- direct divine speech;
- prophetic speech;
- historical narration;
- poetry;
- wisdom;
- genealogies;
- apostolic testimony;
- letters;
- theological argument;
- recorded human speech.

Yet Paul says:

> **all Scripture is God-breathed.**

Therefore the criterion cannot be:

> “Does every line consist of direct speech from God?”

The criterion is:

> **Does the writing belong to Scripture?**

That is the conceptual distinction required by the text.

---

# 31.31 Not Merely Doctrinal Statements

Nor does “all Scripture” mean:

> “All doctrinal statements are inspired.”

That would reduce Scripture to propositions that teach theology explicitly.

But the Bible contains historical accounts, genealogies, songs, narratives, descriptions, commands, prayers, and letters.

These are not disposable packaging around the “real” inspired material.

They belong to Scripture.

Therefore the divine-breathed description applies to the whole scriptural writing.

---

# 31.32 Not Merely Prophecy

This distinction is especially important because the preceding chapters examined multiple modes of divine communication.

God communicates through:

- audible voice;
- angels;
- prophets;
- Christ;
- apostles;
- visions;
- dreams;
- revelation.

But Chapter 29 distinguished:

> **the mode of receiving divine communication**

from:

> **the later written record.**

Chapter 31 reinforces that distinction.

A person may receive revelation through a vision.

But the resulting Scripture is not limited to the vision itself.

A prophet may receive divine speech.

But the prophetic book may contain narrative, historical explanation, symbolic description, and other written material.

“All Scripture” concerns the Scripture as Scripture.

---

# 31.33 Revelation Is Not the Scope-Limiting Criterion

Suppose one argued:

> “Only the sentence directly revealed to the prophet is inspired.”

That would not account adequately for the biblical corpus.

Prophetic books include:

- descriptions;
- historical context;
- names;
- places;
- actions;
- responses;
- narratives;
- written commands;
- visions;
- explanations.

The Scripture encompasses the complete written testimony.

Therefore:

> **inspiration cannot be reduced to isolated moments of revelation.**

The distinction remains:

> **Revelation concerns what God makes known.**

> **Inspiration concerns the written Scripture's relationship to God.**

---

# 31.34 “All” and the Unity of Scripture

The comprehensive scope of inspiration also helps explain why Scripture is treated as one authoritative body despite its many forms.

There are:

- many books;
- many authors;
- many centuries;
- many historical situations;
- many literary forms.

Yet Scripture can be described comprehensively:

> **All Scripture.**

This does not erase diversity.

It identifies a common category.

The books differ.

The Scripture is one category.

---

# 31.35 “All Scripture” and the Doctrine of the Graphē

The Doctrine of the Graphē can now be sharpened.

The word **γραφή** is not merely a synonym for:

> “a religious quotation.”

It is a scriptural category in the New Testament.

When Paul says:

> **πᾶσα γραφή**

he is speaking about Scripture.

Therefore:

> **the whole of Scripture falls within the divine-breathed description.**

The Graphē is consequently not a collection containing:

> inspired passages + uninspired passages.

The biblical category is not presented that way.

---

# 31.36 Individuality Without Partial Inspiration

The fact that individual books have different authors does not imply different levels of inspiration.

Moses does not produce “Moses-level Scripture.”

David does not produce “poetic-level Scripture.”

Luke does not produce “historical-level Scripture.”

Paul does not produce “epistolary-level Scripture.”

John does not produce “Johannine-level Scripture.”

They are different human authors.

The writings are different genres.

Yet where the writings belong to Scripture, the category remains Scripture.

Thus:

> **diversity of authorship does not imply partial inspiration.**

---

# 31.37 Diversity of Style Without Diversity of Authority

The same principle applies to style.

A writer may use:

- Hebrew idiom;
- Aramaic expressions;
- Greek rhetorical forms;
- poetic parallelism;
- narrative structure;
- legal terminology;
- personal correspondence.

Style differs.

Authority does not therefore differ.

The divine-breathed claim does not require God to erase linguistic individuality.

It means God works through real human language and authorship.

This is the same divine-human architecture established in Chapter 30.

---

# 31.38 Inspiration and the Human Mind

“All Scripture” does not imply that the authors were mentally absent.

They:

- remembered;
- investigated;
- reasoned;
- composed;
- arranged;
- prayed;
- wrote;
- testified.

Their mental activity is visible in the texts.

The comprehensive scope of inspiration does not require psychological uniformity.

It requires recognizing the written Scripture as belonging to the divine revelatory economy.

---

# 31.39 Inspiration and Human Research

Luke is especially important here.

His prologue demonstrates that careful historical research is compatible with Scripture.

A narrow concept of inspiration might imply:

> “If God inspired it, human research cannot be involved.”

Luke demonstrates otherwise.

The biblical model permits:

> **human investigation under divine purpose.**

Therefore “all Scripture” includes Scripture produced through historically situated human processes.

---

# 31.40 The Bible Introduction's Scope Claim

The supplied *Bible Introduction* explicitly places:

> **“Inspiration (2 Tim. 3:15–17) — [All scripture] ALL, not a part of Scripture.”**

This is a useful formulation for the present chapter because it identifies the precise safeguard the chapter is designed to protect.

Its force is not that the reference work replaces biblical exegesis.

Rather, it correctly identifies the central scope issue:

> **do not divide Scripture into inspired and uninspired portions.**

The primary evidence remains Paul's text.

The supplied source reinforces the same reading.

---

# 31.41 What “All Scripture” Does Not Yet Decide

A crucial architectural distinction must now be maintained.

The statement:

> **all Scripture is God-breathed**

does not by itself answer every question concerning:

- which books constitute the complete biblical canon;
- when every individual book was recognized;
- which canonical boundaries were disputed;
- how canonical recognition developed;
- how manuscripts were transmitted;
- how textual variants should be evaluated.

Those questions are real.

They belong to later chapters.

The present question is:

> **Once something is identified as Scripture, what is the scope of the divine-breathed claim?**

Paul's answer is comprehensive.

---

# 31.42 Inspiration Is Not Canon

This distinction deserves explicit emphasis.

**Inspiration asks:**

> What is the divine character of Scripture?

**Canon asks:**

> Which writings belong to the authoritative scriptural corpus?

The order of investigation matters.

If a writing is Scripture, 2 Timothy 3:16 describes Scripture as God-breathed.

But determining the boundaries of the category **γραφή** is a separate historical and canonical investigation.

Therefore Chapter 31 establishes:

> **comprehensive inspiration**

without pretending to have already completed:

> **the canon investigation.**

---

# 31.43 Inspiration Is Not Preservation

The same distinction applies to textual transmission.

A manuscript copy is not conceptually identical with the original act of inspiration.

The sequence is:

> **writing → copying → transmission → preservation → textual witnesses.**

The existence of textual transmission questions does not reduce the scope of the original inspiration claim.

Conversely, the doctrine of inspiration does not itself provide a complete theory of manuscript transmission.

Those questions belong to later evidential and textual chapters.

---

# 31.44 Inspiration Is Not Interpretation

Nor does “all Scripture” mean that every reader interprets every passage correctly.

The inspired status of Scripture does not eliminate:

- language;
- context;
- genre;
- historical setting;
- syntax;
- lexical analysis;
- interpretive discipline.

Indeed, because all Scripture is treated as authoritative, the responsibility to interpret it correctly becomes greater.

The text must be handled according to what it actually says.

---

# 31.45 “All Scripture” and Truthful Interpretation

The authority of Scripture does not authorize arbitrary interpretation.

A person cannot quote:

> “All Scripture”

and then make a passage mean whatever he wishes.

The doctrine of inspiration strengthens the requirement for accurate interpretation.

The authoritative text must be allowed to speak in its own literary, historical, grammatical, and canonical context.

---

# 31.46 The Fourfold Function of All Scripture

Paul does not merely tell us that all Scripture is God-breathed.

He tells us what Scripture does.

It is profitable:

### 1. For teaching

Scripture communicates truth that forms instruction.

### 2. For reproof

Scripture exposes error and wrongdoing.

### 3. For correction

Scripture restores what is wrong.

### 4. For training in righteousness

Scripture forms the life of the person under God's instruction.

The scope of inspiration therefore corresponds to the scope of Scripture's usefulness.

The whole Scripture is available for the whole work Paul describes.

---

# 31.47 The Purpose Clause in Verse 17

Verse 17 begins with:

> **ἵνα**

a purpose/result conjunction.

The purpose is that:

> the person of God may be complete;

and:

> equipped for every good work.

The argument therefore has a clear progression:

> **All Scripture**

↓

> **God-breathed**

↓

> **profitable**

↓

> **teaching**

↓

> **reproof**

↓

> **correction**

↓

> **training**

↓

> **completeness**

↓

> **equipping for every good work.**

The comprehensiveness of the Scripture corresponds to the comprehensiveness of its function.

---

# 31.48 “Every Good Work” and “All Scripture”

There is a structural correspondence in Paul's argument.

He uses comprehensive language at both ends:

> **all Scripture**

and

> **every good work.**

The point is functional completeness.

Scripture is sufficient for the task Paul identifies because the whole scriptural corpus is available for teaching, correction, and training.

Therefore the doctrine is not:

> “Use the inspired parts and disregard the rest.”

The argument assumes the usefulness of the whole.

---

# 31.49 The Scope of Scripture and the Character of God

The doctrine also fits the earlier argument concerning God's truthfulness.

If:

> God is truthful;

and:

> Scripture is God-breathed;

then Scripture's divine character is not a statement detached from God's nature.

The argument is cumulative:

> **God is truthful.**

↓

> **God communicates truth.**

↓

> **God gives Scripture.**

↓

> **Scripture is God-breathed.**

↓

> **All Scripture falls under that description.**

This does not replace textual or historical examination.

It establishes the theological architecture within which those examinations occur.

---

# 31.50 The Scope of Inspiration and Human Authors

The comprehensive statement confirms the conclusion of Chapter 30.

If all Scripture is God-breathed, and Scripture contains genuinely human writing, then divine inspiration extends through genuine human authorship.

The result is not:

> God-inspired sections + human-authored sections.

Rather:

> **humanly authored Scripture that is God-breathed.**

Moses remains the writer of Moses' material.

David remains the human author of Davidic material.

Luke remains Luke.

Paul remains Paul.

John remains John.

The divine claim encompasses the writing without destroying the human author.

---

# 31.51 The Scope of Inspiration and Historical Testimony

The same conclusion applies to testimony.

Luke's historical investigation is not excluded.

John's eyewitness testimony is not excluded.

Paul's apostolic testimony is not excluded.

Moses' historical recording is not excluded.

Prophetic testimony is not excluded.

The Bible's human historical form is therefore part of the very thing to which the comprehensive claim applies.

The Bible does not claim to be a divine message that bypassed history.

It presents a written testimony that entered history through human agents.

---

# 31.52 Why the Word “All” Matters

Without the word **all**, the doctrine could be reduced to:

> “Some Scripture is inspired.”

That is not what Paul says.

Without comprehensive scope, the reader could construct a two-level Bible:

### Level 1

Words genuinely from God.

### Level 2

Human religious material attached to them.

Paul's statement resists that division.

The Scripture category itself is described as God-breathed.

Therefore the proper question is not:

> “Which parts of Scripture are inspired?”

but:

> **“What writings constitute Scripture?”**

That is why the later canonical investigation is logically necessary.

---

# 31.53 The Logical Hinge Toward Canon

This chapter therefore creates an important transition.

We have established:

> **If it is Scripture, the biblical claim of 2 Timothy 3:16 applies to it comprehensively.**

The next question is unavoidable:

> **Which writings are Scripture?**

That is not Chapter 31's task to resolve.

It is the task of the canonical investigation.

This distinction prevents a circular argument:

> “These books are inspired because they are in the Bible, and they are in the Bible because they are inspired.”

That merely assumes the conclusion.

Instead, the investigation must distinguish:

> **inspiration**

from:

> **canonical recognition.**

---

# 31.54 The Proper Evidential Sequence

The architecture can now be stated:

> **God communicates.**

↓

> **Divine revelation occurs.**

↓

> **Human agents receive, witness, proclaim, or record.**

↓

> **Scripture is produced.**

↓

> **Scripture is described as God-breathed.**

↓

> **All Scripture is God-breathed.**

↓

> **The historical question becomes: Which writings constitute Scripture?**

That final question belongs to canon.

The present chapter establishes the scope of the inspiration claim.

---

# 31.55 The Comprehensive Character of the Graphē

The Doctrine of the Graphē therefore acquires a more precise form.

The Graphē is not:

> a collection of selectively inspired religious statements.

Nor:

> a mixture of divine and merely human Scripture.

Rather:

> **Scripture, as Scripture, is comprehensively described as God-breathed.**

This does not erase:

- historical authorship;
- literary form;
- human language;
- eyewitness testimony;
- research;
- narrative;
- poetry;
- letters.

It encompasses them.

---

# 31.56 The Scope of “All”

The word **all** is comprehensive, but its logical scope is not unlimited beyond its noun.

“All Scripture” means:

> **all that belongs to Scripture.**

It does not mean:

> every religious writing ever produced.

It does not mean:

> every ancient Jewish document.

It does not mean:

> every Christian document.

It does not mean:

> every sermon.

It does not mean:

> every prophetic claim.

It means:

> **all Scripture.**

Therefore the word **all** establishes the completeness of inspiration **within the category of Scripture**.

The definition of that category remains the subject of canon.

---

# 31.57 Why This Distinction Protects the Argument

This distinction prevents two opposite errors.

### Error 1 — Partial inspiration

> “Only some passages are inspired.”

The text does not say this.

### Error 2 — Unlimited inspiration

> “Everything religiously written is inspired.”

The text does not say this either.

The correct proposition is:

> **All Scripture is God-breathed.**

The scope is complete within the scriptural category.

This is precise, comprehensive, and faithful to the wording.

---

# 31.58 The Bible's Internal Logic

The Bible's internal logic therefore gives us a stable sequence:

### Divine source

God communicates.

### Human mediation

Human beings receive, speak, witness, and write.

### Written Scripture

The communication becomes Scripture.

### Divine-breathed character

Scripture is described as God-breathed.

### Comprehensive scope

All Scripture is included.

### Historical recognition

The boundaries of Scripture must then be investigated.

The next stage is therefore not a change of subject.

It is the natural consequence of the present conclusion.

---

# 31.59 The Necessary Conclusion

The reasoning can now be stated directly.

### Premise 1

Paul refers to sacred writings already known to Timothy.

### Premise 2

Paul identifies Scripture as **γραφή**.

### Premise 3

Paul places **πᾶσα** before **γραφή**, giving the statement comprehensive scope within the scriptural category.

### Premise 4

Paul describes Scripture as **θεόπνευστος**.

### Premise 5

Paul applies the resulting Scripture to teaching, reproof, correction, and training.

Therefore:

> **The Bible's doctrine of inspiration is comprehensive: Scripture as Scripture is God-breathed.**

It is not a doctrine of partially inspired Scripture.

It is not limited to direct divine speech.

It is not limited to prophecy.

It is not limited to doctrine.

It encompasses the written Scripture in its actual historical and literary diversity.

---

# 31.60 What Chapter 31 Has Established

The chapter has established:

1. **πᾶσα γραφή** is the governing expression.
2. **πᾶς** carries comprehensive force according to context.
3. The immediate context concerns sacred writings known by Timothy.
4. **γραφή** functions as the New Testament category of Scripture.
5. **θεόπνευστος** describes Scripture's relationship to God.
6. The statement is not restricted to direct divine speech.
7. Inspiration is not restricted to prophetic passages.
8. Inspiration is not restricted to doctrinal propositions.
9. Inspiration includes Scripture's literary diversity.
10. Human authorship remains genuine.
11. Historical research and testimony are not excluded.
12. The claim applies comprehensively within the category of Scripture.
13. The claim does not itself define the complete canon.
14. Canon and inspiration must therefore remain distinct.
15. The next question is how the scriptural corpus is recognized.

---

# 31.61 The Architecture of “All Scripture”

The doctrine can now be represented as:

> **SCRIPTURE**

↓

> **πᾶσα — ALL**

↓

> **γραφή — SCRIPTURE**

↓

↓

↓

> **EQUIPPED FOR EVERY GOOD WORK**

The point is not simply that Scripture exists.

The point is that:

> **the whole scriptural category is treated as divinely given and functionally authoritative.**

---

# 31.62 The Doctrine in One Sentence

> **Where Scripture is Scripture, the biblical claim is not partial inspiration but comprehensive divine-breathed character: all Scripture is God-breathed.**

This is the precise contribution of Chapter 31 to the Doctrine of the Graphē.

Chapter 29 established:

Chapter 31 has established:

The progression is now:

# Chapter Summary

- **“All Scripture” is the governing scope statement of 2 Timothy 3:16.**
- **πᾶσα (*pasa*) carries comprehensive force within the category of Scripture.**
- **γραφή (*graphē*) identifies the scriptural writing under discussion.**
- **θεόπνευστος (*theopneustos*) describes Scripture as God-breathed.**
- **The claim is not limited to passages containing direct divine speech.**
- **The claim is not limited to prophecy or doctrinal statements.**
- **Narrative, poetry, wisdom, genealogy, historical testimony, Gospel writing, and apostolic letters can all belong to Scripture.**
- **Human authorship, investigation, literary form, and historical testimony remain genuine.**
- **“All Scripture” means comprehensive inspiration within the category of Scripture—not inspiration of every religious writing.**
- **Inspiration must be distinguished from canon, preservation, and interpretation.**
- **The question of which writings constitute Scripture is therefore a subsequent canonical question, not something to be assumed in this chapter.**

## The chapter's central conclusion

> **The Bible's claim is comprehensive: all Scripture is God-breathed. Inspiration is therefore not restricted to selected passages, direct divine utterances, prophetic material, or doctrinal propositions. Wherever a writing belongs to Scripture, the biblical description of Scripture as God-breathed applies to it as Scripture, while the genuine human authorship, literary diversity, historical testimony, and modes of composition through which that Scripture came to us remain intact.**

# Governing Distinctions

> **Revelation:** God makes something known.

> **Inspiration:** Scripture is described as God-breathed.

> **Scope of inspiration:** All Scripture is included within the divine-breathed description.

> **Canon:** Which writings constitute the scriptural corpus.

> **Preservation:** How the written text is transmitted through history.

> **Interpretation:** How the written text is correctly understood.

These are related questions.

They are not the same question.
