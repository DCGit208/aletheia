# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT I — TRUTH AND THE POSSIBILITY OF KNOWLEDGE

# Chapter 7 — Isaiah 1:18: “Let Us Reason Together”

> **Central question:** What does Isaiah 1:18 actually establish about reasonability, and what does it require of the person who encounters the Word of God?

The preceding chapters have established that truth is not created by assertion, that knowledge requires adequate grounds, that evidence must be rationally assessed, and that sound judgment requires disciplined perception, analysis, comparison, distinction, inference, and judgment.

The next question is therefore biblical rather than merely philosophical:

> **Does the Word of God itself require the exercise of such intellectual capacities?**

Isaiah 1:18 supplies one of the clearest starting points for answering that question:

> **“Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow; though they be red like crimson, they shall be as wool.”**  
> — Isaiah 1:18

The verse is frequently quoted as a general encouragement to “reason,” but its force is greater when its context, language, argument, and conclusion are examined.

The Lord does not merely announce a conclusion.

He summons people to engagement.

He presents a matter.

He confronts a condition.

He calls for response.

And He places the matter within a framework in which what is wrong can be confronted, what is right can be distinguished, and the consequences of the alternatives can be understood.

The verse therefore belongs naturally in the intellectual architecture of this book.

It does not establish autonomous human reason as the supreme judge of revelation.

It demonstrates that **reasonability is compatible with, and required by, serious engagement with the Word of God**.

---

# 1. Read the Verse as an Argument, Not a Slogan

The first discipline is to refuse to isolate the familiar phrase:

> **“Let us reason together.”**

Isaiah 1:18 is not an abstract philosophical motto detached from the surrounding passage.

The verse occurs within a larger indictment and summons.

Isaiah 1 begins by identifying Israel's rebellion and estrangement from the LORD. The passage describes a people who have not responded rightly to the One who brought them forth and sustained them.

The Lord then confronts their religious activity.

He says:

> **“To what purpose is the multitude of your sacrifices unto me? saith the LORD: I am full of the burnt offerings of rams, and the fat of fed beasts; and I delight not in the blood of bullocks, or of lambs, or of he goats.”**  
> — Isaiah 1:11

He continues:

> **“Bring no more vain oblations; incense is an abomination unto me; the new moons and sabbaths, the calling of assemblies, I cannot away with; it is iniquity, even the solemn meeting.”**  
> — Isaiah 1:13

And then:

> **“Your new moons and your appointed feasts my soul hateth: they are a trouble unto me; I am weary to bear them.”**  
> — Isaiah 1:14

The issue is therefore not whether religious activity exists.

It is whether the activity corresponds to truth and righteousness.

The Lord says:

> **“And when ye spread forth your hands, I will hide mine eyes from you: yea, when ye make many prayers, I will not hear: your hands are full of blood.”**  
> — Isaiah 1:15

Then comes the summons:

> **“Wash you, make you clean; put away the evil of your doings from before mine eyes; cease to do evil; Learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow.”**  
> — Isaiah 1:16–17

Only then does the verse arrive:

> **“Come now, and let us reason together…”**

This context matters.

The Lord is not inviting an arbitrary debate about whether truth exists.

He is confronting a people whose outward practice is inconsistent with their actual condition and calling them to recognize the matter rightly.

---

# 2. What Does “Reason Together” Mean?

The King James rendering is:

> **“Come now, and let us reason together.”**

The Hebrew verb translated **“reason”** is **יָכַח (*yākaḥ*)**.

Its lexical range includes the ideas of reasoning, deciding, judging, proving, reproving, correcting, and demonstrating a matter.

The important point is that the word does not describe casual conversation.

It belongs to the language of **judgment and determination**.

The sense of the summons is therefore not:

> “Let us exchange personal opinions.”

Nor:

> “Let us negotiate what truth will be.”

Nor:

> “Let us decide which reality we prefer.”

Rather, the Lord calls the people into a confrontation in which the matter can be examined, judged, and settled.

That is precisely why the verse belongs in a study of reasonability.

Reasonability is not the creation of truth.

It is the disciplined engagement of the mind with what is true.

---

# 3. The Lord Is the One Who Issues the Summons

This point must not be lost.

The verse does not say:

> “Come, and let human reason establish what is true.”

It says:

> **“Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD.”**

The authority of the conversation is therefore not generated by the human participants.

The Lord initiates it.

The Lord identifies the matter.

The Lord has already spoken concerning the condition.

And the Lord gives the summons to reason.

This destroys two opposite errors.

### Error one: anti-intellectualism

The idea that submission to God requires the abandonment of thought.

Isaiah 1:18 contradicts that conception.

### Error two: rationalistic autonomy

The idea that human reasoning is itself the ultimate authority capable of determining truth independently of God.

Isaiah 1:18 does not teach that either.

The correct order is:

> **God speaks → the human being hears → the human being perceives → the human being reasons → the human being judges → the human being responds.**

Reasonability therefore operates **under truth**, not as the creator of truth.

---

# 4. What Is Being Reasoned About?

The second half of Isaiah 1:18 supplies the matter:

> **“though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow; though they be red like crimson, they shall be as wool.”**

The Lord is not presenting an empty intellectual exercise.

The subject is moral and factual.

There is a real condition:

> **“your sins be as scarlet”**

and there is a promised transformation:

> **“they shall be as white as snow.”**

Likewise:

> **“though they be red like crimson, they shall be as wool.”**

The reasoning therefore concerns an actual condition and a real divine response.

This is important because it demonstrates that biblical reasonability is not merely abstract intellectualism.

The mind is being engaged **with reality**.

The issue is what is actually true about the people, what God requires, and what God promises.

---

# 5. Reasonability Is Not the Manufacture of Truth

This distinction is fundamental.

If the Lord says:

> “your sins be as scarlet,”

human reasoning does not make that proposition true.

If the Lord says:

> “they shall be as white as snow,”

human reasoning does not create that future reality.

Truth precedes the reasoning.

Reasoning is the intellectual activity by which the human being apprehends, examines, understands, and responds to truth.

Therefore:

> **Reason does not manufacture the object of knowledge.**

It works upon what is presented to knowledge.

This is consistent with the architecture established in Chapters 1–6.

Truth is not produced by the mind.

The mind is responsible for coming into conformity with what is true.

---

# 6. “Let Us Reason” Does Not Mean “Let Us Invent”

The difference can be stated simply:

> **Reason discovers, examines, connects, and follows; it does not arbitrarily invent reality.**

When Scripture presents a proposition, the learner must ask:

- What has actually been said?
- What does it mean?
- What grounds it?
- What follows from it?
- What does not follow from it?
- What response does it require?

The learner must not substitute:

> “What do I think?”

for:

> **“What is true?”**

Nor should the learner substitute:

> “What does my institution teach?”

for:

> **“What has been established?”**

The purpose of reasonability is therefore alignment with truth.

---

# 7. Reasonability Requires Perception

The first intellectual requirement is perception.

If the learner does not accurately perceive the proposition, he cannot reason accurately from it.

Isaiah's summons therefore presupposes that the people can be confronted with the matter.

They must hear.

They must recognize their condition.

They must understand the accusation.

They must consider the offered response.

They must act.

This aligns with the intellectual progression established in Chapter 6:

> **Perception → analysis → comparison → connection → distinction → inference → judgment**

Reasonability begins with correctly apprehending the object.

---

# 8. Reasonability Requires Distinction

Isaiah 1 itself demonstrates distinctions.

The passage distinguishes:

- outward religious activity from acceptable obedience;
- sacrifice from righteousness;
- prayer from genuine response;
- assembly from moral conformity;
- evil from good;
- oppression from justice;
- rebellion from repentance.

This is why the passage is intellectually significant.

The Lord does not treat all religious activity as equivalent.

He distinguishes the outward form from the inward and moral reality.

A person may perform an externally recognizable act while violating the truth that gives the act its proper meaning.

The distinction is therefore necessary:

> **Religious appearance is not identical to conformity with truth.**

The same principle must govern every later investigation of institutions, traditions, doctrines, and interpretations.

---

# 9. Reasonability Requires Comparison

The passage also contains comparison.

The Lord places the existing condition alongside the possibility of transformation:

> **scarlet → white as snow**

> **crimson → wool**

The comparison communicates the magnitude of the change.

But the reader must not turn the imagery into a speculative system detached from the passage.

The comparison serves the argument.

It communicates a movement from the existing condition to the promised condition.

Thus even poetic language can carry precise argumentative force.

The reader must therefore be capable of understanding literary expression without abandoning intellectual discipline.

---

# 10. Reasonability Requires Following the Sequence

Isaiah 1:16–18 has an important movement:

> **Wash → make clean → put away evil → cease to do evil → learn to do well → seek judgment → relieve the oppressed → judge the fatherless → plead for the widow → come now → reason together**

The passage does not present reasonability as detached from conduct.

The intellectual encounter has practical consequences.

The people are not being called merely to win a debate.

They are being called to respond to what is true.

This is crucial for the wider doctrine of knowledge.

> **Knowledge that never reaches judgment and practice has not completed the movement demanded by truth.**

The learner therefore cannot separate intellectual formation from lived response.

---

# 11. Reasonability Requires Judgment

The Hebrew context itself makes judgment important.

Isaiah 1:17 commands:

> **“seek judgment.”**

The issue is therefore not merely information.

It is discernment between right and wrong and the practical determination to act accordingly.

This connects directly with the preceding chapter.

Dianoetic thinking culminates in judgment.

Reasonability is therefore not simply the ability to generate arguments.

It is the capacity to arrive at sound conclusions concerning what is true and what ought to be done.

That is why **sound judgment** is indispensable to the study of the Word.

---

# 12. Reasonability Is Not Relativism

A serious misunderstanding must be rejected.

If God says:

> **“let us reason together,”**

He is not saying:

> “There are no established truths; let every person construct his own.”

The opposite is true.

The Lord's summons assumes that there is a matter that can be rightly judged.

The people have sinned.

Their conduct is wrong.

God calls them to change.

There is therefore an objective moral reality.

Reasonability is the means of engaging that reality intelligently.

Thus:

> **Reasonability does not abolish truth; it presupposes truth.**

---

# 13. Reasonability Is Not Skepticism

Nor does reasoning require permanent suspension of judgment.

The objective is not:

> “Question everything forever.”

The objective is:

> **“Examine sufficiently to know what is established, then judge accordingly.”**

Skepticism can become another form of intellectual laziness when it refuses to recognize conclusions that the evidence adequately establishes.

A mature investigator therefore distinguishes:

- legitimate examination;
- unresolved questions;
- reasonable conclusions;
- and established conclusions.

Reasonability does not require perpetual uncertainty.

It requires proportionate certainty.

---

# 14. Reasonability and the Authority of the Word

The authority relationship can now be stated precisely.

The human mind does not confer authority upon God's Word.

Nor does the human mind manufacture the truth of God's Word.

Rather:

> **The Word presents truth; the human mind is responsible for understanding, reasoning through, judging, and responding to that truth.**

This is why the authority of Scripture does not eliminate intellectual work.

It makes intellectual work necessary.

If the text is authoritative, then misunderstanding it matters.

If the text is true, then misrepresenting it matters.

If the text contains arguments, then failing to follow those arguments matters.

If the text commands action, then knowledge that never becomes practice is deficient.

---

# 15. The Word Must Be Understood, Not Merely Repeated

This is where the lesson connects directly to the problem of quotation.

A quotation can be completely accurate and still be misunderstood.

The learner may know the words:

> **“Let us reason together.”**

and yet misunderstand what the Lord is doing in the passage.

He may turn the words into a slogan for autonomous philosophical debate.

He may detach them from Isaiah 1:16–17.

He may ignore the moral condition being addressed.

He may fail to recognize the relationship between judgment, repentance, and divine promise.

The quotation is correct.

The interpretation may still be wrong.

Therefore:

> **Accurate quotation is knowledge of the written words; mature understanding requires perceiving what those words assert, how they function in context, and what conclusions legitimately follow from them.**

This does not diminish quotation.

Quotation is valuable precisely because the written words are the object being studied.

The distinction is between **possessing the words** and **demonstrating competent understanding of what the words communicate**.

---

# 16. Isaiah 1:18 and the Meaning of “Reasonable”

The word **reasonable** can be used ambiguously.

It can mean:

- sensible;
- proportionate;
- rational;
- intelligible;
- defensible;
- or consistent with sound judgment.

The present investigation uses it in the disciplined sense.

A reasonable judgment is one that can be supported by adequate grounds and whose conclusion is proportionate to those grounds.

It is not merely:

> “What seems reasonable to me.”

That phrase can conceal personal preference.

The question is:

> **Reasonable according to what grounds, what evidence, what propositions, and what inferential structure?**

The preceding chapters provide those categories.

Isaiah 1:18 now supplies the biblical anchor showing that the exercise of such judgment is not foreign to engagement with God's Word.

---

# 17. The Lord's Reasoning Is Not a Human Debate Between Equals

The expression:

> **“let us reason together”**

must not be misunderstood as placing God and humanity on an epistemically equal platform.

The Lord is not a participant who possesses no more knowledge than the human being.

He is the One issuing the summons.

The human being is the one being confronted.

The point is not equality of authority.

The point is meaningful engagement.

God calls the person to consider the matter.

That distinction is vital.

> **Reasonability does not make God subject to human judgment as though He were merely another claimant.**

Rather, God calls the human being to judgment in light of what He has revealed.

---

# 18. Reasonability and Divine Revelation

This gives us a broader epistemological pattern.

When God reveals something:

1. the revelation presents content;
2. the hearer or reader receives it;
3. the mind perceives and understands it;
4. propositions are identified;
5. relationships are examined;
6. implications are followed;
7. conclusions are judged;
8. the person responds.

Revelation therefore does not bypass the intellectual faculties.

It gives them an object they could not independently manufacture.

This distinction will become increasingly important when the book later examines how the written record was given.

---

# 19. Why This Matters for the Doctrine of the Graphē

The Doctrine of the Graphē cannot be reduced to:

> “The Bible is authoritative.”

That conclusion has to become intellectually operative.

If the Graphē is the written authority, then the learner must know how to engage it.

He must be able to:

- read accurately;
- perceive propositions;
- understand language;
- recognize context;
- distinguish speakers;
- identify arguments;
- compare passages;
- detect relationships;
- distinguish interpretation from text;
- infer carefully;
- judge soundly;
- and apply what is established.

This is why reasonability belongs before the full treatment of Scripture.

The authority of the written Word creates a corresponding responsibility in the reader.

---

# 20. Isaiah 1:18 and the Scholarly Character of Scripture

The scholarly character of Scripture is not established merely because Scripture is difficult.

Difficulty alone is not scholarship.

Nor is antiquity.

Nor is cultural influence.

The scholarly impetus arises from the scope, depth, structure, claims, subject matter, internal relationships, historical material, prophetic material, moral instruction, wisdom, testimony, and intellectual demands of the writings.

Isaiah 1:18 illustrates one aspect of this.

The reader is expected to engage a divine communication intelligently.

The passage contains:

- a historical and moral setting;
- a diagnosis;
- commands;
- distinctions;
- an invitation;
- an argument;
- and a promised consequence.

A superficial reading can miss the architecture.

A scholarly reading examines it.

This is why the book will later ask what happens when people fail to recognize the scholarly impetus of Scripture.

---

# 21. Reasonability and the Failure of Skill

The connection to Hebrews 5 must be made carefully.

Hebrews 5:11–14 describes a condition in which the hearers have become:

> **“dull of hearing.”**

The writer says:

> **“For every one that useth milk is unskilful in the word of righteousness: for he is a babe.”**

And:

> **“But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil.”**

The passage connects maturity with **use**, exercise, and discernment.

The problem is therefore not merely the absence of information.

There is a deficiency of developed capacity.

That observation will become important later.

The present chapter establishes the intellectual requirement:

> **Reasonability is necessary for competent engagement with the Word.**

Later chapters will examine what happens when that capacity is absent.

---

# 22. Reasonability and the Responsibility to Learn

The summons of Isaiah 1:18 has an educational consequence.

If the Lord calls people to reason concerning what He has revealed, then the learner cannot legitimately treat ignorance as an ideal.

He must learn.

He must study.

He must examine.

He must exercise judgment.

He must become competent.

This is not intellectual pride.

It is responsibility.

The Word deserves careful treatment precisely because it is the Word.

The more authoritative the object, the more serious the obligation to handle it accurately.

---

# 23. What Reasonability Does Not Permit

Isaiah 1:18 does not authorize the reader to:

- invent doctrines;
- impose personal preferences upon Scripture;
- disregard context;
- turn metaphor into arbitrary allegory;
- ignore explicit statements;
- create contradictions where none exist;
- erase genuine distinctions;
- treat every interpretation as equally valid;
- elevate institutional tradition above the written Word;
- or replace Scripture with philosophical speculation.

Reasonability is disciplined.

It is accountable.

It is truth-directed.

It must therefore operate according to the intellectual standards developed throughout Movement I.

---

# 24. What Reasonability Does Require

Reasonability requires the learner to:

1. **Attend** to what is written.
2. **Perceive** the actual proposition.
3. **Understand** the terms.
4. **Examine** the context.
5. **Analyze** the structure.
6. **Compare** relevant evidence.
7. **Distinguish** what differs.
8. **Connect** what genuinely belongs together.
9. **Infer** what follows.
10. **Judge** the conclusion.
11. **Respond** according to the truth established.

This is not a mechanical formula.

It is a disciplined posture toward knowledge.

---

# 25. The Deeper Meaning of “Together”

The word **“together”** should also be read carefully.

The Lord is addressing persons.

The summons creates a communicative encounter.

The human being is not being told merely to contemplate an abstract proposition.

He is being confronted by a speaking God.

This has an important implication for the nature of revelation.

Truth is not presented in Scripture as merely an impersonal collection of propositions.

The writings record God's dealings, speech, acts, commands, promises, judgments, and purposes.

Yet the human response remains intellectual as well as moral.

The person must understand what has been communicated.

---

# 26. Reasonability Leads to Response

Isaiah 1:18 does not end the argument.

The verse continues:

> **“If ye be willing and obedient, ye shall eat the good of the land.”**  
> — Isaiah 1:19

And:

> **“But if ye refuse and rebel, ye shall be devoured with the sword: for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it.”**  
> — Isaiah 1:20

The passage therefore moves:

> **Revelation → reasoning → judgment → response → consequence**

This is a powerful model for the relationship between knowledge and life.

The purpose of intellectual engagement is not merely to accumulate propositions.

Truth demands an appropriate response.

---

# 27. “For the Mouth of the LORD Hath Spoken It”

The final phrase is especially important:

> **“for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it.”**

The reasoning and the authority are therefore not independent.

The Lord's Word grounds the matter.

The people are called to reason about what He has said.

The authority belongs to the One who has spoken.

This anticipates the central doctrine that the book will eventually establish concerning the written Word.

Reasonability is therefore **downstream from revelation**, not its replacement.

---

# 28. A Model for Reading Scripture

The Isaiah 1:18 model can now be generalized.

When approaching a passage, ask:

### What has God communicated?

Identify the actual content.

### What is the context?

Determine the setting and argument.

### What distinctions does the passage make?

Do not collapse unlike things.

### What relationships does the passage establish?

Do not invent relationships that are absent.

### What conclusion follows?

Trace the argument.

### What response is required?

Move from understanding to judgment and practice.

This is the intellectual discipline that the Doctrine of the Graphē requires.

---

# 29. The Place of Reason in the Hierarchy of Authority

The hierarchy must remain clear.

Reason is an instrument of knowing.

It is not the final source of revealed truth.

Human interpretation is an act of understanding.

It is not identical with the authority of the text.

Institutions can preserve, teach, transmit, and discuss Scripture.

They do not become Scripture merely by doing so.

Commentaries can illuminate.

They do not become the Word merely by discussing the Word.

Books can teach.

They do not acquire divine authority merely because they quote Scripture.

The governing distinction is:

> **Authority belongs to the truth itself and, in the later argument, to the authoritative written revelation established as the Graphē. Reason is the disciplined faculty by which the learner understands and reasons through that truth.**

---

# 30. The Question Isaiah 1:18 Leaves Us With

Isaiah 1:18 answers one important question:

> **Does engagement with God's Word require reasonability?**

Yes.

But it raises another:

> **What happens when people possess the Word but lack the skill to reason through it properly?**

The answer requires moving beyond reasonability itself into the biblical diagnosis of intellectual and scriptural unskillfulness.

Hebrews 5:11–14 provides that diagnosis.

The writer does not merely say that his readers lack information.

He identifies:

- dull hearing;
- immaturity;
- unskillfulness in the Word of righteousness;
- the need for milk;
- the distinction between milk and strong meat;
- and exercised senses capable of discerning good and evil.

The next movement must therefore investigate the relationship between **reasonability, skill, maturity, and doctrinal failure**.

---

# Chapter Conclusion

Isaiah 1:18 cannot responsibly be reduced to the inspirational phrase:

> **“Let us reason together.”**

Its context gives the phrase substance.

The Lord confronts a real condition.

He distinguishes outward activity from genuine righteousness.

He commands correction.

He summons the people to consider the matter.

He presents a real alternative.

He promises a real consequence.

And He concludes with the authority of His own spoken Word.

The verse therefore provides a profound biblical foundation for reasonability.

> **Reasonability does not create truth.**

> **Reasonability engages truth.**

> **Reasonability does not replace revelation.**

> **Reasonability enables the human mind to understand and respond to revelation.**

> **Reasonability does not make human interpretation authoritative.**

> **Reasonability disciplines interpretation under the truth being investigated.**

The proper intellectual movement is therefore:

> **God speaks → the Word is received → the mind perceives → the matter is analyzed → distinctions are recognized → relationships are examined → implications are followed → judgment is made → truth is obeyed.**

This is why the study of Scripture cannot be reduced to passive reading or mechanical quotation.

The Word must be **heard, understood, reasoned through, judged, exercised, and lived**.

And this raises the next question:

> **What happens when a person has access to the Word but remains unskilled in handling it?**

That question leads directly into the biblical diagnosis of immaturity.

# Chapter Reference Notes

## Isaiah 1:18 in immediate context

The chapter should be read together with Isaiah 1:11–20, particularly:

- **Isaiah 1:11** — the multitude of sacrifices;
- **Isaiah 1:13–15** — outward religious observance rejected because of moral corruption;
- **Isaiah 1:16–17** — cleansing, learning to do well, seeking judgment, and defending the oppressed;
- **Isaiah 1:18** — the summons to reason;
- **Isaiah 1:19–20** — willingness and obedience contrasted with refusal and rebellion.

## Hebrew term

**יָכַח — *yākaḥ***

In Isaiah 1:18, this verb is translated **“reason”** in the King James Version. Its semantic range includes ideas associated with deciding, judging, proving, reproving, correcting, and reasoning a matter through.

The chapter uses the term to illuminate the **reasonability and judgment** involved in the divine summons. It does not treat the lexical definition alone as the complete theology of the verse; the verse's context establishes how the summons functions.

## Governing distinctions

| Distinction | Principle |
|---|---|
| Reason / truth | Reason engages truth; it does not create truth. |
| Reason / revelation | Reason responds to and reasons through revelation; it does not replace revelation. |
| Reason / autonomy | Reasonability is not intellectual autonomy. |
| Reason / relativism | “Reason together” does not mean every conclusion is equally valid. |
| Quotation / understanding | Accurate quotation is not identical to demonstrated understanding. |
| Interpretation / text | An interpretation must not be silently substituted for the written proposition. |
| Religious activity / conformity to truth | Outward activity does not establish inward or moral conformity to God's truth. |
| Information / skill | Possession of information does not automatically constitute skill in handling the Word. |
| Knowledge / response | Biblical knowledge culminates in judgment and appropriate response. |

## Inferential movement of Isaiah 1:18–20

> **Existing condition → divine diagnosis → commanded correction → summons to reason → offered transformation → willingness/obedience → blessing**

versus:

> **Existing condition → refusal/rebellion → stated consequence**

The passage therefore demonstrates that reasonability is embedded within a larger movement from **truth to judgment to response**.

---

# Authoring Guardrails

> **Do not quote Isaiah 1:18 without examining Isaiah 1:11–20.**

> **Do not turn “let us reason together” into a slogan for autonomous human reasoning.**

> **Do not make reason the source of truth.**

> **Do not make revelation irrational merely because it is revealed.**

> **Do not reduce reasonability to personal preference or whatever “seems reasonable.”**

> **Do not use the Hebrew lexical range as a substitute for contextual interpretation.**

> **Do not claim that Isaiah 1:18 teaches everything that later chapters establish. Use it for what it actually establishes and build the larger argument cumulatively.**

> **Do not confuse accurate quotation with mature understanding.**

> **Do not separate intellectual engagement from the moral and practical response demanded by the passage.**

> **Do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture. Teach the reader to reason through Scripture.**
