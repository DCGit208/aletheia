# 47.3 Define the Question: Eyewitness Testimony as Positioned Knowledge

An **eyewitness**, in this chapter, is a person whose knowledge of an event is knowledge of what that person saw and heard from a position that made such knowledge possible.

**Eyewitness testimony**, in this argument, is the communication of that knowledge. It is not the mere fact that a body occupied a space near an event. It is not a later homily, commentary, or reconstruction offered by someone who was not so positioned. It is positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard, given as testimony.

Three marks belong to the definition.

First, **position**. The witness occupied a place and a time from which the event could be known. Position is geographical, temporal, and personal. Peter, James, and John on the mountain were positioned for the Transfiguration. Mary Magdalene at the tomb on the first day of the week was positioned for the discovery of emptiness. Paul on the Damascus road was positioned for the appearance he claims. A later reader of their writings is not so positioned. The later reader receives testimony.

Second, **knowledge**. Position without knowledge is not eyewitness. A man may stand in a crowd at a crucifixion and know only that a condemned person died. Another may stand nearer, hear the inscription, see the women, and know specified facts. Eyewitness is knowledge, not occupancy. The biblical language is the language of seeing, hearing, beholding, handling, and then bearing witness — a movement from encounter to knowledge to speech.

Third, **what was seen and heard**. The object is sensory encounter of a specified event, not later interpretation detached from that encounter. Interpretation may follow. Chapter 35 already distinguished observation from interpretation. This chapter takes that distinction as a premise. The eyewitness claim is a claim about the encounter. Later commentary may be true, false, or mixed. It is a different kind of speech.

**Central authentication line**, in this chapter, names the function of that testimony inside Movement VI. The locked TOC assigns this chapter eyewitness evidence, including 2 Peter 1:16 and related apostolic testimony. The assignment is therefore not a free selection of pious scenes of seeing. It is the examination of what the record places as the human link between the authenticated Person and later knowledge of Him.

The question of this chapter joins those definitions:

> **Does the authentic testimony present positioned witnesses of what was seen and heard concerning this Person — of His majesty in the ministry and of His raising — such that their testimony functions as a central authentication line of Movement VI?**

Several elements belong to the question as this chapter defines it.

First, **explicit biblical grounding**. 2 Peter 1:16–18 does not merely illustrate a modern category. It names the category: eyewitnesses of His majesty, over against fables, with seeing and hearing specified, and with the holy mount as the place.

Second, **the resurrection appearances as eyewitness events**. Chapter 46 established that "he was seen" belongs to the hinge. This chapter examines that seeing as testimony: who was positioned, what they claim to have seen, how the claim is given.

Third, **other positioned witnesses of the public ministry**, given proportional force: disciples who companied with Him from John's baptism, women who marked the tomb and first reported emptiness, named lists that make inquiry possible.

Fourth, **examinability**. These are claims inside a warranted written testimony about a historical Person. They can be defined, distinguished, compared with reductions, and reasoned through. They are not exempt from investigation because they are theological. They are not reduced to psychology because they are personal.

Thus:

> **The question of the eyewitness testimony is the question of positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard concerning the historical Person already established, under the writings already warranted as testimony, with 2 Peter 1:16–18 as an explicit grounding of the category and with the resurrection appearances as claimed eyewitness events of the raising.**

# 47.4 Distinguish Six Objects That Must Not Be Collapsed

The investigation fails at the first step if it treats six different objects as one.

## 47.4.1 Historical existence, identity, claims-and-works, and the hinge

These are the objects of Chapters 43–46. They include a real person, identity with the Truth, the public authentication axis of claims and works, and death-and-resurrection as the central historical claims of the career's end. Those conclusions are premises here. This chapter does not re-try them. It examines the seers of the Person so established.

## 47.4.2 Eyewitness testimony as positioned knowledge

This is the object of the present chapter. It includes: the definition of eyewitness as positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard; 2 Peter 1:16–18 in context as explicit grounding; the Transfiguration as the named event of that claim; the resurrection appearances as claimed eyewitness events; other positioned witnesses of the public ministry with proportional force; the failure of specified reductions; and the warrant for treating this testimony as a central authentication line of Movement VI.

## 47.4.3 Testimony in general

Not all testimony is eyewitness testimony. Chapter 18 already distinguished direct eyewitness, participant-witness, investigative witness, documentary or transmitted witness, prophetic witness, and revelatory witness. Luke investigates what eyewitnesses delivered. Paul receives and delivers a formula he also confirms by his own seeing. A prophet bears witness of what he received. Those are real forms of testimony. They are not this chapter's defined object. Collapsing them into "eyewitness" inflates the word until it means every speaker. Restricting "testimony" to eyewitness starves the record of forms it actually uses. The distinction protects both.

## 47.4.4 Mere presence

A crowd is not a panel of eyewitnesses. Presence is a necessary condition of ordinary seeing. It is not a sufficient condition of positioned knowledge. The Gospels themselves distinguish the many who saw signs from the few who are named as witnesses of specified events. Acts 10:41 distinguishes showing Him openly from showing Him to all the people. Presence without knowledge, or presence without specified encounter, is not the object under examination.

## 47.4.5 Later commentary

A later writer may speak truly about an event he did not see. He may also speak falsely. In either case his speech is not eyewitness testimony. Homily, theology, liturgy, and reconstruction are further operations. They may rest upon eyewitness testimony. They are not substitutes for it. The investigator who treats a second-century interpretation of the Transfiguration as if it were Peter's "we were with him in the holy mount" has changed the object.

## 47.4.6 Confirmation of Scripture and apostolic authority as office

Chapter 48 examines Christ's treatment of the Scriptures He received, quoted, interpreted, and appealed to as authoritative. Chapters 49–50 examine His relationship to the apostles and the authority of their testimony. Seeing the Transfiguration is not yet His confirmation of the Graphē. Being an eyewitness of the resurrection is a qualification named in Acts 1:22; it is not yet the finished doctrine that apostolic testimony carries His authority as a closed case of office. Those files remain later because they are different questions.

The governing distinction is therefore:

> **Prior Movement VI conclusions ≠ eyewitness as positioned knowledge ≠ testimony in general ≠ mere presence ≠ later commentary ≠ Scripture-confirmation and apostolic-office files.**

Each is a real object. Each has its place. This chapter takes the second.

# 47.6 Eyewitness Defined: Positioned Knowledge of What Was Seen and Heard

The definition may now be stated with the precision the argument requires.

An eyewitness is not "anyone who was nearby." An eyewitness is a person who, from a position that made knowledge possible, saw and heard a specified event, and whose testimony is the communication of that knowledge.

**Positioned** names the relation between the person and the event. The mountain, the tomb, the locked room, the lakeside, the Damascus road — these are not decorations. They are the places at which knowledge of this kind is claimed. Without position, "I saw" is an empty verb.

**Knowledge** names the cognitive content. The witness knows that this occurred, not merely that he occupied a space while something occurred. The biblical movement is from encounter to witness-speech: "that which we have seen and heard declare we unto you" (1 John 1:3). Declaration without prior seeing and hearing is a different speech. Seeing and hearing without declaration may be private. Testimony is the joining of the two.

**What was seen and heard** names the object as sensory encounter. The Transfiguration is seen majesty and heard voice. The appearances are seeing of the raised One, and in specified narratives hearing, touching, and eating. The tomb-morning is seeing of emptiness and hearing of announcement. These are not metaphors of insight. They are claims of encounter in the world.

The definition excludes two counterfeits at once.

It excludes the reduction of eyewitness to **mere presence**. Occupancy is not knowledge.

It excludes the inflation of eyewitness to **later commentary**. A true sentence about an unseen event is not an eyewitness sentence.

Thus:

> **Eyewitness testimony in this argument is positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard, communicated as testimony. Presence without that knowledge is not the object. Later speech without that position is not the object.**

# 47.7 Presence Is Not Eyewitness

The distinction requires its own statement because the word "witness" is commonly stretched to cover everyone who was in the vicinity.

The Gospels themselves refuse the stretch. Crowds follow. Crowds eat bread. Crowds see signs. Crowds cry "Hosanna" and "Crucify." Those crowds are historically real. They are not, by that fact, the eyewitness file. John 6:26 records that many sought Him because they ate of the loaves and were filled, not because they saw signs as signs. Presence at a feeding is not positioned knowledge of what the feeding meant, and it is not even, in that sentence, positioned knowledge of the sign as sign.

Acts 10:40–41 is explicit about a further limit. God raised Him and "shewed him openly; not to all the people, but unto witnesses chosen before of God, even to us, who did eat and drink with him after he rose from the dead." Open showing, not to all the people. The appearances are real encounters in the world. They are not a demonstration before Pilate's tribunal. The limitation is the record's description of the mode. Chosen witnesses, including table-fellowship after the raising, are the positioned class. The city that knew the crucifixion is not thereby a city of eyewitnesses of the raising.

The same distinction applies to the Transfiguration. Matthew 17:1, Mark 9:2, and Luke 9:28 name three: Peter, James, and John. The rest of the Twelve are nearby in the narrative world and are not on the mountain. Presence in the company of Jesus is not presence on the holy mount. 2 Peter 1:18 says "when we were with him in the holy mount." The "we" is the positioned we, not the census of all disciples.

Presence remains relevant. It is the ordinary condition of seeing. Chapter 35 already made opportunity to know foundational to credibility. This chapter does not retract that. It refuses to treat opportunity as identical with testimony. Opportunity is a condition. Eyewitness is knowledge under that condition, given as speech.

Thus:

> **Mere presence is a condition of ordinary seeing. It is not eyewitness testimony. The record itself names specified seers of specified events and distinguishes them from the surrounding many.**

# 47.8 Later Commentary Is Not Eyewitness

The distinction requires its own statement because later speech about Christ is abundant, and some of it is true.

A commentary may correctly say that Jesus was transfigured. A liturgy may correctly confess that He rose from the dead. A historian may correctly conclude that the crucifixion occurred under Pilate. None of those sentences, as later sentences, is eyewitness testimony. They may rest upon eyewitness testimony. They may also rest upon other grounds. The kind of speech they are is not the kind 2 Peter 1:16 is.

2 Peter 1:16 is first-person and event-located: we were eyewitnesses; we heard this voice; we were with Him in the holy mount. 1 John 1:1–3 is first-person and sensory: we have heard, seen with our eyes, looked upon, handled. John 19:35 is first-person or near-first-person about a specified act: he that saw it bare record. Luke 1:2 distinguishes those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses from the writer who investigated what they delivered. The record knows the difference between the seer and the later orderly account.

Chapter 14 already established that human accounts about God must themselves be tested and must not be confused with the biblical record. The present distinction is narrower and evidential. Later commentary is not being dismissed as worthless. It is being classified. Classification is the discipline of the whole book. A true later sentence does not become an eyewitness sentence by being true. A false later sentence does not become an eyewitness sentence by being old.

The investigator who treats the history of interpretation as if it were a growing cloud of eyewitnesses has multiplied speakers and thinned the object. The object is positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard. Later labour upon that testimony is a different labour. It has later chapters where interpretation, mishandling, and skill are the questions. It is not this file.

Thus:

> **Later commentary, even when true, is not eyewitness testimony. Eyewitness is the seer's positioned knowledge, given as testimony. Commentary is subsequent speech about that testimony or about the event.**

# 47.9 Eyewitness Distinguished from All Testimony

Testimony is the genus. Eyewitness is a species.

Chapter 18's taxonomy remains in force. Direct eyewitness is one form. Participant-witness may include more than a single glance: a companion of the ministry sees many things over time. Apostolic witness, as a mode of giving, includes commission and proclamation and is not exhausted by seeing. Investigative witness, as in Luke 1:1–4, is knowledge acquired by examining what eyewitnesses delivered. Documentary or transmitted witness is knowledge carried in writing or tradition. Prophetic witness is knowledge of what was received from God. Revelatory witness, as in the Apocalypse, is knowledge of what was shown. Christ as the faithful witness is unique and is not a member of the human panel.

The present chapter's object is the species: positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard concerning this Person. The genus remains real. Paul delivers what he received and also claims, "have I not seen Jesus Christ our Lord?" (1 Corinthians 9:1). The same man can stand in two relations: recipient of a formula, seer of an appearance. Luke can stand in a third: investigator of what eyewitnesses delivered. Collapsing these relations into one word produces either inflation ("everyone who speaks of Jesus is an eyewitness") or starvation ("only a person who wrote a Gospel from memory counts").

The distinction has a further use. It prevents the illicit demand that every biblical sentence be an eyewitness sentence before it may be believed. Genealogies, legal corpora, prophetic oracles, and psalms are not eyewitness reports of Galilean days. They are other forms, already examined under other chapters as other forms. This chapter does not conscript them. It examines the seers of Christ.

Thus:

> **Eyewitness is not all testimony. Testimony includes forms the record actually uses that are not positioned seeing. The authentication line of this chapter is the species, not the erasure of the genus.**

# 47.10 Apostolic Eyewitness Distinguished from Apostolic Office

The distinction requires its own statement because the same men often occupy both relations, and later chapters will examine the office.

An apostle, in the sense Chapter 27 already treated as a mode of giving, is a commissioned messenger. Acts 1:21–22 names companionship through the ministry and witness of the resurrection as qualifications for the one who would take Judas's place. Seeing is therefore relevant to office. It is not identical with office.

Peter is an eyewitness of the Transfiguration and of appearances, and he is an apostle. The first fact is this chapter's object. The second is Chapters 49–50. Paul claims both: he has seen the Lord, and he is an apostle (1 Corinthians 9:1; 15:8–9). The women at the tomb are eyewitnesses of emptiness and, in specified narratives, of appearance. They are not, by that seeing, made apostles in the sense of the Twelve. James the Lord's brother is named as a seer in 1 Corinthians 15:7 and as a pillar in Galatians 2:9. Seeing and office overlap in some lives and do not in others.

If this chapter treated every seer as already an apostle in the finished sense of authority, it would smuggle Chapter 50 into the mountain and the tomb. If it treated apostolic office as irrelevant to seeing, it would ignore Acts 1:22. The correct operation is distinction: eyewitness is positioned knowledge; office is commission and authority. The overlap is historically real. The identity of the two objects is not.

Thus:

> **Apostolic eyewitness is not the finished doctrine of apostolic office. Seeing is a qualification named for office. It is not yet the closed case that apostolic testimony carries His authority as Chapters 49–50 will examine it.**

# 47.11 Seeing and Hearing Distinguished from Confirmation of Scripture

The distinction requires its own statement because the same scenes often include both seeing and appeal to what is written.

Luke 24:25–27, 44–47 records that the raised Jesus interprets Moses, the prophets, and the psalms as concerning His suffering and rising. 2 Peter 1:19, immediately after the eyewitness claim, turns to "a more sure word of prophecy." The earliest formula says "according to the scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3–4). Acts 2 argues from Psalm 16. Those clauses are real. They are not this chapter's object.

Seeing the Transfiguration is not the same act as treating the Graphē as the court of truth. Hearing the Father's voice on the mountain is not the same act as Christ's quoting, interpreting, and appealing to the writings He received. Chapter 48 is assigned that treatment. This chapter records that seers saw and heard. Where a scene also includes His use of Scripture, the scene is noted as containing that further act, and the further act is left in its place.

2 Peter 1:19–21 will therefore be named as the turn the letter itself makes, from eyewitness of majesty to the prophetic word. The turn is not treated here as the finished doctrine of inspiration's guarantees, nor as the finished doctrine that He confirms the Graphē, nor as infallibility. Movement IV already treated inspiration as a biblical category. Movement VII will secure guarantees. Chapter 48 will examine His confirmation. The present file stops where the letter's eyewitness claim stops: we saw, we heard, we were with Him in the holy mount.

Thus:

> **Seeing and hearing are not yet Christ's confirmation of Scripture. Scenes that contain both acts are not thereby a license to consummate Chapter 48. The eyewitness line is the seers' positioned knowledge. The Graphē-argument remains the next movement of Movement VI, not this one.**

# 47.12 The Biblical Language of Witness

The New Testament's language of witness is not a single English word stretched over every speaker. It is a field of verbs and nouns of seeing, hearing, and bearing record.

**μάρτυς (*martys*, G3144)** names a witness: one who bears testimony concerning what he knows. **μαρτυρέω (*martureō*, G3140)** is the verb: to bear witness, to testify. **μαρτυρία (*marturia*, G3141)** and **μαρτύριον (*marturion*, G3142)** name the testimony or the witness borne. The field is legal and historical before it is later associated with death for the testimony. A martyr in that later sense is a witness whose testimony has cost his life. The cost is evidentially relevant, as Chapter 35 already established. It is not the definition of the word.

**ὁράω (*horaō*, G3708)** and **εἶδον (*eidon*)** are the ordinary verbs of seeing. 1 Corinthians 15:5–8 uses **ὤφθη (*ōphthē*)**, "he was seen," of the appearances. Chapter 46 already noted that the verb's range includes theophany and ordinary seeing, and that sequence controls the verb: died, buried, raised, seen. This chapter takes that conclusion as a premise and uses the verb as the record's own word for the appearance-encounters.

**ἀκούω (*akouō*, G191)** is hearing. 2 Peter 1:18: "we heard this voice." 1 John 1:1: "which we have heard." Acts 4:20: "the things which we have seen and heard."

**θεάομαι (*theaomai*, G2300)** is beholding, looking upon. 1 John 1:1 uses it alongside seeing with the eyes. John 1:14: "we beheld his glory."

**ψηλαφάω (*psēlaphaō*, G5584)** is handling, feeling. 1 John 1:1: "our hands have handled." Luke 24:39 invites handling: "handle me, and see."

The language is concrete. It is the language of encounter in the world, not the language of a school that happened to use sensory metaphors for insight. The investigator who translates all of it into "they had a powerful experience" has performed a reduction, not an exegesis.

Thus:

> **The biblical language of witness is a field of seeing, hearing, beholding, handling, and bearing record. The authentication line of this chapter is that field applied to this Person, not a modern slogan laid upon the text.**

# 47.13 Lexical Grounding: *Epoptai*, *Autoptai*, *Martys*

Three nouns require particular notice because the chapter's assignment names 2 Peter 1:16 and related apostolic testimony.

**ἐπόπτης (*epoptēs*, G2030)**, plural **ἐπόπται (*epoptai*)**, is the word 2 Peter 1:16 uses for "eyewitnesses." It denotes a spectator, one who looks upon, an eyewitness. In wider Greek it can carry the overtone of one admitted to see what is shown, including the language of sacred spectacle. The New Testament uses it here. Context, not a mystery-religion reconstruction, controls the sense. Peter is not claiming initiation into a cult. He is claiming that they looked upon Christ's majesty, and he immediately specifies the claim by the Father's voice and the holy mount. The word is a seeing-word. It is not a commentary-word.

**αὐτόπτης (*autoptēs*, G845)**, plural **αὐτόπται (*autoptai*)**, is Luke 1:2's word for those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses. The formation is transparent: seeing for oneself. Luke places those seers as the source from which the things were delivered, and then places his own labour as investigation of what was delivered. The word distinguishes the seer from the later orderly writer. That distinction is this chapter's distinction between eyewitness and later account, already present in the prologue of a Gospel.

**μάρτυς (*martys*, G3144)** remains the broader noun of witness. Revelation 1:5 calls Christ **ὁ μάρτυς ὁ πιστός**, "the faithful witness." That unique title was Chapter 18's and Chapter 26's object as a mode of giving, and Chapter 44's as identity. It is not a member of the human eyewitness panel. Human μάρτυρες in Acts are those who bear witness of what they have seen and heard, especially of the resurrection (Acts 1:8, 22; 2:32; 3:15; 5:32; 10:39–41; 13:31). The overlap of *martys* with eyewitness is historically real in those sermons. The identity of *martys* with eyewitness in every use is not. A witness may testify to what he received. An eyewitness testifies to what he saw and heard.

Lexical range does not by itself prove a theological conclusion. Chapter 21 already established that rule. The contribution of these nouns is more modest and more useful: the record has words for seers as seers, and it uses them of this Person's majesty and of the delivery of the gospel-tradition. The argument is not etymology. It is the use of the words in their sentences.

Thus:

> **Epoptai in 2 Peter 1:16, autoptai in Luke 1:2, and martys in the Acts sermons are the record's own names for seers and witnesses. They ground the category in the text. They do not replace the sentences in which they stand.**

---

# 47.14 2 Peter 1:16–18 in Context

2 Peter 1:16–18 is the chapter's assigned explicit grounding. It must be read as sentences in a letter, not as a detached slogan.

Peter has just written that he will not be negligent to put them always in remembrance of these things, though they know them, and that he thinks it meet, as long as he is in this tabernacle, to stir them up, knowing that shortly he must put off this tabernacle, even as the Lord Jesus Christ hath shewed him, and that he will endeavour that after his decease they may be able to have these things always in remembrance (2 Peter 1:12–15). The letter presents a man providing for memory after his death. The next sentences are the ground of what has been made known, not an ornamental vision-report.

He writes:

> "For we have not followed cunningly devised fables, when we made known unto you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but were eyewitnesses of his majesty. For he received from God the Father honour and glory, when there came such a voice to him from the excellent glory, This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. And this voice which came from heaven we heard, when we were with him in the holy mount."
> — 2 Peter 1:16–18

The unit has a structure.

**A rival is refused.** "Cunningly devised fables" — σεσοφισμένοις μύθοις (*sesophismenois muthois*) — are sophisticated myths, artfully constructed stories. The proclamation of the Lord's power and coming is set over against that class. The sentence is a classification-claim: this is not that.

**A positive ground is stated.** "But were eyewitnesses of his majesty." Ἐπόπται γενηθέντες τῆς ἐκείνου μεγαλειότητος: having become eyewitnesses of that one's μεγαλειότης (*megaleiotēs*, G3168), majesty, magnificence, greatness. The object is His majesty, not their insight.

**The ground is specified.** He received from God the Father honour and glory. A voice came to Him from the excellent glory: "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased." The honour and glory are not a private estimate. They are the Father's ascription, given as a voice.

**The specification is sensory and located.** "This voice which came from heaven we heard, when we were with him in the holy mount." Hearing is named. Company is named: we were with Him. Place is named: the holy mount. The claim is not "we have always felt Him to be glorious." It is "we were there; we saw; we heard."

The "we" of these verses is the we of the mountain, not the we of all later Christians. Peter writes as one of a small company. The Synoptic Transfiguration narratives name the company: Peter, James, and John. The letter does not here name James and John. It does not need to. It names the event by its marks: majesty, the Father's voice, the beloved Son, the mount.

What is being made known in the letter is "the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ" — δύναμιν καὶ παρουσίαν (*dunamin kai parousian*). The Transfiguration is presented as the eyewitness ground of that making-known, not as a curiosity of biography. Majesty seen on the mount is the exhibited glory of the One whose power and coming have been proclaimed. Chapter 44 already identified Him as the Truth and the revealer of the Father. This chapter does not re-try that identity. It records that the identity so named has an eyewitness claim attached to a specified event.

Thus:

> **2 Peter 1:16–18 is an explicit biblical grounding of eyewitness as positioned knowledge: a rival class (fables) is refused; seeing of majesty is claimed; the Father's voice is heard; the holy mount is the place; the company is "we" with Him. The category is not imported. It is the letter's own argument.**

# 47.15 "Cunningly Devised Fables" versus Eyewitnesses of His Majesty

The contrast in 2 Peter 1:16 is the letter's own authentication architecture in miniature.

On one side: σεσοφισμένοι μῦθοι — myths that have been given a craft, stories devised with sophistication. The accusation the letter refuses is not that someone has told a simple tale. It is that the proclamation might be classified with artful religious invention. The refusal is therefore a classification-refusal. The power and coming have been made known. That making-known is not a member of the fable-class.

On the other side: ἐπόπται of His majesty. The positive class is eyewitness. The object is majesty. The move is the move Chapter 33 named as authentication: a claim is shown to be genuine by relevant grounds. Here the ground offered is not institutional decree and not later consensus. It is positioned seeing.

The contrast is not a claim that no myths exist. It is a claim that this proclamation does not belong among them, and that the reason is eyewitness of majesty. A critic may dispute the seeing. He may not, without remainder, pretend that the letter failed to offer a ground. The ground is offered. It is this chapter's object to examine it.

The contrast also protects the definition. If the proclamation were later commentary upon an unseen Jesus, the fable-accusation would have a different shape: speech without position. The letter answers by naming position. If the proclamation were mere presence in Galilee, the majesty-claim would be too large for occupancy. The letter answers by naming what was seen and heard, not merely that they walked about.

Chapter 42 already used this contrast as a warrant-form for the writings as authentic testimony rather than fable. This chapter uses the same contrast as Movement VI's filling: the Person now located, identified, authenticated, and examined at the hinge is the One of whom Peter says, we were eyewitnesses of His majesty. The form is the same. The filling is the file.

Thus:

> **The refusal of cunningly devised fables is not a mood of sincerity. It is a classification-claim whose positive ground is eyewitness of majesty. Authentication here is the exhibited alternative to myth.**

# 47.16 The Transfiguration as the Named Event

The holy mount, the Father's voice, the beloved Son, honour and glory — these marks name the Transfiguration.

Matthew 17:1–8, Mark 9:2–8, and Luke 9:28–36 narrate the event. Jesus takes Peter, James, and John up a high mountain. He is transfigured before them. His face shines; His raiment is white. Moses and Elijah appear and speak with Him. A cloud overshadows them. A voice from the cloud says, in Matthew's form, "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased; hear ye him," and in Mark and Luke, "This is my beloved Son: hear him." The disciples fear. Jesus touches them. They see Jesus only.

2 Peter 1:17's wording of the voice is nearer Matthew's "beloved" and "well pleased" than Mark's and Luke's shorter form, and it does not here include "hear ye him." Difference of wording is not difference of event. Chapter 35 already established that agreement concerns substantive correspondence, not identical wording. The marks correspond: the three, the mountain, the glory, the Father's voice identifying the Son.

The event, as narrated, is public in the restricted sense this chapter has already used. It is not a demonstration to the nation. It is an event in the world, before three named companions, involving what is seen and what is heard. Luke 9:32 notes that they were heavy with sleep and, when they were awake, saw His glory and the two men. Seeing is named after waking. The narrative is capable of noting human limitation in the seers. Limitation of seers is not deletion of seeing.

Moses and Elijah belong to the scene. Their presence is not this chapter's confirmation of Scripture as Graphē-doctrine. It is part of what the seers are said to have seen. The Law and the Prophets appearing with the glorified Son is a theological architecture the later chapter on confirmation will have a right to examine. The present right is more limited and more basic: three men are claimed to have seen glory and heard a voice on a mountain. That is an eyewitness claim.

Peter's later letter treats the event as the ground of having made known the Lord's power and coming. The Transfiguration is therefore not, in the letter's argument, a private spiritual curiosity. It is the exhibited majesty of the One proclaimed. Chapter 45 already treated works as public witness to claims. The Transfiguration is a work of a particular kind: a disclosure of glory to positioned seers, with the Father's voice as interpretation. It belongs to the eyewitness file even more directly than to the works-catalogue, because the letter names it as the seeing.

Thus:

> **The Transfiguration is the named event of 2 Peter 1:16–18. Three Gospels narrate it with the same positioned company, the same mountain, the same glory, and the same Father's voice. The letter's eyewitness claim is a claim about that event.**

---

# 47.17 Visual and Auditory Testimony Together

2 Peter 1:16–18 joins seeing and hearing. The joining is evidentially relevant.

Majesty is the object of the eyewitness-noun. The voice is the object of the hearing-verb. Honour and glory are what He received from the Father when the voice came. The event is not exhausted by a light. It is not exhausted by a sentence. It is light and sentence together, glory and ascription together.

The Synoptic narratives exhibit the same joining. What is seen: face, raiment, Moses and Elijah, cloud. What is heard: the voice identifying the Son, and in Matthew and the two shorter forms the charge to hear Him. Mark 9:6 records Peter's speech about three tabernacles and adds that he wist not what to say, for they were sore afraid. The seers are not presented as calm professional observers. They are presented as men who saw and heard and were afraid and spoke poorly. That presentation is the opposite of a polished fable's preference for dignified witnesses. It is the presentation of positioned knowledge under stress.

Chapter 35 distinguished observation from interpretation. The Father's voice is interpretation given inside the event, not a later homily. The seers see glory. The voice says who He is. The joining of visual and auditory testimony is therefore also a joining of encounter and ascription at the scene, not a later caption added in a study.

A reduction that treats the Transfiguration as a dream must then take both the light and the sentence as dream. A reduction that treats the voice as later liturgy must explain why the letter locates the hearing on the mount with Him. A reduction that treats the glory as insight without event must explain the mountain, the three, and the sleep Luke notes. The joining of seen and heard is part of the object. Splitting them is an operation performed upon the testimony, not an operation the testimony performs.

Thus:

> **The eyewitness claim of 2 Peter 1:16–18 is visual and auditory together: majesty seen, voice heard, place named, company named. The joining is the event as the seers present it.**

# 47.18 Why 2 Peter Grounds the Category for Movement VI

The assignment named 2 Peter 1:16 because the verse does what a grounding text must do.

It **names the category**. Eyewitnesses — ἐπόπται — of His majesty. The word is a seeing-word used of this Person.

It **names the rival**. Cunningly devised fables. Authentication is exhibited as the alternative to myth, not as a mood.

It **specifies the event**. Honour and glory from the Father, the voice, the beloved Son, the holy mount, "we were with him."

It **joins seeing and hearing**. Positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard, not occupancy, not later caption.

It **attaches the seeing to proclamation**. "When we made known unto you the power and coming." The eyewitness claim is the ground of a making-known, not a private diary.

It **stands inside a letter providing for memory after the writer's death**. 2 Peter 1:12–15. The seeing is given as the ground of a tradition that must outlive the seer. That is the situation of every later reader. It is also the reason the seeing must be examined as testimony and not as a vanishing privilege.

Chapter 18 named the verse as category. Chapter 35 named it as exhibiting presence, seeing, hearing, testimony. Chapter 42 named it as warrant-form. This chapter names it as Movement VI's explicit grounding of the seers of this Person, now that the Person is located, identified, authenticated in the ministry, and examined at the hinge. The verse was never a decoration. It is now in its appointed place.

2 Peter 1:19–21 turns from this grounding to the prophetic word. The turn is real. It is not taken here. The eyewitness unit is 1:16–18. The prophetic unit is the next question's neighbour, not this question's substitute.

Thus:

> **2 Peter 1:16–18 grounds the category for Movement VI because it names eyewitness, refuses fable, specifies the Transfiguration, joins seeing and hearing, and attaches the seeing to the proclamation of this Person. The grounding is the letter's. The filling is this chapter's.**

# 47.19 Gospel Transfiguration Narratives as Corroborating Record

The three Synoptic narratives are independent in wording and common in substance. They are later written testimony of the event Peter's letter names in the first person.

Independence here is the independence Chapter 35 defined: not the absence of any relationship among the Gospels, but the presence of distinct selection, wording, and emphasis such that the common substance is not a single sentence copied three times. Matthew includes "in whom I am well pleased" and "hear ye him." Mark notes the raiment exceedingly white as no fuller on earth can white them, and Peter's ignorance of what to say. Luke dates the event about eight days after previous sayings, notes prayer, sleep, and the subject of the conversation: His decease which He should accomplish at Jerusalem. Those are not identical wording. They are corresponding testimony.

The corroboration is internal to the authentic testimony. It is not the corroboration of a Roman inscription of the Transfiguration. Chapter 39 already established that external corroboration is proposition-specific and that absence of an artifact is not automatic disproof. A mountain-event before three companions is not the class of public fact that generates imperial archives. It is the class that generates positioned testimony. The three Gospels and the letter are that testimony.

Luke's note that they spoke of His decease at Jerusalem joins the Transfiguration to the hinge Chapter 46 examined, without turning this chapter back into a re-trial of the death. The glory is disclosed on the way to the cross. The seers of majesty are seers on a path that runs to Golgotha. The joining is the narrative's. It is not a license to collapse Chapter 46 into Chapter 47.

Mark 9:9–10 and parallels record that He charged them to tell no man what they had seen till the Son of man were risen from the dead, and that they questioned among themselves what the rising from the dead should mean. The charge of silence until the raising is a narrative link between the mountain and the appearances. The dedicated appearance-file follows. The present point is that the Transfiguration is not, in the Gospels, a rival Easter. It is a disclosure of majesty before the death, remembered as seen, and in Peter's letter used as the eyewitness ground of proclamation.

Thus:

> **The Synoptic Transfiguration narratives are corroborating written testimony of the event 2 Peter 1:16–18 names. Common substance with distinct selection is the form of correspondence Chapter 35 already warranted as evidentially relevant.**

# 47.20 1 John 1:1–3: Hearing, Seeing, Beholding, Handling

The opening of 1 John is related apostolic testimony of the same species, not a second Transfiguration.

> "That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled, of the Word of life; (For the life was manifested, and we have seen it, and bear witness, and shew unto you that eternal life, which was with the Father, and was manifested unto us;) That which we have seen and heard declare we unto you, that ye also may have fellowship with us."
> — 1 John 1:1–3

The movement is explicit:

> **manifestation → hearing → seeing → beholding → handling → bearing witness → showing → declaring → fellowship**

The object is "the Word of life," "that eternal life, which was with the Father, and was manifested unto us." Chapter 44 already treated the Word made flesh and the revelation of the Father. This chapter takes that identity as a premise and uses 1 John 1 for what it contributes to the eyewitness file: the proclamation is presented as arising from sensory encounter with the manifested life.

Four verbs of encounter are stacked: heard, seen with the eyes, looked upon, handled. The stack is not rhetorical waste. Hearing might be rumour. Seeing with the eyes narrows it. Looking upon — θεάομαι — is beholding, not a glance. Handling is tactile. The incarnation Chapter 35 already named as the deepest pattern of witness is here the condition of the stack: the Word of life was manifested in a form that could be heard, seen, beheld, and handled.

The purpose-clause is fellowship, not a courtroom. The evidential form remains historical. A philosophical principle is not handled with hands. A later commentary is not "which we have seen with our eyes." The letter presents the authors as positioned knowers of a manifested life, declaring what they encountered so that the readers may have fellowship with them. The readers become fellow-partakers of the testimony, not fellow-eyewitnesses of the manifestation.

Thus:

> **1 John 1:1–3 is related apostolic testimony that the proclamation rests on positioned sensory encounter with the manifested Word of life. It is the same species as 2 Peter 1:16–18: seeing and hearing as the ground of declaring, not as a metaphor of later insight.**

# 47.21 John 19:35 and John 21:24: Written Witness of a Seer

John 19:35 connects a specified historical act with a witness, a record, and a purpose.

The act is the piercing of Jesus' side, with blood and water coming out, after His death, in a passion-narrative already examined as hinge in Chapter 46. The present use is the witness-clause:

> "And he that saw it bare record, and his record is true: and he knoweth that he saith true, that ye might believe."
> — John 19:35

The structure is:

> **event → seer → record → claim of truthfulness → purpose (that ye might believe)**

This is eyewitness as this chapter defines it. Position: he that saw it. Knowledge: the specified act. Testimony: he bare record. Writing: the record. The later reader is not the seer. The later reader is given the record of the seer.

John 21:24 identifies the disciple as the one who testifies of these things and wrote them, and adds a communal affirmation:

> "This is the disciple which testifieth of these things, and wrote these things: and we know that his testimony is true."

Chapter 35 already used this verse for testimony within a network that can assess the witness. The present use is the same network as a condition of written eyewitness: the seer writes; others who know him affirm. That is not yet canonical recognition as Movement VII. It is the historical form of a community that can say of a named disciple, we know that his testimony is true.

John 1:14 belongs with these verses as related Johannine testimony: "the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory...)." Beholding glory is the Johannine companion of Peter's ἐπόπται of majesty. The object is this Person's glory. The we is the we of encounter. The sentence is not later commentary in the sense 47.8 defined. It is the prologue's claim that glory was beheld.

Thus:

> **John 19:35 and 21:24 present written eyewitness: a seer of a specified act bears record; the record is written; a community affirms the witness. John 1:14 names the beholding of glory as the incarnation's public consequence for those who dwelt with Him.**

# 47.22 Luke 1:1–4: *Autoptai* and Investigation

Luke's prologue is related testimony of a different relation inside the same architecture: the investigator of eyewitnesses.

> "Forasmuch as many have taken in hand to set forth in order a declaration of those things which are most surely believed among us, Even as they delivered them unto us, which from the beginning were eyewitnesses, and ministers of the word; It seemed good to me also, having had perfect understanding of all things from the very first, to write unto thee in order, most excellent Theophilus, That thou mightest know the certainty of those things, wherein thou hast been instructed."
> — Luke 1:1–4

The sequence is the sequence Chapter 18 already reconstructed:

> **events → eyewitnesses and ministers of the word → delivery → investigation → orderly writing → the reader's certainty**

αὐτόπται names the seers as those who saw for themselves, from the beginning. ὑπηρέται τοῦ λόγου names them also as ministers of the word: not mute spectators, but servants of the testimony. παρέδοσαν — they delivered. Luke's own labour is παρηκολουθηκότι ἄνωθεν πᾶσιν ἀκριβῶς: having followed all things closely from the first. The purpose is ἵνα ἐπιγνῷς ... τὴν ἀσφάλειαν: that Theophilus may know the certainty of what he has been taught.

Two conclusions belong to this chapter.

First, **Luke distinguishes himself from the eyewitnesses**. He does not claim to have been from the beginning a seer of the ministry. He claims to have investigated what the seers delivered. That is the distinction between eyewitness and later orderly account, written into the opening of a Gospel. The Gospel is not thereby dismissed. It is classified as investigated testimony of eyewitness delivery. Chapter 35 already established that a non-eyewitness can be a reliable historian. This chapter uses that conclusion.

Second, **the prologue makes eyewitness delivery the source of the things believed**. The faith of the community is not presented as a self-originating mood. It is presented as having been delivered by those who saw from the beginning. Certainty for a later reader is possible because investigation of that delivery is possible. That is this book's own epistemology applied to Christ: do not make the reader choose between reason and Scripture; teach the reader to reason through the testimony.

Acts 1:1–3 continues the same writer's claim: the former treatise of all that Jesus began to do and teach, until the day He was taken up, after He through the Holy Ghost had given commandments unto the apostles whom He had chosen, to whom He shewed Himself alive after His passion by many infallible proofs, being seen of them forty days. τεκμηρίοις — tokens, proofs. ὀπτανόμενος — being seen. The appearances are here summarized as showing alive by many proofs, over many days. Chapter 46 established the appearances as belonging to the hinge. This chapter records Luke's word for them as seeing, and his word for their function as proofs to the chosen apostles.

Thus:

> **Luke 1:1–4 grounds the related architecture: eyewitnesses from the beginning delivered; an investigator wrote in order; a later reader may know certainty. Luke is not himself the mountain-seer. He is the writer who treats those seers as the source.**

# 47.23 Acts 1:21–22: Companionship and Witness of the Resurrection

The choice of a replacement for Judas is not yet the closed doctrine of apostolic office. It is, in this chapter, a ranking of what an apostle must be able to attest, and a definition of the companionship that makes attestation possible.

> "Wherefore of these men which have companied with us all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us, Beginning from the baptism of John, unto that same day that he was taken up from us, must one be ordained to be a witness with us of his resurrection."
> — Acts 1:21–22

Two conditions are named.

**Companionship through the ministry.** "All the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us," from John's baptism to the taking up. This is positioned knowledge extended through a public career, not a single glance. It is the participant-witness Chapter 18 distinguished: a companion of the days.

**Witness of the resurrection.** The office-act — to be ordained to be a witness with us of His resurrection — takes the raising as the fact that must be attested. Chapter 46 already used this verse as ranking: the resurrection is the fact an apostle must be able to attest. This chapter uses it as eyewitness-definition: the witness of the resurrection is drawn from those who companied through the ministry. Position across the career is the condition of attesting the raising as the raising of this Jesus, not of an unknown.

The verse therefore joins this chapter's two fields: seers of the ministry and seers of the raising. The same men, in the Jerusalem company's account, occupy both. That joining is historically the joining of Chapters 45 and 46 in a person: the One whose works were seen is the One whose raising is attested. The office-question remains later. The eyewitness-question is answered in the qualifications: companionship and seeing of the raising.

Matthias is numbered with the eleven (Acts 1:26). The act of numbering is office. The criteria of selection are eyewitness criteria. This chapter takes the criteria. It leaves the authority of the numbered college to Chapters 49–50.

Thus:

> **Acts 1:21–22 defines the positioned class from which resurrection-witness is drawn: companions of the ministry from John's baptism to the taking up. Witness of the raising is ranked as the attestation required. Companionship is the condition. Office remains a later file.**

# 47.24 Acts Preaching as "We Are Witnesses"

The sermons in Acts present the speakers as witnesses of specified events, especially of the raising.

**Acts 2:32:** "This Jesus hath God raised up, whereof we all are witnesses." The sermon has already named the public career, the crucifixion, and the raising. The witness-clause attaches the speakers to the raising as seers, not as later theorists of a death.

**Acts 3:15:** "And killed the Prince of life, whom God hath raised from the dead; whereof we are witnesses."

**Acts 4:20:** "For we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard." The infinitive is compulsion of speech, not of invention. The object is things seen and heard. The sentence is this chapter's definition in the mouths of the speakers.

**Acts 5:32:** "And we are his witnesses of these things; and so is also the Holy Ghost, whom God hath given to them that obey him." Human witness and divine witness are named together. The divine witness is not treated here as a substitute for the human seeing, nor as the Graphē-gate. It is recorded as the sermon's pairing. The human clause remains: we are His witnesses of these things.

**Acts 10:39–41**, already cited for the limitation "not to all the people," also states the positive: "we are witnesses of all things which he did both in the land of the Jews, and in Jerusalem; whom they slew and hanged on a tree: Him God raised up the third day, and shewed him openly... even to us, who did eat and drink with him after he rose from the dead." Witness of the ministry's deeds, of the death, and of table-fellowship after the raising: the whole career as positioned knowledge.

**Acts 13:31:** "And he was seen many days of them which came up with him from Galilee to Jerusalem, who are his witnesses unto the people." The seers are the companions from Galilee. The seeing lasted many days. The witnesses are those companions.

Across these sermons the first-person plural is the we of positioned knowledge, not the we of later Christendom. The speakers claim to have seen and heard. Chapter 46 established that the content of the preaching is death-and-resurrection. This chapter establishes that the form of the preaching is eyewitness testimony of that content.

Thus:

> **Acts presents the Jerusalem proclamation as speech of seers: we are witnesses; we cannot but speak what we have seen and heard; He was seen many days of those who came up with Him from Galilee. The form is eyewitness. The content was Chapter 46's object.**

# 47.25 Related Commission-Texts: John 15:27, Luke 24:48, Acts 1:8

Three further sentences attach witnessing to companionship and to sending, without closing the office-file.

**John 15:27:** "And ye also shall bear witness, because ye have been with me from the beginning." The because is the because of position. Bearing witness is grounded in having been with Him from the beginning. The future of testimony rests on the past of companionship. That is this chapter's definition applied to the farewell setting.

**Luke 24:48:** "And ye are witnesses of these things." The things, in context, are the suffering, the rising the third day, and the preaching of repentance and remission in His name among all nations, beginning at Jerusalem (Luke 24:46–47). The raised Lord names them witnesses. The naming is not yet the finished doctrine of apostolic authority. It is the raised Lord's own ranking of their relation to the events.

**Acts 1:8:** "But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth." Witness unto Me, in expanding geography. The power is promised. The identity of the speakers as witnesses is stated. Geography is the reach of the testimony, not a change in its kind. The kind remains witness of Him.

1 Peter 5:1 belongs with these as related Petrine speech: "who am also an elder, and a witness of the sufferings of Christ, and also a partaker of the glory that shall be revealed." Witness of the sufferings is positioned knowledge of the passion. Partaker of the glory that shall be revealed looks forward. The verse does not here name the Transfiguration. It names sufferings as an object of witness. Peter is presented, in this other letter, as a seer of the passion as well as, in 2 Peter 1, a seer of majesty.

These texts do not add a new species. They confirm the species already defined: those who were with Him, who saw His sufferings and His glory and His raising, are named as witnesses, and are sent as such. Sending will matter for Chapters 49–50. Being such is this chapter's object.

Thus:

> **John 15:27, Luke 24:48, Acts 1:8, and 1 Peter 5:1 name the companions as witnesses because they were with Him, of these things, of His sufferings, unto Him. Commission is noted. Office is not closed. The eyewitness relation is the because of the witnessing.**

# 47.26 The Resurrection Appearances as Claimed Eyewitness Events

Chapter 46 established that the resurrection claim is bodily, public, and a claim of appearances; that 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 is the earliest surviving apostolic form; that the Gospel narratives are later written testimony of the same sequential claim; and that "he was seen" is the claim's appointed attestation. Those conclusions are premises. They are not re-tried.

This chapter's operation is different:

> **To examine the appearances as claimed eyewitness events — the witnessing of the risen Christ — under the definition now in force: positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard.**

The appearances are eyewitness events in the sense this chapter defines if, and in the way, the testimony presents positioned persons encountering the raised Jesus, knowing Him as the same One, and giving that knowledge as testimony. They are not eyewitness events if they are only later moods of consolation, only private interior visions as the whole claim, or only liturgical captions. Chapter 46 already refused those reductions as global accounts of the hinge. This chapter asks the remaining question: what kind of seeing is claimed?

The seeing claimed is seeing of the raised One by named persons and groups, in named settings, over a period of days, including speech, and in specified narratives including touch and table-fellowship. Acts 10:41: they did eat and drink with Him after He rose from the dead. Luke 24:39: "a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have." John 20:27: the wounds offered to Thomas. These are claims of encounter in the world. They are the species: positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard, now of the raising.

The limitation already noted remains: not to all the people, but unto witnesses chosen before of God. The limitation is mode, not fraud. Chosen seers of a public raising announced in the city of the death are still seers. They are not the census of Jerusalem. They are the eyewitness file of Easter.

Thus:

> **The resurrection appearances, already belonging to the hinge as its appointed attestation, are here examined as claimed eyewitness events of the risen Christ. The object is the witnessing. The raising as historical claim is already in force.**

---

# 47.27 1 Corinthians 15:5–8 as Appearance-Testimony

1 Corinthians 15:3–8 was examined in Chapter 46 as the earliest form of the four-clause gospel. This chapter takes clauses one through three as premises and examines clause four as eyewitness testimony.

"He was seen" — ὤφθη — of:

1. **Cephas** — an individual, already a known pillar (Galatians 1:18; 2:9), a companion of the ministry, a seer of the Transfiguration, a denier in the passion-night, a preacher at Pentecost. The mix of relations is the man. The appearance-claim is that this man saw the raised Lord.

2. **The twelve** — a defined group, a ministry-institution already in existence before Easter. Group-seeing is not a solitary consolation. It is positioned knowledge claimed for a body of companions.

3. **Above five hundred brethren at once** — a large group, with the note "of whom the greater part remain unto this present, but some are fallen asleep." The note is an invitation to inquiry in the 50s. Living seers, still available, are part of the testimony's form. Checkability is not a modern demand laid upon the text. It is the text's own implication.

4. **James** — an individual, "the Lord's brother" in Galatians 1:19, a man of whom John 7:5 records that His brethren did not believe, and who is a leader in Jerusalem by Galatians 2. The seer is not drawn only from the already-devoted.

5. **All the apostles** — a group not identical in wording with "the twelve." A wider commissioned circle is named as seers.

6. **Paul** — last, as of one born out of due time, a former persecutor. "And last of all he was seen of me also" (1 Corinthians 15:8). "Am I not an apostle? am I not free? have I seen Jesus Christ our Lord?" (1 Corinthians 9:1). Paul's seeing is later and of a different proximity-class. It is still a claim of seeing. The letter does not hide the difference. It names it: ἐκτρώματι, one untimely born.

The mix is the evidential form of the list: individuals and groups; intimates and a brother who had not believed; a large company and a hostile persecutor; early seers and a last seer. Chapter 46 already used the mix against hallucination as a global explanation of the hinge. This chapter uses the mix as the structure of an eyewitness file: different positions, different prior relations to Jesus, one claim of having seen Him raised.

1 Corinthians 15:15 makes the speakers' truthfulness hang upon the event: if Christ be not raised, "we are found false witnesses of God." The sentence is the seers' own ranking of their speech. Eyewitness testimony here is not a hobby of memory. It is the speech upon which they say they stand or fall as witnesses of God.

Thus:

> **1 Corinthians 15:5–8 is appearance-testimony: ordered, named, mixed in kind, checkable in the 50s, and ranked by the speaker as the condition of the witnesses' truthfulness. It is the earliest surviving form of the Easter eyewitness file.**

# 47.28 Gospel Appearance Narratives as Seeing

The Gospel narratives are later written testimony of the same seeing. Distinction of date and detail was Chapter 46's conclusion. This chapter asks what kind of seeing they narrate.

**Mary Magdalene** (John 20:11–18; cf. Matthew 28:9–10) sees and hears. She mistakes Him for the gardener until He names her. She is sent to the brethren: "I have seen the Lord." The sentence is this chapter's definition in a woman's mouth: positioned encounter, knowledge, testimony.

**The other women** (Matthew 28:9–10) hold Him by the feet and worship. Touch is named. Fear and great joy are named. They are sent.

**Cleopas and the other on the Emmaus road** (Luke 24:13–35) walk, hear exposition of Moses and the prophets, see Him known in the breaking of bread, and return to Jerusalem the same hour with the report. Seeing is delayed in recognition and then given. The delay is the narrative's. It is not unbelief in the narrator. It is the form of an encounter that becomes knowledge.

**The gathered disciples** (Luke 24:36–49; John 20:19–23) see Him, are terrified as if seeing a spirit, are shown hands and feet, are invited to handle, watch Him eat fish, receive opening of the Scriptures and a commission. John adds the locked room and the showing of hands and side, and the sending as the Father sent Him.

**Thomas** (John 20:24–29) is absent, doubts, is invited eight days later to reach his finger and his hand, and confesses. The narrative includes a reluctant seer. Unfavorable and reluctant testimony is a credibility-mark Chapter 35 already named. The file is not a panel of the already-convinced.

**Galilee** (Matthew 28:16–20; John 21) extends the seeing to the land of the ministry: a mountain, a lakeside, a catch of fish, a breakfast, a restoration of Peter. Mark 16:7 already promised Galilee. Multiple settings over many days (Acts 1:3) are what a period of being seen would produce.

The narratives insist on recognition of the same Jesus: wounds, voice, table, the name spoken. They insist on the resistance of the seers: fear, doubt, unbelief of the women's report (Luke 24:11), slowness of heart. They insist on sending. They are not a different religion of inner light. They are later written testimony of positioned seeing of the raised One.

Luke 24:25–27, 44–47 records His interpretation of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms. That act is noted as belonging to an appearance-narrative. It is not consummated as Chapter 48. John 20:21 and Matthew 28:18–20 record commission. That act is noted. It is not consummated as Chapters 49–50.

Thus:

> **The Gospel appearance-narratives are testimony of seeing: named persons, named places, resistance in the seers, bodily marks, speech, table, sending. They fill the formula's "he was seen" without becoming a rival gospel.**

# 47.29 Women at the Tomb: Place, First Report, Positioned Knowledge

The women require their own section because their force is proportional and specific, and because two illicit treatments must be refused together: dismissing them because they are women in a male-legal world, and inflating them into the whole Easter file.

**Markers of the place.** Chapter 46 already established that the women mark the burial: they see where He is laid (Mark 15:47; Matthew 27:61; Luke 23:55). That marking is positioned knowledge of a location in the ordinary world. Without it, the first-day visit would be a search for an unknown grave. With it, the visit is a visit to a known place. Burial-knowledge is eyewitness of a disposal, not of a raising. It belongs to the file as knowledge of the place the raising-claim concerns.

**First reporters of emptiness.** All four Gospels place women first at the tomb on the first day of the week. They find the stone not where they expected. They find emptiness. They receive an announcement that He is risen and is not here. They are sent to the disciples. Luke 24:11 records the reception: "their words seemed to them as idle tales, and they believed them not." The first report is unfavorable in its first hearers. Chapter 35 already treated unfavorable testimony as evidentially relevant. A late improver constructing a case for a male-legal world would more naturally have Cephas find the tomb. The Gospels have Mary Magdalene. Paul has Cephas as first in the official appearance-list. The two facts remain compatible: women discover emptiness and first report; Cephas is named as first among the official seers of appearance.

**Positioned knowledge, not mere presence.** These women are not a crowd that happened to walk by a garden. Luke 8:1–3 names Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Susanna, and many others as companions and supporters of the ministry. Mark 15:40–41 names Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James the less and of Joses, and Salome as among those who followed Him in Galilee and ministered unto Him, and many other women who came up with Him to Jerusalem. They are participant-witnesses of the career, then markers of the burial, then first discoverers of emptiness, and in specified narratives seers of the raised Lord. The chain is position across time, not a single accidental glance.

**Proportional force.** The women do not replace Cephas, the twelve, the five hundred, James, or Paul. They do not need to. Their contribution is place, first report, and, in Matthew and John, appearance. Force is proposition-specific. For the proposition "the tomb was known and found empty on the first day," they are primary. For the proposition "He was seen of Cephas, then of the twelve," they are not the official list. Classification is the discipline.

Thus:

> **The women at the tomb are positioned witnesses of the place and first reporters of emptiness, companions of the ministry rather than passers-by, and in specified narratives seers of the raised Lord. Their force is primary for place and first report, proportional for the whole Easter file, and not to be dismissed because the official appearance-list names Cephas first.**

# 47.30 Named Lists and Checkability

Named lists belong to the eyewitness file because names make inquiry possible.

The Twelve are named (Matthew 10:2–4; Mark 3:16–19; Luke 6:13–16; Acts 1:13). A named college is not an anonymous cloud. Cephas, James, John, Andrew, Philip, Thomas, Bartholomew, Matthew, James of Alphaeus, Simon Zelotes, Judas of James, and the rest are persons of whom the community could say, this one was with Him.

1 Corinthians 15:5–8 names Cephas, the twelve, five hundred with a note of present survivals, James, all the apostles, Paul. "The greater part remain" is an invitation issued in the 50s. Checkability is internal to the formula.

Luke 8:2–3 names women of the ministry. Mark 15:40–41 names women at the cross. Luke 24:18 names Cleopas. John names Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathea in the burial. Acts 1:13 names the eleven in an upper room, with the women, Mary the mother of Jesus, and His brethren.

Chapter 35 already established that named witnesses matter because testimony can exist within a network of people who know the witness and can assess the testimony. This chapter uses that conclusion of this Person's seers. An unnamed "someone saw something" is a weaker form. The authentic testimony does not use only that weaker form. It names.

Naming is not yet verification by a modern archive. It is the historical form of a movement that located its claims in persons who could be asked. Galatians 1:18–19 records that Paul went to Jerusalem to see Peter and saw James. The names are not literary types. They are the men a later apostle says he visited.

Thus:

> **Named lists make the eyewitness file checkable in the first-century community. The Twelve, the 1 Corinthians 15 catalogue, the named women, Cleopas, and the Jerusalem company are persons, not allegories. Checkability is the testimony's own form.**

---

# 47.31 Disciples of the Public Ministry

The public ministry had positioned witnesses before the mountain and before the tomb.

The disciples companied with Him. They heard the claims Chapter 45 examined. They saw the works. They were present at table, at confrontations, at the sea, at Jairus's house, at the sending of the seventy, at the entry into Jerusalem. John 15:27 grounds their future witnessing in that past: because ye have been with me from the beginning. Acts 1:21–22 makes the same companionship a condition of resurrection-witness.

This is participant-witness extended through a career. It is not a single glance at a single marvel. It is knowledge accumulated under conditions of travel, conflict, misunderstanding, and fear. The Gospels retain the misunderstanding. Mark's disciples repeatedly fail to understand. That retention is the opposite of a fable's preference for flawless seers. It is the presentation of positioned knowers who did not yet know all they saw.

The seventy (Luke 10:1, 17) and the wider company (Acts 1:15 names about a hundred and twenty in the days after the taking up) extend the positioned class beyond the Twelve. Proportional force again: the Twelve are the named college; the wider company are companions and seers of a public career. The public character of the ministry Chapter 43 and Chapter 45 established is the condition of this wider seeing. A secret teacher has no such file. A public career in Galilee and Judea has.

John 2:23 records that many believed when they saw the miracles He did at the feast. John 6 distinguishes seeing signs from eating bread. Presence of many is not the eyewitness file. The file is those whose knowledge of specified events is positioned and then given as testimony. The many are historically real and evidentially mixed. The companions who from the beginning were with Him, who delivered to Luke, who stand up in Acts and say "we are witnesses," are the file.

Thus:

> **The disciples of the public ministry are participant-witnesses of a public career: claims heard, works seen, companionship from the beginning. Their seeing is the ministry's own authentication-axis as encountered, not as later reconstructed. Misunderstanding retained in the narratives is a mark of positioned knowers, not of flawless literary types.**

---

# 47.32 Peter as Continuous Participant-Witness

Peter occupies more than one relation in the file, and the relations must be named rather than flattened.

He is a companion from the early calling (Mark 1:16–18). He is named first among the Twelve. He is on the holy mount (Matthew 17:1; 2 Peter 1:16–18). He confesses at Caesarea Philippi (Matthew 16:16) and is rebuked in the next movement of the same scene. He denies in the passion-night (Mark 14:66–72). He runs to the tomb (Luke 24:12; John 20:3–6). He is first in the official appearance-list (1 Corinthians 15:5; cf. Luke 24:34). He preaches at Pentecost as a witness of the raising (Acts 2:32). He writes of being an eyewitness of majesty and a witness of the sufferings.

The continuity is the man. The file does not require him to have been a perfect observer. It requires him to have been positioned, and the testimony presents him as positioned across the career, the mountain, the denial, the tomb, the appearance, and the public preaching. Denial is unfavorable to a theory of collusive invention. A collusive inventor does not ordinarily give his chief seer a threefold denial and tears. Chapter 35 already treated the witnesses' imperfection as not destroying their testimony. This chapter uses Peter's imperfection as a mark of the file's form: continuous participation, including failure, then seeing of the raised Lord, then public witness.

2 Peter 1:16–18 is this man's first-person grounding of the category. 1 Peter 5:1 is this man's first-person claim to be a witness of the sufferings. Luke 24:34 is the gathered disciples' report: "the Lord is risen indeed, and hath appeared to Simon." The relations converge on one name. Convergence on a named, imperfect, public man is the opposite of an anonymous legend.

Thus:

> **Peter is a continuous participant-witness: companion, mountain-seer, denier, tomb-runner, first official seer of appearance, Pentecost preacher, writer of 2 Peter 1:16–18. Continuity and imperfection belong to the file. They do not cancel it.**

# 47.33 John as Sensory Witness

The Johannine testimony, already cited, may now be gathered as a class of positioned knowledge.

1 John 1:1–3 stacks hearing, seeing, beholding, handling of the manifested Word of life. John 1:14 names dwelling among us and beholding glory. John 19:35 names the seer of the piercing. John 21:24 names the disciple who testifies and writes, with communal affirmation. John 20:8 records that the other disciple went in, saw the linen, and believed. John 21 records a lakeside recognition: "It is the Lord."

The beloved disciple is presented as a companion of the ministry, present at the cross (John 19:26–27), a runner to the tomb, a seer of the raised Lord, and a writer of testimony. Whether every Johannine "we" is that one man, or a circle that includes him, the species remains: sensory encounter with the manifested life, given as written witness.

John 20:30–31 states the purpose of the written signs: that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing ye might have life through His name. Chapter 45 already used that purpose of the signs as authentication-architecture. This chapter uses the same verses for the written character of eyewitness: the seer writes so that those who did not see may believe. John 20:29 blesses those who have not seen and have believed. The blessing does not abolish the seeing. It distinguishes the later believer from the seer, which is this chapter's distinction between eyewitness and later reception.

Thus:

> **Johannine testimony is sensory witness of the manifested Word, written so that later readers may believe. The seer is distinguished from those who have not seen. The distinction is the Gospel's own.**

---

# 47.34 Paul as a Different Kind of Witness

Paul must be classified, not assimilated.

He was not a companion of the earthly ministry in the way of the Twelve. He persecuted the assembly (Galatians 1:13; 1 Corinthians 15:9; Acts 8:3; 9:1). He claims a later encounter with the risen Christ (Acts 9; 22; 26; 1 Corinthians 9:1; 15:8; Galatians 1:12, 16). He also received and delivered the formula of 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, which names seers other than himself. Received testimony and personal seeing coexist in one apostle, as Chapter 18 already established.

Acts 26:16 records the risen Lord's stated purpose in Paul's own defense: "I have appeared unto thee for this purpose, to make thee a minister and a witness both of these things which thou hast seen, and of those things in the which I will appear unto thee." Witness of what he has seen: the appearance is the object. Minister and witness: commission is joined to seeing. The joining is noted. The office-file remains later.

Paul's proximity-class is therefore:

- not Transfiguration-seer;
- not companion from John's baptism;
- seer of a later appearance;
- recipient and transmitter of the earlier appearance-tradition;
- visitor of Cephas and James (Galatians 1:18–19);
- former enemy.

The class is still eyewitness of an appearance. It is not the same class as Peter's continuous participation. Chapter 35's principle remains: not every witness possesses the same kind of proximity; the investigator must specify what each witness claims to know. Paul claims to have seen the Lord. He does not claim to have been on the holy mount. The authentic testimony is capable of that specification. So is this chapter.

Thus:

> **Paul is a later encounter-witness of the risen Christ, a former persecutor, a recipient of earlier appearance-tradition, and a visitor of prior seers. His seeing is real as claimed and different in proximity-class. Difference of class is not deletion of seeing.**

# 47.35 Multiple Witnesses, Independence, and Cost

Deuteronomy 19:15 states a legal architecture the writings themselves know: at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established. The Transfiguration has three named seers. The appearances have many, mixed in kind. The tomb-morning has more than one woman. The ministry has a college and a wider company. Multiplicity is the record's form.

Independence, as Chapter 35 defined it, is not the absence of any contact among seers. Companions of the same ministry will have been together. Independence is the presence of distinct positions, distinct prior relations, and distinct interests such that a single inventor does not obviously generate the whole. Peter the denier, Thomas the doubter, James the previously unbelieving brother, Paul the persecutor, Mary Magdalene first at the tomb, five hundred at once — these are not one temperament. A collusive circle can still exist. The mix raises the cost of that explanation.

Cost belongs to sincerity without being identical with accuracy, as Chapter 35 established. The seers of this Person proclaim a crucified Messiah in the city of the crucifixion, and the later record includes imprisonment, beating, and death for the testimony. Acts 4–5, 7, 12; 2 Corinthians 11:23–28. Cost does not by itself prove truth. Men have died for errors. Cost does prove that the testimony was not, in the seers' own ranking, a pastime. Combined with position, naming, mix of seers, and the hinge already in force, cost is a conjunct, not a solitary proof.

Publicity of the ministry and of the death supplies a further condition. The career was not a secret. The crucifixion was not a secret. False testimony about a public career and a public death, offered in the same city within weeks (Acts 2), faces neighbors who knew the career and the death. The appearances were not shown to all the people. The preaching was. The combination is the record's: restricted seers of the raising, public proclamation at the scene of the death.

Thus:

> **Multiple seers, mixed in kind, named, at cost, proclaiming a public death in the city of that death, constitute the form of the file. Multiplicity is not mechanical proof. It is the architecture Deuteronomy 19:15 names and the Gospels and Paul exhibit.**

# 47.36 How This Relates to Movement V and Chapters 43–46 Without Circularity

The circularity to refuse is this: the Gospels are inspired Scripture in the finished Movement VIII sense; therefore whatever they report of seers is true; therefore the seers authenticate Him; therefore the Gospels are inspired.

That chain is refused. Inspiration's guarantees, canon, and Graphē as primary written authority remain later.

The actual operation is this:

Movement V warranted the writings as authentic written testimony of the claims under investigation: genuine, recoverable, historically embedded, internally coherent testimony of claimed divine communication and historical acts. That warrant did not yet isolate the seers of this Person as Movement VI's dedicated file.

Chapters 43–46 located the Person, identified Him, authenticated the ministry's claims-and-works conjunction, and established death and resurrection as the central historical claims, with appearances as the appointed attestation.

This chapter reads the seers from writings already warranted as testimony, about a Person already located, identified, authenticated, and examined at the hinge. The reading does not assume Graphē-gate authority. It does not re-try the hinge. It is the next earned operation of Movement VI: the witnessing of the One so established.

> **The eyewitness line is read from writings already warranted as testimony, about a Person already located, identified, authenticated, and examined at the hinge. The reading does not assume Movement VIII, and it does not pretend to be the resurrection case a second time. It is the next earned operation of Movement VI.**

# 47.37 Competing Claim: Mere Presence Suffices

The reduction says:

> **Anyone who was nearby is an eyewitness. Crowds at feedings, at the entry, at the crucifixion, are the file. The named seers are only the loudest of a multitude.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **the definition already distinguishes occupancy from knowledge**. A crowd may occupy a space. Eyewitness is positioned knowledge of a specified event, given as testimony.

Second, **the record itself distinguishes the many from the named seers**. Three on the mountain, not the Twelve. Chosen witnesses of the raising, not all the people. Women who marked the tomb, not every passer-by in Jerusalem. Acts 10:41 is explicit.

Third, **the reduction cannot explain 2 Peter 1:16–18**. Cunningly devised fables are not refused by "we were in Galilee at the time." They are refused by "we were eyewitnesses of his majesty" and "we heard this voice" and "we were with him in the holy mount." Specification is the letter's answer to inflation of presence.

Fourth, **the reduction cannot explain the official appearance-list**. If presence sufficed, the five hundred and Cephas and Paul would be a pointless specification. The formula specifies.

Presence remains a condition of ordinary seeing. It is not the authentication line. The reduction explains occupancy and cancels knowledge. It fails.

---

# 47.38 Competing Claim: Later Commentary Is Eyewitness

The reduction says:

> **The Gospels, the sermons, and 2 Peter are later religious commentary upon Jesus. Calling them eyewitness testimony is a category error. They are theology in narrative dress.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **the writings themselves distinguish seer from later writer**. Luke 1:1–4: eyewitnesses delivered; Luke investigated. John 20:29: those who have not seen. 2 Peter 1:16 is first-person and event-located. A commentary-reduction must then claim that these distinctions are themselves costume. Costume-theories that explain every first-person seeing-claim as costume explain too much and test too little.

Second, **event-location is not the form of later commentary**. The holy mount, the first day of the week, the locked room, the lakeside, the Damascus road, "the greater part remain" — these are the form of positioned claims. Later commentary can imitate that form. The earliest formula in the 50s, with living seers noted, is early for the imitation-theory's convenience.

Third, **Movement V already warranted the writings as authentic testimony, not as late folklore**. Local questions of how a particular scene was remembered remain legitimate. They are not a global solvent that converts every seeing-claim into a homily.

Fourth, **this chapter's own distinction is the reduction's proper remainder**. Later commentary exists and is not eyewitness. The reduction takes that true distinction and applies it to the seers' own sentences. Application to the wrong object is the error.

The Gospels contain interpretation. 2 Peter contains exhortation. Acts contains sermons. Interpretation, exhortation, and sermons are not thereby the deletion of seeing. The reduction takes a real later operation and reads it backward as the origin of the file. It fails as an account of 2 Peter 1:16–18, 1 John 1:1–3, 1 Corinthians 15:5–8, and Luke 1:1–4 together.

---

# 47.39 Competing Claim: Legend Displaces Positioned Seeing

The reduction says:

> **A historical teacher existed. Stories of a transfiguration, of appearances, of named seers, accrued as legend. The eyewitness file is a literary product of decades.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **Chapter 43 already destroyed the vacuum and the coreless legend**. A located man, a short public career, crucifixion under Pilate, a brother named James, a movement in the 50s — these remain. Legend upon a core must then explain the content, the date, and the form of the seeing-claims.

Second, **the date of the appearance-list is not late enough for the reduction's convenience**. 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 is received tradition delivered at Corinth's founding, written in the 50s, originating in the first years after the crucifixion, as Chapter 46 established. "The greater part remain" is not the idiom of a legend that has outlived its seers.

Third, **the form is not the ordinary form of legend**. Legend typically smooths, removes friction, and accumulates wonders without the threefold denial, without Thomas, without the women's report dismissed as idle tales, without the charge to silence after the Transfiguration, without Paul's "untimely born." The file retains friction. Inflation that produces Mark's Peter would ordinarily omit the denial or the ignorance on the mount.

Fourth, **multiple streams carry the seeing**. Petrine first-person of the mountain; Synoptic Transfiguration narratives; Johannine sensory stack; Pauline appearance-list; Lukan investigation of αὐτόπται; Acts sermons of "we are witnesses." Independent idiom with the same species is the same evidential fact Chapter 44 used of identity. Legendary displacement must then be multiple, early, and coincident.

Fifth, **hostility and shame as sources of preservation work against free inflation**. Crucifixion remains a scandal (1 Corinthians 1:23). Appearances of a crucified man, proclaimed in Jerusalem, are not the easiest legendary gifts. They are the career's crisis continued into testimony.

The community remembered, preached, and wrote. Memory is not the same act as the creation of mountain-seers and Easter-seers out of a mild teacher within a generation. The reduction fails as an explanation of the first-century conjunction.

# 47.40 Competing Claim: Hallucination as Global Account of the Seers

The reduction says:

> **The seers experienced grief-induced or ecstatic visions. The Transfiguration and the appearances are interior events. Positioned knowledge of an event in the world is a misdescription of psychology.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **Chapter 46 already refused hallucination as a global explanation of the hinge**, on the mix of seers, the bodily marks, the table-fellowship, the empty-tomb conjunction, and the public preaching. This chapter does not re-try that case. It adds what the eyewitness-file adds.

Second, **the Transfiguration is a different event, with three named seers, a mountain, sleep noted, a voice, and a charge of silence until the raising**. A grief-vision of Easter does not generate a pre-death mountain-event remembered as glory on the way to Jerusalem. The reduction must then become two psychologies, or a solvent that turns every sensory claim into vision.

Third, **group-seeing and mixed prior relations strain a single mechanism**. Consolation-vision, if it explains Peter, does not obviously explain James the previously unbelieving brother, or Paul the persecutor, or five hundred at once, or Thomas after eight days, or table-fellowship of leftover fish. Stretching is not explanation.

Fourth, **the seers themselves distinguish spirit from encounter**. Luke 24:37–39: they supposed they had seen a spirit; He refuses the supposition with flesh and bones and eating. The narrative is aware of the interior-vision option and locates the claim over against it. A hallucination-reduction must then claim that the distinction is itself hallucinated. Again the theory explains too much.

Fifth, **2 Peter 1:16's rival is fable, not "we had a powerful inner life."** The letter answers by naming mountain, voice, and company. Psychology may still be discussed as a local question about a particular seer. It is not a global account of this file.

Grief is real. Ecstasy is a real human capacity. Neither, as a global solvent of the Transfiguration, the appearance-list, the tomb-morning, and the Acts sermons together, matches the specified clauses. The reduction fails.

# 47.41 Competing Claim: Collusion or Fabrication

The reduction says:

> **The seers agreed to say they had seen. The Transfiguration, the appearances, and the women's report are coordinated falsehood, pious or political.**

The reduction fails for specified reasons.

First, **the chief seer is given a threefold denial**. Coordinated falsehood ordinarily protects its speakers. The Gospels expose Peter's failure in all four canonical accounts of the passion-night. Fabrication that produces the denial has chosen an unlikely gift.

Second, **the first tomb-reporters are women whose words are dismissed as idle tales**. A fabrication for a male-legal world has again chosen an unlikely gift. The official list can name Cephas first. The narratives still have Mary Magdalene first at the place.

Third, **Paul's inclusion as untimely born and former persecutor** is an inclusion of an enemy who, on his own account, received the formula from others and then saw. A collusive Galilean circle does not obviously generate Paul. Paul's independence is not absolute — he visited Cephas and James — but his origin as persecutor is the opposite of a founding collusion.

Fourth, **cost follows the speech**. If the collusion is political, the politics are poor: a crucified Messiah, proclaimed in Jerusalem, yielding imprisonment rather than advantage. If the collusion is pious, piety has chosen to hang its truthfulness on an event it knows to be false (1 Corinthians 15:15). Possible in the abstract. Costly as a global account of this mix, this date, and this hinge.

Fifth, **friction among the seers remains in the record**. Thomas doubts. Some worshipped and some doubted on the Galilean mountain (Matthew 28:17). The women are not believed. Paul withstands Peter at Antioch (Galatians 2:11–14). Coordinated fabrication typically smooths the panel. This panel is not smooth.

Fabrication of a particular sentence remains a local possibility for a local critic. Fabrication as a global account of 2 Peter 1:16–18, 1 Corinthians 15:5–8, the tomb-women, the Synoptic mountain, and the Acts sermons fails as an explanation of the conjunction. The reduction fails.

# 47.42 Reason Through the Evidence

The reasoning may now be stated so that it can be inspected.

**Step 2.** Eyewitness testimony, in this argument, is positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard, communicated as testimony. It is not mere presence. It is not later commentary. It is not all testimony. It is not yet apostolic office. It is not yet confirmation of Scripture.

**Step 4.** Related apostolic testimony exhibits the same species: 1 John 1:1–3 (hearing, seeing, beholding, handling); John 19:35 and 21:24 (seer, record, communal affirmation); Luke 1:1–4 (αὐτόπται deliver; an investigator writes); Acts 1:21–22 (companionship and witness of the resurrection); Acts sermons ("we are witnesses"; "things seen and heard"); John 15:27, Luke 24:48, Acts 1:8 (witness because they were with Him).

**Step 5.** The resurrection appearances, already belonging to the hinge, are claimed eyewitness events of the risen Christ: 1 Corinthians 15:5–8 as ordered, named, mixed, checkable appearance-testimony; Gospel narratives as later written testimony of seeing, with resistance in the seers and bodily marks; women as markers of place and first reporters of emptiness, with proportional force; named lists as the form of checkability.

**Step 7.** The live reductions — mere presence, later commentary as eyewitness, legend as global displacement, hallucination as global account of the seers, collusion as global fabrication — fail as accounts of this conjunction. They explain a strand and cancel the structure.

**Step 8.** Therefore the investigator is warranted, with full force, in concluding that the authentic testimony presents positioned witnesses of what was seen and heard concerning Jesus Christ — of His majesty in the ministry and of His raising — and that this presentation is a central authentication line of Movement VI, the next established operation after the hinge.

The form of reasoning is the same form the book has used: abductive historical and textual warrant. The writings say this. They are authentic testimony. The Person exists, has been identified, authenticated, and examined at the hinge. The seers are specified, mixed, named, and ranked by the record as witnesses of what they saw and heard. The reductions fail. The authentication line follows. It is not Euclidean demonstration. It is not impression. It is the exhibited bearing of the record upon the proposition.

# 47.47 From Eyewitness Testimony to Christ's Confirmation of Scripture

The seers have been examined as seers. The mountain has been heard. The appearances have been taken as witnessing of the risen Christ. The women have been given their place. The named lists stand.

That is not yet Chapter 48.

> **Is eyewitness testimony positioned knowledge of what was seen and heard concerning this Person, and does that testimony function as a central authentication line?**

The question is:

> **How did this Person treat the Scriptures He received, and what does His confirmation of those writings establish now that He has been located, identified, authenticated, examined at the hinge, and attested by seers?**

That question is not a retreat from the seers. It is the seers' Lord examined in His relation to the writings. The investigation therefore moves from:

> **We were eyewitnesses of His majesty; He was seen; we are witnesses of what we have seen and heard.**

to:

> **What did He confirm as written, and with what force?**

That is the proper open of the next chapter of Movement VI. The eyewitness line was never meant to be the last word.
