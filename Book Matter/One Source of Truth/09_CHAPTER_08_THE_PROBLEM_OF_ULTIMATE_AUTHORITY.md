# ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH
## The Doctrine of the Graphē

# MOVEMENT I — TRUTH AND THE POSSIBILITY OF KNOWLEDGE

# Chapter 8 — The Problem of Ultimate Authority

> **Central question:** When competing claims about truth exist, what can finally govern judgment—and why should that authority itself be accepted as authoritative?

The investigation has now reached a necessary threshold.

We have examined truth, reality, error, knowledge, evidence, rational warrant, inference, disciplined thinking, sound judgment, and the biblical summons:

> **“Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD.”**  
> — Isaiah 1:18

That summons established something important: human beings are responsible to engage what is true intelligently. But reasonability alone does not answer every question.

A person can reason correctly **from false premises**.

A person can possess evidence but interpret it badly.

Two people can reason carefully from different ultimate assumptions and arrive at incompatible conclusions.

Institutions can make competing claims.

Teachers can disagree.

Traditions can conflict.

Books can contradict one another.

Experiences can be interpreted differently.

And even a person who possesses considerable intellectual ability can ask:

> **What finally governs my judgment when authorities conflict?**

That is the problem of ultimate authority.

It is not a problem that can be solved simply by naming an authority.

If someone says:

> “This institution is the final authority,”

the immediate question is:

> **Why is that institution authoritative?**

If the answer is:

> “Because the institution says it is,”

the argument is circular.

If someone says:

> “This book is the final authority,”

the question remains:

> **Why should this book be regarded as authoritative?**

If the answer is merely:

> “Because the book says so,”

the same problem appears.

The issue is not whether an authority may make claims about itself. It certainly may.

The issue is whether the **grounds for recognizing that authority** have been established.

This chapter therefore does not prematurely declare the conclusion that later chapters will establish.

It defines the problem that must be solved.

---

# 1. Authority and Truth Are Not the Same Question

The first distinction is essential.

> **Truth asks what is actually the case.**

> **Authority asks what has the right to govern judgment concerning what is the case.**

These questions are related but not identical.

A proposition can be true even when a person refuses to recognize it.

Likewise, a person can possess institutional authority without every statement made by that institution being true.

Therefore:

> **Authority does not manufacture truth.**

Truth is not true because an institution declares it.

Nor does a proposition become false merely because an institution rejects it.

The investigation must consequently avoid a common intellectual mistake:

> **confusing the authority to determine or govern a matter with the truth of the matter itself.**

The two must be related through adequate grounds.

---

# 2. What Is Authority?

In the present investigation, **authority** concerns the right or standing to govern judgment, teaching, interpretation, or action within a specified domain.

Authority therefore has a relational structure.

Someone or something is authoritative **with respect to something**.

A judge has authority within a judicial jurisdiction.

A laboratory instrument may have authority concerning a measurement only insofar as it is a reliable means of measurement.

A qualified witness may have evidential authority concerning something personally observed.

An institution may possess delegated authority concerning matters within its lawful competence.

But none of these examples establishes **ultimate authority**.

They illustrate subordinate or domain-specific authority.

Ultimate authority is different.

---

# 3. What Makes an Authority “Ultimate”?

An authority becomes ultimate in a particular investigation when there is no higher authority to which its governing judgment can be appealed.

That creates an unavoidable question.

If A is said to be the ultimate authority, ask:

> **What makes A worthy of that position?**

If B is offered as the ground of A's authority, then B is functioning as a higher authority.

If C is then offered as the ground of B, the same question arises again.

This produces a regress:

> **A → B → C → D → ...**

If every authority requires a higher authority to authenticate it, no ultimate authority is ever reached.

The problem therefore cannot be solved merely by continuing to nominate authorities.

At some point the chain must reach a **sufficient ground**.

That is why the question of ultimate authority is inseparable from the question of grounding.

---

# 4. The Authority Regress

Consider the following argument:

> “This teacher is authoritative because the school recognizes the teacher.”

Ask:

> Why is the school authoritative?

Suppose the answer is:

> “Because the governing body recognizes the school.”

Ask:

> Why is the governing body authoritative?

Suppose the answer is:

> “Because its constitution grants it authority.”

Ask:

> Why should the constitution itself govern?

The sequence can continue.

The same structure appears in religious, philosophical, political, academic, and institutional settings.

The existence of a hierarchy does not by itself establish an ultimate foundation.

A long chain of delegated authorities is still dependent upon whatever ultimately grounds the delegation.

Therefore:

> **Delegation can transmit authority; it cannot by itself create ultimate authority.**

---

# 5. Evidence Is Not the Same Thing as Authority

Another distinction must be maintained.

Evidence supports a conclusion.

Authority governs a judgment.

They can interact, but they are not identical.

For example, testimony may provide evidence that an event occurred.

The testimony does not become the event itself.

Likewise, a manuscript can provide evidence concerning what was written.

The manuscript's evidential value is a question distinct from the ultimate authority of the proposition recorded in it.

This distinction becomes crucial later when the book examines the Bible.

The question will not simply be:

> “Does the Bible contain authoritative statements?”

It will be:

> **What is the Bible, what does it claim to be, what evidence bears upon those claims, and what justifies the conclusion that its written testimony possesses divine authority?**

Those are different questions.

---

# 6. Evidence Cannot Be Used Carelessly

The fact that evidence exists does not automatically establish the conclusion someone attaches to it.

A disciplined investigation asks:

> **What does this evidence actually establish?**

and:

> **What does it not establish?**

For example, historical evidence that a person existed does not automatically establish every statement attributed to that person.

Evidence that a document is ancient does not automatically establish that everything in it is true.

Evidence that several witnesses agree does not automatically prove that they are correct.

Evidence must therefore be interpreted.

The relationship is:

> **Evidence → analysis → inference → conclusion**

not:

> **Evidence → automatic certainty**

This is why the evidential and authority questions must remain distinct.

---

# 7. An Authority Must Have Grounds

Suppose someone declares:

> **“This is the final authority.”**

The declaration itself is a claim.

It is not yet the complete justification for the claim.

The investigation must ask:

1. What is the proposed authority?
2. What does it claim concerning itself?
3. What is the nature of that claim?
4. What grounds support the claim?
5. What evidence bears upon those grounds?
6. Are the grounds reliable?
7. Is the inference from the evidence to the authority valid?
8. Does anything contradict the proposed authority?
9. If so, is the contradiction real or only apparent?
10. What conclusion is proportionate to the total evidence?

This is the application of the master reasoning discipline:

> **Claim → Definition → Evidence → Analysis → Inference → Conclusion → Consequence**

The authority claim must therefore enter the same intellectual process as every other major claim.

---

# 8. Does This Make Authority Dependent on Human Judgment?

This question is important.

If human beings examine evidence and conclude that something is authoritative, does that mean human beings have become the ultimate authority?

No.

There is a difference between:

> **recognizing authority**

and:

> **creating authority.**

A person may recognize a fact without creating the fact.

A person may recognize a valid law of logic without legislating it into existence.

A person may recognize a reliable witness without making the witness truthful.

Likewise, if a divine revelation possesses authority independently of human recognition, human investigation does not create that authority.

The investigation seeks to determine whether the claim to authority is warranted.

This distinction will become fundamental when divine revelation is examined.

---

# 9. Recognition and Authentication

This gives us two related but distinct concepts.

### Recognition

Recognition concerns the act of identifying something as what it is.

### Authentication

Authentication concerns the grounds by which a claim to genuineness, reliability, origin, or authority is established.

If a document claims to be an authentic communication from God, the question is not answered merely by saying:

> “I recognize it as God's Word.”

The investigation must ask why.

Likewise, if an institution says:

> “This is the authentic interpretation,”

the claim requires examination.

Authentication therefore precedes confident submission.

---

# 10. The Difference Between “Authority Says It” and “Authority Is Established”

These statements must not be confused:

> **“The authority says X.”**

and:

> **“X is established because the authority is established as authoritative.”**

The first reports a claim.

The second depends upon a prior argument.

This is one reason the book must not repeatedly treat the Bible as an undefined authority while simultaneously attempting to establish why it is authoritative.

The argument has to be built.

The reader must eventually be able to see:

> **what the Bible is → how its revelation was given → how the written record came into existence → what evidence authenticates it → why its authority follows.**

Only then can the final conclusion concerning the Graphē be properly drawn.

---

# 11. The Problem of Circular Reasoning

Circular reasoning occurs when a conclusion is used, directly or indirectly, as the premise required to establish itself.

For example:

> The Bible is authoritative because the Bible says it is authoritative.

That statement may contain a genuine biblical claim, but by itself it does not constitute the complete external structure of an authentication argument.

The question remains:

> **Why should the biblical record be accepted as the kind of record whose testimony concerning itself is trustworthy?**

That question cannot be avoided.

At the same time, avoiding circularity does **not** mean that every proposition concerning Scripture must be established by something outside Scripture.

That would introduce another problem.

If the final authority must always be validated by a higher external authority, the regress returns.

The solution requires careful distinctions between:

- **what an authority claims about itself;**
- **what evidence bears upon its authenticity;**
- **what premises establish the reliability of those evidences;**
- **and what conclusion follows from their convergence.**

The later movements of this book are designed to work through these distinctions.

---

# 12. The Ultimate Authority Cannot Be a Mere Preference

A person may say:

> “This is my authority.”

That expresses a personal commitment.

It does not, by itself, establish universal truth.

Likewise:

> “My tradition is authoritative.”

does not establish the truth of the tradition.

Nor does:

> “My church says so.”

Nor:

> “My professor says so.”

Nor:

> “Most scholars agree.”

Nor:

> “Everyone has always believed it.”

Popularity, antiquity, institutional status, and personal conviction are not identical to truth.

An ultimate authority must therefore be grounded in something more substantial than preference.

---

# 13. Institutional Authority Is Not Ultimate Authority

Institutions can possess genuine authority.

But institutional authority is ordinarily:

- delegated;
- bounded;
- conditional;
- jurisdictional;
- or derived.

An institution does not become infallible merely by becoming old.

It does not become true merely by becoming large.

It does not become divine merely by using a sacred name.

It does not become an extension of the Word merely by quoting the Word.

This is particularly important for the present investigation.

The question is not:

> “Which institution should we trust?”

The prior question is:

> **What source has the authority by which institutions themselves should be judged?**

That question moves the investigation toward the need for an ultimate standard.

---

# 14. Tradition Is Not Self-Authenticating

Tradition presents a similar problem.

A teaching may have been transmitted for centuries.

That establishes historical continuity.

It does not, by itself, establish truth.

The proper questions remain:

- What is the teaching?
- What is its original basis?
- What evidence supports it?
- Does the evidence establish the claim?
- Does the claim agree with the authoritative record?
- If it conflicts with that record, which authority has the higher warrant?

Tradition can therefore be evidence of what people historically believed.

It is not automatically evidence that what they believed was true.

---

# 15. Scholarship Is Not an Ultimate Authority

The same distinction applies to scholarship.

Scholarship is indispensable when it provides:

- linguistic analysis;
- historical investigation;
- manuscript study;
- archaeological evidence;
- textual comparison;
- logical analysis;
- historical reconstruction;
- and other disciplined forms of inquiry.

But scholarship is an **activity of investigation**, not an infallible oracle.

Scholars can disagree.

Scholars can make errors.

Scholars can begin from different premises.

Scholarly consensus can change.

Therefore:

> **Scholarship can contribute evidence and analysis without becoming the final judge of truth merely because it is called scholarship.**

This distinction protects the book from becoming either anti-intellectual or intellectually naïve.

The objective is not to reject scholarship.

It is to place every human intellectual product within the correct hierarchy.

---

# 16. Philosophy Is Not the Final Court

Philosophical reasoning can clarify concepts and expose contradictions.

It can assist in examining:

- existence;
- causality;
- identity;
- morality;
- knowledge;
- logic;
- evidence;
- inference;
- and competing worldviews.

But philosophy itself contains competing systems and premises.

Therefore philosophical argument must also be examined.

A philosophical conclusion cannot become true merely because it is expressed in sophisticated terminology.

The relevant question remains:

> **Is the reasoning sound, are the premises established, and does the conclusion actually follow?**

This is exactly the dianoetic discipline developed earlier.

---

# 17. Human Reason Is Necessary but Not Ultimate

The same principle applies to reason itself.

The preceding chapters have defended reasonability.

This chapter must not undo that work by treating human reasoning as worthless.

Reason is necessary for:

- understanding propositions;
- evaluating evidence;
- recognizing contradictions;
- following arguments;
- interpreting testimony;
- making inferences;
- and judging conclusions.

But the fact that reason is necessary does not make human reasoning infallible.

A person can reason badly.

A person can begin with false premises.

A person can commit a logical fallacy.

A person can misinterpret evidence.

A person can misunderstand a text.

Thus:

> **Reason is indispensable to the investigation of truth without being the source of all truth.**

This distinction will remain important when the book later examines revelation.

---

# 18. Experience Is Not Ultimate Authority

Human experience can provide genuine evidence.

A person can experience an event.

A person can witness a phenomenon.

A person can observe a result.

But experience requires interpretation.

Two people may experience the same event and explain it differently.

A subjective experience therefore cannot automatically function as the final standard for everyone.

The question remains:

> **What actually occurred, what does the experience establish, and how should it be interpreted?**

This protects the investigation from another common substitution:

> **experience → truth**

when the actual intellectual movement must be:

> **experience → observation → evidence → interpretation → inference → judgment**

---

# 19. Why the Ultimate Authority Question Cannot Be Avoided

Every worldview eventually has to answer:

> **What finally determines whether a proposition is true?**

Even the person who says:

> “There is no ultimate authority”

has adopted a position concerning how truth claims are to be evaluated.

Even radical skepticism operates from assumptions about knowledge.

Even relativism makes a claim about the nature of truth.

There is therefore no intellectually neutral escape from the question.

The issue is not whether one has an ultimate standard.

The issue is:

> **What is that standard, and why is it justified?**

---

# 20. The Ground Must Be Stronger Than the Claim It Grounds

A foundational principle now becomes necessary.

If a proposition depends upon a premise, the premise must possess sufficient warrant for the conclusion to stand.

If an authority depends upon another authority, the higher authority must be examined.

If a truth claim depends upon a definition, the definition must be adequate.

If an interpretation depends upon a textual premise, the premise must actually be present in the text.

The ground cannot simply be a restatement of the conclusion.

Thus:

> **A conclusion cannot acquire stronger warrant merely by being repeated as its own premise.**

This is why the coming investigation into God, truth, revelation, and Scripture must be cumulative.

---

# 21. What Would Qualify as an Ultimate Ground?

At this stage, the book should not simply announce the answer.

Instead, establish the requirements.

An ultimate ground of truth would need to be adequate to explain or ground objective truth rather than merely describe human preference.

It would need to avoid dependence upon a still-higher authority.

If the proposed ground is personal and revelatory, its character would matter.

If it communicates, the reliability of its communication would matter.

If it claims to reveal truth, the authenticity of that revelation would matter.

If the revelation is committed to writing, the reliability and nature of that written record would matter.

Thus the investigation naturally moves toward a series of questions:

> **What grounds truth?**

> **Can truth have an ultimate personal ground?**

> **What would it mean for God to be that ground?**

> **If God reveals truth, how can that revelation be known to be genuine?**

These are not arbitrary new subjects.

They are the questions generated by the authority problem itself.

---

# 22. Why the Investigation Must Now Turn to God

The movement from ultimate authority to God is therefore not a leap.

It follows from the problem already established.

If truth is objective, and if ultimate authority must possess sufficient grounding, then the investigation must ask whether reality itself has an ultimate ground.

That is the question of the next movement.

The investigation will not begin:

> “God exists, therefore the Bible is true.”

That would be premature.

Instead, it will ask:

> **What grounds truth?**

Then:

> **What candidate adequately accounts for that ground?**

Then:

> **What does the biblical record establish concerning God?**

Then:

> **What follows if God is truthful, faithful, immutable, and incapable of falsehood?**

Only after these premises are developed can the argument proceed to revelation.

---

# 23. The Authority Problem and the Bible

The Bible eventually becomes central to this investigation, but not as an unexplained starting assumption.

The proper sequence is cumulative:

> **Truth**

→ **Reality**

→ **Knowledge**

→ **Evidence**

→ **Reason**

→ **Ultimate authority**

→ **Ground of truth**

→ **God**

→ **Divine revelation**

→ **Bible**

→ **Graphē**

→ **Authentication**

→ **Authority**

This sequence protects the investigation from circularity.

It also prevents the opposite error of treating Scripture as though it were merely another ancient religious document whose claims can be dismissed before its nature has even been examined.

The Bible must first be defined.

Its claims must be understood.

Its evidences must be examined.

Its record must be investigated.

Its testimony must be considered.

Its relationship to revelation must be established.

Then its authority can be properly evaluated.

---

# 24. A Critical Test for Every Proposed Authority

Whenever an authority is proposed, apply the following audit.

### Question 1 — What is it?

Define the proposed authority precisely.

### Question 2 — What does it claim?

Do not substitute someone else's summary for its actual claim.

### Question 3 — What is the basis of its authority?

Identify the grounds.

### Question 4 — What evidence supports those grounds?

Separate evidence from assertion.

### Question 5 — Is the evidence reliable?

Examine credibility, consistency, correspondence, testimony, and other relevant considerations.

### Question 6 — Does the inference follow?

Do not leap from evidence to conclusion.

### Question 7 — Is there a higher authority?

If yes, the proposed authority is not ultimate.

### Question 8 — Is the proposed authority internally coherent?

A purported ultimate authority that contradicts itself requires explanation.

### Question 9 — Does the conclusion exceed the evidence?

Avoid overstatement.

### Question 10 — What follows if the authority is established?

Only then should submission, interpretation, or practice be demanded.

This audit will govern the subsequent movements.

---

# 25. The Authority of the Word Cannot Be Established by Institutional Status

This principle deserves explicit preservation because it will later become decisive.

A document does not become Scripture because an institution labels it Scripture.

An institution recognizes something; it does not thereby create its divine origin.

Likewise, a teacher does not make a statement true by teaching it.

A Bible school does not make a doctrine true by including it in a curriculum.

A denomination does not make a doctrine true by adopting it.

A government does not make a proposition true by legislating it.

Truth and authority must therefore be traced to their proper grounds.

This is precisely why the investigation must eventually examine **canon** rather than merely accept an institutional list of books without analysis.

---

# 26. Authority Must Ultimately Be Accountable to Truth

The relationship can now be stated in its proper order:

> **Truth is not accountable to authority.**

Rather:

> **A claimed authority is accountable to truth unless that authority is itself established as the ultimate ground of truth.**

This distinction is critical.

A court can issue a judgment.

The judgment can still be wrong.

A scholar can publish a conclusion.

The conclusion can still be wrong.

A church can adopt a doctrine.

The doctrine can still be wrong.

An institution can call itself Christian.

The label does not establish conformity to Christ or truth.

The question remains:

> **What is true?**

and:

> **What has sufficient authority to govern our judgment concerning that truth?**

---

# 27. The Coming Investigation Must Avoid Two Opposite Errors

### Error A — Authority without examination

> “Accept this because we declare it authoritative.”

This produces dogmatism without demonstrated grounding.

### Error B — Examination without any possible final authority

> “Everything must remain permanently subject to human doubt.”

This prevents knowledge from ever reaching a justified conclusion.

The proper course is neither.

It is:

> **Examine → establish → distinguish → conclude → submit to what is established.**

This is the intellectual posture required by the entire architecture of this book.

---

# 28. What This Chapter Has Established

The chapter has not yet established the final authority of Scripture.

That would violate the knowledge flow.

It has established the **problem that any final authority must solve**.

An ultimate authority must not merely be named.

Its authority must be grounded.

Its claim must be distinguishable from the evidence supporting the claim.

The evidence must be assessed.

The inference must be justified.

The authority must not depend indefinitely upon a higher authority.

And the investigator must distinguish the authority itself from every human interpretation or institution claiming to represent it.

This gives us the necessary bridge to Movement II.

---

# Chapter Conclusion

The question of ultimate authority is unavoidable because every serious investigation of truth eventually asks:

> **What finally governs judgment?**

The answer cannot simply be:

> “Whatever I believe.”

Nor:

> “Whatever my institution teaches.”

Nor:

> “Whatever tradition has preserved.”

Nor:

> “Whatever the majority accepts.”

Nor:

> “Whatever a scholar says.”

Nor even:

> “Whatever seems reasonable to me.”

Each of these can be examined, corrected, or contradicted.

The ultimate question is therefore deeper:

> **What is sufficiently grounded to function as the final standard of truth and judgment?**

And once an authority is proposed, the next question is equally unavoidable:

> **What authenticates that authority?**

The investigation has now reached the boundary between epistemology and ontology.

We have asked how truth can be known.

We must now ask:

> **What ultimately grounds truth itself?**

That is not a detour.

It is the next necessary premise.

# Chapter Reference Notes

## Primary conceptual distinctions

| Concept | Proper question |
|---|---|
| **Truth** | What is actually the case? |
| **Knowledge** | How can a person know what is the case? |
| **Evidence** | What bears rationally upon a truth claim? |
| **Warrant** | What makes a conclusion sufficiently justified? |
| **Authority** | What has the right or standing to govern judgment? |
| **Ultimate authority** | What governs judgment where there is no higher appeal? |
| **Authentication** | What establishes genuineness, reliability, origin, or authority? |
| **Recognition** | How is an existing authority identified as such? |
| **Interpretation** | How is an authoritative text correctly understood? |

## Governing sequence

> **Claim → Definition → Evidence → Analysis → Inference → Conclusion → Consequence**

## Governing authority sequence

> **Proposed authority → grounds → evidence → authentication → warranted recognition → submission**

## Essential guardrails

- Do not assume that naming an authority establishes its authority.
- Do not confuse truth with authority.
- Do not confuse evidence with authority.
- Do not confuse recognition with creation.
- Do not make institutional status equivalent to divine authority.
- Do not make scholarly consensus equivalent to truth.
- Do not make tradition self-authenticating.
- Do not make human reason the creator of truth.
- Do not make human reason irrelevant to the investigation.
- Do not establish Scripture as the final authority before the premises required for that conclusion have been developed.
- Do not create an artificial external standard that would make ultimate authority impossible to establish.
- Do not use circular reasoning as a substitute for authentication.
- Do not prematurely solve the God question in Chapter 8; Chapter 8 establishes why the God question must now be asked.
