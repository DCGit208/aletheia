## Author's Preface

This book begins with a declaration made in heaven: “Thou art worthy, O Lord, to receive glory and honour and power: for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created” (Revelation 4:11, KJV).

The declaration is not a decorative line. It is a claim about God, creation, worthiness, purpose, and the meaning of existence. If it is true, then creation is not an accident, human life is not purposeless, and pleasure cannot be dismissed as though it were inherently shameful or morally suspect. The question is not whether pleasure matters. The question is what pleasure means when its source and measure are the Creator.

This book argues that pleasure belongs within the very purpose of creation. It challenges religious portrayals of God as an oppressor who begrudges creaturely joy, and cultural portrayals that identify pleasure exclusively with appetite, consumption, excess, or the disregard of others. Both distort the subject. Pleasure must be understood in relation to the goodness, will, wisdom, and purpose of God.

The argument is deliberately ambitious. It moves from the throne of God to the purpose of creation; from the meaning of pleasure to the moral question of what is worthy of desire; from the pleasures of human life to the dangers of disordered appetite; and from the abundant life announced by Christ to the responsibilities of people, rulers, institutions, and societies.

The book does not claim that every experience called pleasurable is good. Scripture itself speaks of the pleasures of sin and of people who love pleasure more than God. Those passages cannot be ignored. They force us to distinguish pleasure as a good gift and proper end from gratification detached from truth, justice, love, and the good of others. The argument is not that appetite makes an act righteous. It is that pleasure, rightly understood and rightly ordered, is not the enemy of righteousness.

The reader is invited to examine the scriptural record, follow the reasoning, test the distinctions, and consider the consequences.

## Introduction — The Question Religion Was Afraid to Ask

Why should the word *pleasure* make a religious person uncomfortable? Why is pain so often treated as though it were more spiritual than joy? Why have people learned to suspect delight, to distrust enjoyment, and to imagine that holiness must mean the absence of happiness?

There are reasons for caution. Human beings can desire what harms them. A person can enjoy domination, deception, cruelty, intoxication, exploitation, or the humiliation of another. The fact that an experience feels pleasurable does not prove that it is good. But the opposite conclusion is equally false: the existence of corrupt pleasures does not make pleasure itself corrupt.

A category must not be condemned because some instances within it are evil. Food can nourish or poison. Power can protect or oppress. Speech can tell truth or deceive. Sexuality can be expressed in covenantal love or in exploitation. The misuse of a good does not establish that the good itself is evil.

The governing question of this book is therefore not, “Can pleasure be abused?” Of course it can. The governing question is, “What is pleasure in the order of God's creation, and what does the Creator reveal about its place in human life?”

Revelation 4:11 places the question before the throne. The living creatures and elders do not pronounce God worthy because He has satisfied a human theory. They declare His worthiness because He is the Creator and because all things exist by His will and for His pleasure. The verse directs attention away from human speculation and toward divine purpose.

John 10:10 brings the question into the mission of Christ: “I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly.” Abundant life is not a licence for selfish excess. It is life in the fullness of the purpose of God. Christ does not introduce a rival purpose to creation; He comes as the one through whom life is given, restored, and made abundant.

The thesis of this book is that pleasure, understood as delight in what is truly good, belongs to the purpose of God for creation and human existence. The highest pleasure cannot be separated from the highest good. Since God is good, the fullest and most enduring delight is found in rightly relating to Him and in participating in the good He wills.

This thesis challenges two opposite errors. The first is religious suspicion: the idea that pleasure is inherently worldly, that God is glorified by human misery, or that holiness requires a permanent hostility toward enjoyment. The second is ungoverned hedonism: the idea that whatever an individual desires or enjoys is therefore justified. Neither follows from Revelation 4:11.

The chapters that follow define pleasure, examine the scriptural language of divine delight and human enjoyment, distinguish good pleasure from corrupt gratification, and consider the moral, personal, social, and political consequences of recovering pleasure as a subject of serious thought.

# Chapter 1 — The Throne and the Declaration of Purpose

Revelation 4 is a vision of the throne of God. Its setting matters. The declaration of verse 11 is not spoken in a marketplace, a court of human opinion, or a private conversation about personal preference. It belongs to a scene of heavenly worship in which the Creator's worthiness is acknowledged.

The elders say: “Thou art worthy, O Lord, to receive glory and honour and power.” They then give the reason: “for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created” (KJV). Worthiness, glory, honour, power, creation, and pleasure are joined in one declaration. The verse does not present creation as purposeless material that God later tries to make meaningful. Creation exists because of Him and in relation to His will.

The wording of the King James Version deserves careful handling. The Greek phrase commonly represented in modern translations as “because of your will” uses the word *thelēma*, meaning will, desire, or that which is willed. The KJV renders the thought “for thy pleasure.” This lexical observation does not cancel the KJV's theological force; it helps the reader understand the relationship between divine will and divine pleasure. God's pleasure is not an arbitrary mood detached from His character. It is bound to what He wills as the good Creator.

The argument must therefore be made carefully. Revelation 4:11 explicitly declares that all things were created by God's will and that their existence is related to His pleasure. The further claim that pleasure is the purpose of all things is the theological conclusion this book develops from that declaration and from the wider scriptural witness. It is not necessary to pretend that one English word settles every philosophical question. The strength of the argument lies in following the declaration through Scripture.

Why is God worthy? Not because creation grants Him worth, as though He depended on creatures to become glorious. He is worthy because He is the Creator. The created order owes its existence to Him. Glory and honour are fitting responses to the One from whom all things derive their being.

This establishes an order: God is not judged by creation; creation is understood in relation to God. Human beings do not invent the final meaning of pleasure and then impose it upon the Creator. They must learn what pleasure means in the light of the Creator's character and purpose.

The throne therefore confronts every account of existence that treats life as accidental, meaningless, or self-authorizing. It also confronts religious accounts that speak as though God created only to demand service while remaining indifferent or hostile to the good of His creation. The declaration is richer: the Creator wills, creates, sustains, and takes pleasure.

The first conclusion is foundational: **existence is purposeful, and its purpose must be understood from the One who created it.**

# Chapter 2 — Who Is the God Whose Pleasure Matters?

A discussion of pleasure becomes confused when it begins with human appetite and then projects that appetite onto God. The God of Scripture is not a magnified human consumer. He does not seek pleasure because He is incomplete, bored, needy, or dependent upon creation to repair a deficiency in Himself.

God's pleasure must be understood in relation to who He is. Scripture describes Him as good (Psalm 100:5; Mark 10:18), righteous (Psalm 11:7), merciful (Exodus 34:6–7), truthful (Titus 1:2), loving (1 John 4:8), and wise (Romans 16:27). His will is not morally empty. He does not call evil good because He happens to desire it. His pleasure is consistent with His nature.

This matters because the word *pleasure* can refer to very different things. A person may take pleasure in beauty, friendship, discovery, justice, music, food, rest, generosity, or the flourishing of a child. Another may take pleasure in cruelty, manipulation, or the defeat of a rival. The emotional category is similar; the moral objects are not. We must ask not only whether someone experiences pleasure, but what is being enjoyed, by whom, at whose expense, and in what relation to truth and goodness.

The Scriptures speak of God taking pleasure in His people (Psalm 149:4), in those who fear Him and hope in His mercy (Psalm 147:11), and in the prosperity or well-being of His servant (Psalm 35:27). Zephaniah 3:17 portrays God rejoicing over His people with joy. Luke 12:32 says it is the Father's good pleasure to give the kingdom. These are not pictures of a God who is emotionally hostile to the good of His creatures.

God's pleasure is not selfishness. Selfishness seeks one's own gratification by disregarding the good of others. God's pleasure is the delight of perfect goodness; it does not compete with the good of His creation. When He wills what is good, His creatures are not rivals to His joy. Their flourishing can be an object of His delight.

The claim that God is good is therefore central, not incidental. If God were cruel, His pleasure would be terrifying. But Scripture does not present cruelty as His essence. It presents Him as the good Creator whose judgments are righteous and whose mercy is real. The question of divine pleasure must be approached through that revelation, not through caricatures of God as a cosmic tyrant.

To call God the God of Pleasure is not to reduce Him to pleasure. It is to recognize that His creative will, His delight in goodness, and His purpose for creation belong together. He is the source, measure, and judge of what is truly good.

# Chapter 3 — What Is Pleasure? A Full Account of the Word

Pleasure is a broad human experience and a philosophical category. It cannot be reduced to one sensation. It includes delight, enjoyment, satisfaction, gladness, gratification, contentment, joy, appreciation, fulfilment, and the positive experience of attaining or participating in something regarded as good.

Pleasure may be bodily, emotional, intellectual, relational, aesthetic, moral, spiritual, or contemplative. The pleasure of a meal is not identical to the pleasure of understanding a difficult truth. The pleasure of rest is not the same as the delight of reconciliation. The enjoyment of music differs from the satisfaction of completing a just task. Yet all belong to the broad field of positive experience.

Pleasure can be immediate or reflective. Immediate pleasure is felt in the moment: the taste of food, the warmth of sunlight, laughter, a melody, physical comfort. Reflective pleasure arises when a person recognizes the meaning or goodness of something: the satisfaction of keeping a promise, the joy of seeing a student understand, the delight of a family reconciled, or the peace of having acted with integrity.

Pleasure can be sensory, but it is not only sensory. It can be intellectual: the delight of discovery, comprehension, invention, and insight. It can be relational: affection, friendship, belonging, trust, intimacy, and shared celebration. It can be moral: the satisfaction of justice, mercy, courage, generosity, and truth. It can be spiritual: worship, gratitude, communion with God, hope, and the joy of His presence.

Pleasure can also be described by its duration and depth. Some pleasures are brief and intense; others are quiet and enduring. Some satisfy a momentary appetite; others shape a whole life. Some depend upon external circumstances; others arise from settled character, meaningful relationships, and confidence in what is true.

A comprehensive account must also distinguish pleasure from neighbouring concepts:

- **Happiness** often describes a broader state of well-being or positive life experience, while pleasure may describe a particular delight or satisfaction.
- **Joy** can include deep gladness that persists even when circumstances are difficult; it is not identical to momentary comfort.
- **Gratification** is the satisfaction of a desire or appetite. It may be good or bad depending on the desire and the means of satisfying it.
- **Contentment** is a settled sufficiency or peace, not necessarily the intensity of enjoyment.
- **Fulfilment** concerns the realization of a capacity, purpose, commitment, or meaningful end.
- **Delight** is a strong positive response to something valued; it may involve affection, admiration, wonder, or enjoyment.
- **Satisfaction** is the experience that a need, desire, task, or expectation has been adequately met.
- **Eudaimonia**, in philosophical discussion, concerns flourishing or living well; it is broader than a passing pleasurable sensation.

Pleasure may accompany the possession of a good, the exercise of a capacity, the appreciation of beauty, the completion of a worthy action, or the presence of someone loved. It may also arise from false beliefs and corrupt desires. That is why pleasure needs discernment.

The central definition advanced here is: **pleasure is the positive experience of delight, enjoyment, satisfaction, or fulfilment arising from an object, action, relationship, condition, or reality that a person experiences as desirable.** Its moral quality depends upon the nature of the object, the truth of the desire, the means by which it is pursued, and its consequences for the person and others.

This definition is intentionally wider than bodily sensation. A society that recognizes only consumption as pleasure has impoverished the concept. A religion that recognizes only suffering as spiritually serious has also impoverished it. Human beings can delight in truth, justice, wisdom, beauty, love, work, rest, generosity, worship, and the flourishing of others.

# Chapter 4 — Pleasure, Goodness, and the Highest Good

The question “What is pleasurable?” is not the same as the question “What is good?” A person can enjoy what is destructive. The fact that an action produces pleasure does not, by itself, establish its moral worth. A complete ethical account must explain the relationship between pleasure and goodness.

This book argues that pleasure reaches its highest form when it is the delight of what is truly good. The highest good cannot be an isolated sensation, because sensations pass and can be produced by morally corrupt objects. Nor can the highest good be mere painlessness, because a life without pain could still be empty of love, truth, justice, purpose, and meaningful activity.

If God is the supreme good, then delight in God and in what accords with His goodness has a claim higher than temporary gratification. Psalm 16:11 speaks of fullness of joy in God's presence. Psalm 37:4 calls the reader to delight in the Lord. These texts do not describe God as the enemy of joy; they locate joy in the right object.

Pleasure is ethically significant because human beings are not indifferent machines. We pursue, avoid, love, fear, admire, and desire. The objects that attract us shape our habits and our character. An ethical life must therefore ask what should be loved, what should be desired, what should be enjoyed, and what kinds of pleasure should be rejected.

The book's claim that pleasure is the highest good must be understood in this ordered sense: not every pleasure is the highest good, but the fullest good is not hostile to delight. Goodness is not made good by a passing feeling; rather, rightly ordered pleasure is the fitting enjoyment of what is good. Pleasure divorced from goodness becomes an unreliable guide. Goodness divorced from all delight becomes a distorted account of the life God gives.

This distinction is essential to the book's use of the word *hedonism*. In philosophy, hedonism has several forms. Psychological hedonism claims that people are ultimately motivated by pleasure or the avoidance of pain. Ethical hedonism claims that pleasure is intrinsically good or that it has a central place in the moral evaluation of life. These positions are not identical, and neither should be confused with the popular caricature that hedonism means “do whatever feels good.”

The argument here is a theological account of pleasure: the Creator is the source of life and the proper measure of the good; therefore pleasure must be understood in relation to His will and character. If the term *divine hedonism* is used, it must mean that delight in the good has its source, order, and final measure in God—not that every appetite is self-justifying.

A moral principle that celebrates one person's gratification while permitting another person's misery cannot claim to express the goodness of God. The pleasure worth defending is not the pleasure of exploitation. It is pleasure consistent with truth, justice, love, freedom from oppression, and the flourishing of persons.

# Chapter 5 — The Difference Between Pleasure and Indulgence

One of the strongest objections to a theology of pleasure is the obvious fact that people can use pleasure as an excuse for destructive behaviour. Scripture itself warns about this. Hebrews 11:25 speaks of the pleasures of sin. James 4:1–3 connects conflict with disordered desires. Second Timothy 3:4 describes people as lovers of pleasures more than lovers of God. These passages must be faced directly.

They do not establish that all pleasure is evil. They establish that desire can become disordered and that pleasure can be pursued against God. The phrase “pleasures of sin” already distinguishes pleasure in itself from pleasure attached to sin. Likewise, “lovers of pleasures more than lovers of God” identifies a question of priority and allegiance. The problem is not that a person experiences delight; the problem is that pleasure becomes an ultimate authority and displaces the good.

Indulgence is not simply enjoyment. It is the ungoverned or excessive satisfaction of appetite, especially where the desire ignores truth, responsibility, proportion, or the well-being of others. A person may indulge anger, greed, vanity, lust, consumption, or the desire to dominate. The pleasurable feeling does not sanctify the object.

The distinction can be stated plainly:

- Pleasure is a broad category of positive experience.
- Appetite is a desire for some perceived good or satisfaction.
- Indulgence is the pursuit or satisfaction of appetite without fitting restraint or moral order.
- Sin is not defined by the intensity of enjoyment but by its relation to God's will and righteousness.
- Discipline is not hatred of pleasure; it is the ordering of desire toward what is genuinely good.

A person who refuses to exploit others is not rejecting pleasure. That person is rejecting a corrupt means of gratification. A person who chooses fidelity over betrayal is not choosing misery as a virtue. That person is protecting a deeper good. A person who exercises restraint may be preserving health, trust, freedom, and future joy.

Pain is not automatically virtuous, just as pleasure is not automatically righteous. Pain can warn, discipline, accompany sacrifice, or result from injustice. It can also be needless, cruel, and destructive. Pleasure can be wholesome or corrupt. Neither pain nor pleasure should be worshipped as an isolated absolute. Both must be understood in relation to truth and the good.

The book therefore rejects the false choice between holiness and enjoyment. Holiness is not permission for appetite to rule; neither is it a command to despise every form of delight. It is a life ordered toward God, in which desires are examined, disciplined, and directed toward what is good.

# Chapter 6 — The Pleasure of God in the Scriptural Record

The theme of divine pleasure does not rest on one verse alone. Scripture repeatedly describes what God delights in, what pleases Him, and what He wills for His people.

Psalm 149:4 says that the Lord takes pleasure in His people. Psalm 147:11 says that the Lord takes pleasure in those who fear Him and hope in His mercy. Psalm 35:27 calls for the Lord to be magnified, “which hath pleasure in the prosperity of his servant.” These passages present divine pleasure as directed toward people and toward their good.

Zephaniah 3:17 describes the Lord in the midst of His people as mighty to save and rejoicing over them with joy. Isaiah 62:4 uses the language of delight in describing God's relation to His people. Luke 12:32 says, “it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the kingdom.” Philippians 2:13 says that God works in believers both to will and to do of His good pleasure. Ephesians 1:5 and 1:9 speak of the good pleasure of His will.

The cumulative picture matters. God is not portrayed as a being who takes pleasure only in commands being obeyed while remaining indifferent to the life of His people. His pleasure is connected to mercy, salvation, relationship, kingdom, obedience, and the fulfilment of His purpose.

The Scriptures also speak of what does not please God. He does not delight in wickedness (Psalm 5:4); He rejects injustice and hypocrisy; He calls for righteousness, mercy, and truth. The contrast is instructive. Divine pleasure is morally defined. It is not indiscriminate approval.

A serious study must gather these passages and allow them to illuminate one another. The Bible Introduction supplied for this project emphasizes collecting the relevant biblical information across Scripture rather than treating the Bible as a systematic discourse on every topic in one location. This book follows that method: it begins with Revelation 4:11 and examines the wider record concerning God's will, delight, goodness, and the life He gives.

The result is not a God who is embarrassed by pleasure. It is a God whose delight is inseparable from His goodness and whose will gives creation its purpose.

# Chapter 7 — Human Pleasure: Gift, Responsibility, and Abundant Life

The biblical account of human life includes enjoyment. Ecclesiastes speaks of eating, drinking, and finding satisfaction in one's labour as gifts from God (Ecclesiastes 2:24; 3:12–13; 5:18–20). First Timothy 6:17 describes God as the One “who giveth us richly all things to enjoy.” These texts resist the claim that enjoyment is inherently suspect.

The gift, however, carries responsibility. Enjoyment is not an excuse to forget the Giver, to waste what has been entrusted, or to harm another person. The ability to enjoy food does not justify gluttony that destroys health or the exploitation of those who produce it. The pleasure of wealth does not justify oppression. The pleasure of intimacy does not justify coercion or betrayal. The enjoyment of authority does not justify domination.

John 10:10 declares Christ's purpose in terms of life abundant. Abundance should not be reduced to possessions, constant excitement, or the fulfilment of every appetite. Life is more than accumulation. It includes reconciliation with God, truth, freedom from destructive bondage, meaningful relationships, purposeful work, peace, wisdom, and the capacity to give and receive good.

Christ's words do not introduce a divine hostility toward human flourishing. They expose the thief's destructive work and announce life in abundance. This abundance is not measured merely by the number of pleasurable sensations in a day. It is the richness of life restored to its proper source and direction.

The claim that Jesus came to testify to or make manifest a purpose already present in the Creator's will should be developed with care. John 10:10 directly states His mission in terms of abundant life. Revelation 4:11 speaks of creation's relation to God's will and pleasure. The theological connection is that the life Christ gives is consistent with the Creator's purpose; it is not a rival to it. Where sin has damaged human life, Christ brings life rather than celebrating the damage.

Human pleasure is therefore a gift to receive with gratitude, a capacity to exercise with wisdom, and a responsibility to order toward the good. The mature person does not need to despise enjoyment to prove seriousness. Nor does the mature person surrender judgment to appetite. Maturity is the ability to recognize and pursue the pleasures that belong to a good life.

# Chapter 8 — The Ethics of Pleasure: The Good of the Other

If pleasure is to be defended as an ethical principle, the argument must pass beyond the private individual. A person may ask, “What gives me pleasure?” The ethical question asks, “What kind of pleasure can be pursued without denying the good, dignity, and rightful claims of another?”

A pleasure founded on another person's degradation contradicts the goodness this book seeks to defend. Exploitation may gratify the exploiter, but it is not a worthy expression of the Creator's purpose. Cruelty may entertain a cruel person, but the experience does not become good because it is enjoyed. The pleasure of power must be distinguished from the goodness of responsible authority.

The principle is not that every person's preferences must be satisfied. Desires conflict, resources are finite, and some desires are unjust. The principle is that human beings should seek forms of enjoyment consistent with truth, justice, love, and the flourishing of others. The pleasure of one should not be purchased through the unjust destruction of another.

This gives social meaning to generosity. A person can take pleasure in another's success, recovery, learning, freedom, or happiness. Such delight expands rather than diminishes the good. It is the opposite of envy, which experiences another's good as a personal loss.

Families are transformed when the pleasure of each member is not pursued at the expense of the others. Institutions become more humane when they recognize that people are not merely instruments of output. Economies are judged not only by production but by the quality of life they make possible and the harms they permit. Education should awaken the delight of understanding, discovery, competence, and contribution. Leadership should take pleasure in the flourishing of those entrusted to its care.

This is why pleasure has public consequences. A society that stigmatizes every enjoyment produces shame and hypocrisy. A society that worships gratification produces exploitation and exhaustion. A society ordered toward genuine good seeks conditions in which people can live, learn, create, work, rest, love, worship, and flourish without being crushed by the appetites or power of others.

# Chapter 9 — Kings, Power, and the Pleasure of a Just Society

Those who govern have a special responsibility because their decisions shape the conditions in which millions of people live. Power can create the conditions for flourishing, or it can manufacture misery. A ruler may take pleasure in wealth, praise, control, and the obedience of subjects. But these pleasures are not proof of worthy leadership.

The question Revelation 4:11 places before every ruler is ultimate: the Creator is worthy; the ruler is not the creator. Authority is therefore accountable. Human power is delegated, limited, and morally answerable. A king who treats people as instruments for personal gratification has confused office with ownership.

A just ruler should take pleasure in the good of the people: security, honest courts, meaningful work, education, health, opportunity, peace, and the protection of the vulnerable. This is not sentimental politics. It is a practical recognition that authority exists within a moral order and that the well-being of people is not an incidental concern.

The prophets repeatedly condemn rulers who exploit the poor, manipulate justice, and use power for private gain. Scripture's criticism is not a criticism of pleasure as such; it is a criticism of pleasure detached from righteousness. The ruler's delight in luxury cannot excuse the misery of those whose labour or rights have been stolen.

The same principle applies to institutions, churches, businesses, and families. Leadership should not be measured by how much pleasure the leader extracts from the position. It should be measured by the good the position makes possible for those served.

A society awakened to the Creator's purpose will ask different questions. Does our economy enable people to live meaningful lives? Does our education cultivate understanding and capability? Do our laws protect the weak from the strong? Do our institutions reward truth? Do our leaders take pleasure in the flourishing of the people or in their dependence and fear?

Kings should tremble—not because the book threatens them with theatrical language, but because it reminds them that power is not ultimate and that the Creator's goodness is the measure by which human authority is judged.

# Chapter 10 — The Stigma of Pleasure and the Awakening of Society

The stigma surrounding pleasure has often been produced by confusion. Some have treated the abuse of pleasure as proof that pleasure itself is evil. Others have used religious language to justify control, deprivation, or suspicion toward ordinary human enjoyment. At the opposite extreme, commercial culture has reduced pleasure to consumption and instant gratification. Both distort the human person.

The recovery of pleasure requires intellectual honesty. We must acknowledge the biblical warnings against corrupt desire while also acknowledging the biblical celebration of joy, delight, gratitude, feasting, beauty, rest, friendship, and the enjoyment of God's gifts. Neither group of texts should be silenced to protect a predetermined ideology.

This book challenges religious beliefs that portray God as an oppressor who begrudges human happiness. It also challenges cultural beliefs that define freedom as the right to satisfy every appetite without regard for consequence. The first produces needless guilt; the second produces bondage under desire. Neither is abundant life.

A transformed person learns to desire what is good. Such a person does not merely ask whether something feels good now, but whether it is truly good, whether it is just, whether it builds life, whether it honours God, and whether it contributes to the flourishing of others. This is not the death of pleasure. It is the education of pleasure.

A transformed society would not make suffering its badge of virtue or gratification its highest law. It would value the conditions in which people can experience meaningful joy: truthful relationships, just institutions, safe communities, purposeful work, access to knowledge, creative opportunity, rest, beauty, worship, and the freedom to develop their God-given capacities.

The awakening begins when pleasure is rescued from caricature. It is not inherently evil, and it is not self-authenticating. It is a profound dimension of human experience that must be understood from the Creator and ordered toward the good.

## Conclusion — For Thy Pleasure

The declaration of Revelation 4:11 remains: all things were created by God's will and in relation to His pleasure. The Creator is worthy. Creation is purposeful. Pleasure cannot be dismissed as an embarrassing subject, because the scriptural witness places divine pleasure within the account of who God is and why all things exist.

The argument has not been that every desire is righteous, every gratification is beneficial, or every experience called pleasure should be pursued. Scripture itself rejects the pleasures of sin and warns against loving pleasure more than God. Those warnings do not abolish pleasure; they establish the need for truth, order, and moral discernment.

The central claim is stronger and more careful: pleasure rightly understood belongs to the good purpose of God. The highest delight is not found in rebellion against the Creator but in the enjoyment of what is truly good. The abundant life Christ announces is consistent with the Creator's purpose and calls human beings out of destructive bondage into life.

For individuals, this means refusing both shame-driven denial and appetite-driven slavery. For families, it means making room for joy, affection, safety, and mutual flourishing. For institutions, it means measuring success by the good they produce rather than the gratification of those who control them. For rulers, it means recognizing that authority is accountable to the Creator and should take pleasure in justice and the flourishing of the people.

The question is no longer whether pleasure belongs in serious theology, ethics, or public life. The question is whether we will understand it truthfully.

The God of Pleasure is not the patron of selfish excess. He is the good Creator whose will gives existence purpose, whose delight is consistent with His character, and whose life-giving purpose challenges every system that glorifies misery, exploitation, or empty gratification.

The invitation is to recover pleasure without surrendering truth, to pursue delight without abandoning righteousness, and to understand abundant life in the light of the One who created all things.

**“Thou art worthy, O Lord, to receive glory and honour and power: for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created.” — Revelation 4:11 (KJV)**
