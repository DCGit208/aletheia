# 11. Creation Is Not Merely Being: The Goodness of Creaturely Life

Revelation 4:11 concerns all things, not merely the human capacity to think about God. The question is larger than whether an individual may enjoy a meal, music, friendship, or a day of rest. It concerns the character of the created order and the purpose for which it exists.

Genesis 1 repeatedly presents God's assessment of creation as good. The repeated verdict is not a statement that every creature has the same function or that every created thing is pleasurable in the same way. It is the Creator's approval of an ordered creation that fulfils His intention. The final declaration that creation is “very good” (Genesis 1:31) gives the reader a foundation for rejecting the notion that material existence is inherently contemptible.

Psalm 104 invites the reader to consider a world filled with provision, movement, food, water, shelter, work, and creaturely dependence. The psalm celebrates the Creator's wisdom and provision. Psalm 19 speaks of the heavens declaring God's glory. Psalm 65 describes the earth as watered and enriched. Psalm 145 celebrates God's open hand and the satisfaction of the desire of every living thing. These passages do not say that every event in nature is comfortable or that creation is free from disorder. They show that provision, beauty, abundance, and satisfaction belong within biblical praise of the Creator.

The distinction between existence and flourishing is important. To exist is to be; to flourish is to live in the proper development and exercise of one's capacities. A tree may exist while deprived of water; its existence is not the same as its flourishing. A person may remain alive while deprived of safety, affection, knowledge, meaningful work, or freedom from oppression. The fact of existence alone does not exhaust the good of life.

The book's thesis is not that every creature experiences pleasure in the same conscious way. Nor does it claim that every created event is pleasant to human beings. Rather, the Creator's purpose includes the goodness, order, provision, and flourishing of creation. Human pleasure is one dimension of that wider goodness, and it must be understood within it.

The supplied *Bible Introduction* describes the Bible as revealing God's dealings with humanity and says that “man's good” is its design and “the glory of God” its end. It also calls the Bible “a world of pleasure.” That phrase is a striking part of the source's description of Scripture itself. It does not independently prove every philosophical conclusion in this book; it demonstrates that the supplied study tradition does not treat pleasure as a forbidden subject. Its language joins the good of humanity and the glory of God rather than placing them in automatic opposition.

The same source warns that the Bible is not a systematic discourse on one subject. Its instruction must be gathered from different writers and passages. This is the method required here. Genesis, Psalms, Proverbs, the Prophets, the Gospels, and the Epistles each contribute to the subject. No single text should be forced to carry the entire weight of a doctrine when the wider record supplies the fuller account.

The conclusion is not that creation exists to satisfy human appetite. Human beings are not the measure of all things. The conclusion is that creation is not purposeless, and its goodness is not an embarrassment to divine holiness. The Creator's pleasure and the creature's proper good are not enemies.

# 12. The Vocabulary of Pleasure: What the Words Can and Cannot Prove

A book about pleasure must take language seriously. English translations use several words—pleasure, delight, joy, gladness, satisfaction, desire, good pleasure, and enjoyment. These words overlap, but they are not interchangeable in every passage. The original languages also use more than one term. A doctrine cannot be established by treating every occurrence of an English word as though it translated the same Hebrew or Greek word.

Revelation 4:11 is the first essential lexical control. The Greek text has the noun *thelēma* (θέλημα), commonly translated “will.” The King James Version renders the phrase “for thy pleasure”; many modern translations render it “because of your will.” The Cambridge Greek Testament commentary explains that the Authorized Version's “pleasure” is to be understood as “good pleasure,” not necessarily as the idea that God created things simply to entertain Himself. The lexical point is not a reason to discard the KJV. It is a reason to understand its older English use accurately: pleasure here is connected to God's will, purpose, and good pleasure.

That connection is central to the thesis. God's pleasure is not an impulse that competes with His wisdom. It is His good will in action. The Creator does not need to justify His will by appealing to a standard outside Himself; nevertheless, the Scriptures reveal His will as consistent with His character—good, righteous, faithful, and true.

The New Testament noun *hēdonē* (ἡδονή) is the source of the English word “hedonism.” It occurs five times in the Greek New Testament: Luke 8:14; Titus 3:3; James 4:1; James 4:3; and 2 Peter 2:13. In these contexts it is associated with pleasures or desires that choke fruitfulness, accompany former enslavement, wage war within people, motivate selfish requests, or characterize unrighteous conduct. These uses must not be concealed. They show that the New Testament can use *hēdonē* negatively when pleasure becomes disordered or controlling.

Second Timothy 3:4 uses a different word: *philēdonos* (φιλήδονος), “lover of pleasure,” formed from the idea of loving and pleasure. The verse condemns people who are “lovers of pleasures more than lovers of God” (KJV). The comparison is decisive. The text is not condemning every enjoyment; it condemns a love of pleasure that outranks love for God. It would be inaccurate to claim that this verse teaches that all lovers of pleasure are exemplary. It is equally inaccurate to use it to argue that all pleasure is evil. The moral issue is priority, object, and order.

The New Testament also uses *eudokia* (εὐδοκία) for good pleasure or gracious purpose in passages such as Ephesians 1:5, Ephesians 1:9, and Philippians 2:13. Hebrews 11:25 uses a different term, *apolausis*, in the phrase concerning the enjoyment of the pleasures of sin for a season. These distinctions matter because a doctrine of divine pleasure cannot be constructed by flattening the vocabulary into one undifferentiated word.

The Hebrew Scriptures likewise employ a range of terms for delight, desire, favour, and pleasure. Psalm 1:2 speaks of delight in the law of the Lord; Psalm 37:4 speaks of delighting in the Lord; Psalm 147:11 speaks of the Lord taking pleasure in those who fear Him and hope in His mercy. The precise Hebrew terms and grammatical forms differ. The passages should be read in context rather than treated as interchangeable proof texts.

The lexical conclusion is modest but important: Scripture has a rich vocabulary for divine good pleasure and human delight, while also warning against particular pleasures and disordered desire. The existence of negative uses does not make the whole category negative. The existence of positive uses does not make every desire good. Context, grammar, object, and argument determine the meaning in each passage.

A further distinction is necessary. The philosophical term *hedonism* is a later label used for several theories about pleasure, motivation, and moral value. Scripture does not use the modern academic label as a technical doctrine. This book therefore does not claim that the historical philosophical school called hedonism began as a formal system in the Bible. It argues something more fundamental: pleasure as a created human experience, and delight in what is good, cannot be separated from the Creator who made all things and whose good pleasure is revealed in Scripture.

# 13. The Cross, Self-Denial, and the Place of Pain

Any serious theology of pleasure must answer the question of suffering. If pleasure belongs to God's purpose, why does Scripture speak so frequently about pain, sacrifice, persecution, discipline, grief, and the cross? Does the Christian life not require self-denial? Did Christ not suffer?

The answer begins by refusing a false opposition. The existence of pain does not prove that pain is the highest good. The existence of pleasure does not prove that pleasure is the highest good in every form. Pain and pleasure are experiences; their moral meaning depends upon their context and relation to the good.

Pain can serve as warning. It can reveal injury, danger, injustice, or a need for change. It can accompany the cost of doing what is right. A parent may endure sleepless nights for a child; a physician may undertake difficult training to relieve suffering; a person may accept the discomfort of telling the truth rather than preserve a convenient lie. In such cases, the pain is not loved for its own sake. It is endured for the sake of a good that is judged more important.

The same distinction applies to self-denial. Biblical self-denial is not the denial that good things are good. It is the refusal to make the self, its appetites, or its immediate preferences the final authority. Jesus calls His disciples to take up the cross and follow Him (Matthew 16:24). The call is costly because loyalty to God may require surrendering what is desired in the moment. It does not teach that suffering is intrinsically superior to joy.

Hebrews 12:2 presents Jesus as enduring the cross “for the joy that was set before him.” The verse places suffering and joy in the same account. The joy did not make the cross painless; the pain did not erase the joy set before Him. The passage is not a licence to romanticize suffering. It shows that a worthy end can sustain endurance through suffering.

The cross also prevents a shallow account of abundant life. John 10:10 cannot mean that believers will never experience loss, illness, grief, persecution, or death. The New Testament itself records these experiences. Abundant life is not a promise of uninterrupted comfort. It is life in relation to Christ, a life whose source and hope are not destroyed by present affliction.

The doctrine of pleasure must therefore reject two errors. The first is pain-worship: treating deprivation, misery, or suffering as proof of holiness. The second is comfort-worship: treating any pain, restraint, or sacrifice as a violation of the good life. Both make an experience the final measure. Scripture instead directs the person toward faithfulness, righteousness, love, endurance, and the hope of life.

It is wrong to tell an oppressed person that suffering is automatically God's preferred condition for them. It is also wrong to promise that a faithful person will never suffer. The moral response to unjust suffering is not to praise the injustice; it is to oppose it, relieve it where possible, accompany those who endure it, and remain faithful to what is good.

Pleasure is not the denial of pain. It is the recovery of the good for which life is given. Sometimes the pursuit of that good requires endurance. Sometimes it requires rest, celebration, restoration, and delight. Wisdom knows the difference.

# 14. Is Hedonism from God? Recovering the Claim Without Confusing the History

The title of this book deliberately places God and pleasure together. The argument also challenges the stigma attached to hedonism. But a serious work must distinguish the origin of pleasure from the history of a philosophical label.

The English word *hedonism* derives from Greek *hēdonē*, pleasure. In the history of philosophy, the label has been applied to different positions. Some accounts describe pleasure as the only intrinsic good; others describe human motivation in terms of pleasure and pain; still others distinguish immediate bodily gratification from more durable forms of enjoyment. Ancient schools associated with Aristippus and Epicurus did not hold identical positions, and modern utilitarian thinkers developed still other accounts of pleasure and welfare.

This book does not need to claim that God authored those historical schools, that every philosopher who used the term understood Scripture, or that every theory called hedonism is true. Such claims would confuse the Creator's relation to creation with the later history of human ideas.

The stronger theological claim is this: **pleasure itself is not the property of a secular ideology.** Human beings did not invent the capacity for delight. They did not create the world of taste, sound, beauty, friendship, bodily comfort, intellectual discovery, or relational joy. They did not create the good things that can rightly be enjoyed. These belong to the created order and must be understood in relation to the Creator.

If a philosophical school identifies pleasure as important, that observation does not make the school the source of pleasure. The source of the created capacity is prior to every human theory about it. A map is not the territory; a theory of pleasure is not the origin of pleasure.

Yet the book must not turn this argument into a shortcut. It is not enough to say “God created pleasure, therefore every hedonistic proposition is correct.” The conclusion does not follow. Theories must be examined. A position that makes an individual's immediate gratification the sole moral criterion cannot account adequately for justice, fidelity, truth, responsibility, or the claims of other persons. A position that treats pleasure as one dimension of a flourishing life may be closer to the argument developed here, but it too must be tested.

The book's proposal may be described as **theological hedonism** if the term is carefully defined: the Creator is the source and measure of genuine good; pleasure is a real dimension of the enjoyment of good; and human desire is to be ordered toward God and the good He reveals. This is not the claim that “whatever feels good is good.” It is the claim that goodness is not complete in a creaturely life that is permanently alienated from delight.

The phrase “lovers of pleasure” requires the same care. Second Timothy 3:4 does not commend those who love pleasure more than God. It exposes the disorder of a life in which pleasure has become a rival master. The book therefore calls people to become lovers of what is truly good and to delight in it—not to enthrone appetite. The most worthy person is not the person who gratifies every desire, but the person whose loves are rightly ordered and whose enjoyment does not depend on the diminishment of others.

That is the distinction on which the argument stands. Pleasure has its source in the Creator; the moral evaluation of a particular pleasure depends on its object, means, order, and fruit.

# 15. The Modern Stigma: Religious Suspicion and Cultural Reduction

The modern world often speaks about pleasure in contradictory ways. Commercial culture sells pleasure as a product, promising that the next purchase, experience, image, or upgrade will finally satisfy. At the same time, religious communities may speak about pleasure as though enjoyment itself were dangerous. One side commodifies pleasure; the other may stigmatize it. Both can prevent a serious understanding of what pleasure is.

The word “woke” is used in public debate in different and often conflicting ways. It can refer to awareness of injustice, or it can be used as a broad polemical label for progressive cultural positions. This book does not treat the label as an argument. It identifies specific claims and evaluates them. Where a contemporary ideology reduces moral good to personal preference, treats every restraint as oppression, or dismisses the Creator's authority as inherently hostile to human freedom, it must be challenged. Where religious rhetoric portrays God as an enemy of human flourishing, it must also be challenged.

A critique is only strong when it names the actual proposition it opposes. The book therefore refuses to use “woke” as a substitute for analysis. It asks: What is being claimed about the body? About desire? About freedom? About moral limits? About the good of others? About God? The answer must be examined rather than assumed.

The cultural reduction of pleasure to consumption is especially damaging. If pleasure is equated with buying, eating, sexual access, entertainment, or status, people are trained to become consumers rather than persons capable of deep delight. The pleasures of learning, craftsmanship, contemplation, friendship, worship, service, and creative contribution become less visible. A life can be full of stimulation and empty of fulfilment.

Religious suspicion creates another distortion when it treats the body as though it were an obstacle to spirituality, or regards ordinary enjoyment as evidence of weak faith. Scripture does not support a blanket rejection of created goods. It calls for gratitude, moderation, discernment, and obedience. First Timothy 6:17 speaks of God giving richly all things to enjoy. Ecclesiastes repeatedly recognizes enjoyment of food, work, and life as gifts from God, while also confronting vanity and the limits of human achievement.

The answer to excess is not contempt for enjoyment. It is formation. The answer to shame is not indulgence. It is truth. The answer to the commodification of pleasure is not to deny that people need joy; it is to recover the forms of delight that cannot be purchased or monopolized.

A society that takes pleasure seriously will ask whether its people have access to education, safety, meaningful work, clean environments, trustworthy relationships, rest, beauty, and the opportunity to develop their abilities. These are dimensions of a life in which human beings can flourish.

# 16. The Public Measure: Economics, Work, Education, and the Flourishing of Nations

A theology of pleasure cannot remain a private sentiment. If human beings are created with capacities for relationship, learning, work, creativity, responsibility, and delight, then social arrangements matter. Poverty, corruption, violence, preventable disease, ignorance, and exploitation can deprive people of conditions in which life may flourish.

This does not mean that public policy can guarantee happiness or that governments should attempt to manufacture a single approved form of pleasure. Human beings differ in their vocations, cultures, temperaments, and lawful preferences. A just society protects the conditions in which people can pursue meaningful goods without being coerced or exploited.

Work is a central example. Work can be a source of provision, skill, contribution, identity, and satisfaction. It can also become a site of exploitation when workers are treated as disposable instruments. A society that values human flourishing will ask not only how much output is produced, but whether work is safe, fairly organized, meaningful, and compatible with family and rest.

Education is another example. Education should not be reduced to credential collection or preparation for repetitive labour. It should cultivate perception, knowledge, reasoning, judgment, skill, creativity, and the delight of understanding. The 2015 Aletheia Libraries leaflet supplied for this work emphasizes the scholarly labour of Scripture, diligence in study, and the development of sound judgment through knowledge, perception, learning, and reasoning. That source is addressing Christian formation, not public education policy; nevertheless, its intellectual discipline is relevant to the book's argument that delight must be joined to understanding rather than left to impulse.

Leadership must be judged by the same standard. A ruler's pleasure in wealth or power is not a measure of the people's good. The prophetic critique of unjust rulers and the biblical concern for the poor show that authority is accountable. A king should take pleasure in justice, peace, honest judgment, and the flourishing of the people. The ruler who enjoys the misery of subjects has not discovered a higher form of pleasure; he has exposed a corrupt desire.

The economic implications are substantial. Wealth can enable health, shelter, education, innovation, mobility, and generosity. But wealth can also become an instrument of domination. Economic success should therefore be evaluated in relation to the lives it makes possible and the harms it permits. The good of a nation is not exhausted by aggregate output, nor is it served by policies that celebrate consumption while neglecting dignity and opportunity.

A society awakened to the Creator's purpose will not confuse austerity with righteousness or luxury with flourishing. It will seek the just conditions under which people can develop their capacities, contribute to the common good, and enjoy the fruits of honest labour.

# 17. The Objections That Must Be Answered

A book that argues for the moral importance of pleasure must answer the strongest objections rather than dismiss them.

**Objection 1: If pleasure is central, does that not justify selfishness?** No. The argument distinguishes pleasure from selfish gratification. A pleasure that depends on deception, coercion, exploitation, or the destruction of another person's good is not justified merely because it is enjoyed. The moral object and the means matter. The good of another person is not an optional decoration added after one's pleasure has been secured.

**Objection 2: Does Scripture not condemn lovers of pleasure?** Second Timothy 3:4 condemns those who love pleasures more than God. The comparison is explicit. The verse describes disordered allegiance, not a universal prohibition of enjoyment. Luke 8:14, Titus 3:3, James 4:1–3, and 2 Peter 2:13 likewise describe pleasures or desires that become spiritually unfruitful or destructive. These texts must be read alongside passages that describe God's delight in His people and the enjoyment of His gifts. The whole scriptural record is necessary.

**Objection 3: Does suffering disprove a world created for God's pleasure?** Suffering is real and must never be minimized. Revelation 4:11 does not say every creature experiences uninterrupted comfort, nor does it explain every instance of suffering in one sentence. The wider biblical account includes human rebellion, injustice, mortality, lament, judgment, redemption, and promised restoration. A doctrine of divine pleasure must account for these realities, not pretend they do not exist. The existence of suffering raises profound questions about evil and providence; it does not by itself prove that pleasure is evil or that the Creator's purpose is meaningless.

**Objection 4: Is the book merely redefining pleasure so that only morally good things count?** The book distinguishes the experience of pleasure from the moral quality of its object. This is necessary precision. People can enjoy harmful things. If “pleasure” means any felt gratification, some pleasures are destructive. If it means delight in what is genuinely good, moral evaluation is part of the definition. The book states both senses rather than confusing them.

**Objection 5: Does Revelation 4:11 really say that all things were created for pleasure?** The KJV reads, “for thy pleasure they are and were created.” The Greek phrase includes *thelēma*, “will,” and is commonly translated “because of your will.” A Cambridge Greek Testament commentary notes that the KJV's “pleasure” is to be understood as “good pleasure.” The verse relates creation to God's will and good pleasure. The book's broader thesis—that creation's purpose includes the good and delight of the Creator—is developed from this verse together with the wider biblical witness. It does not pretend that the Greek noun *hēdonē* appears in Revelation 4:11; it does not.

**Objection 6: Is pleasure really the highest ethical principle?** Not if “pleasure” means the unexamined satisfaction of any desire. That would make the pleasure of a tyrant morally equivalent to the joy of a person who protects the vulnerable. The argument is that the highest good is not hostile to delight, and that the fullest pleasure is found in what is truly good. Pleasure is ethically significant because it concerns what people love and pursue; it must be ordered by truth, righteousness, and love.

**Objection 7: Does this argument make God dependent on creation for His pleasure?** No. Revelation 4:11 declares God's worthiness as Creator. It does not say that God was incomplete before creation or that creatures supply a deficiency in Him. Divine pleasure is not human need magnified. The book speaks of God's good will and delight, not dependence.

The objections sharpen the thesis. They do not destroy it. Pleasure must be distinguished from indulgence, divine will from arbitrary appetite, abundant life from uninterrupted comfort, and the history of philosophical hedonism from the theological claim that the Creator is the source of created delight.

## Author's Note on Method and Sources

This manuscript is governed by a simple distinction: what the biblical text says, what a source says the biblical text means, and what the author infers must not be collapsed into one another.

The supplied *Bible Introduction* (Dake Study Bible Notes) contributes two important methodological points. First, it states that the Bible is not a systematic discourse on one subject and that the reader must gather information from different passages and writers to understand a subject fully. Second, in its description of the Bible, it calls Scripture “a world of pleasure” and describes Christ as its grand subject, man's good as its design, and God's glory as its end. These statements are treated as the supplied source's framing, not as a substitute for examining the cited biblical passages.

The 2015 Aletheia Libraries leaflet, *A Concise Definition for True Christ-ian Experience*, is not a treatise on pleasure. It is used for its stated methodological emphasis: diligence in study, sound judgment, knowledge developed through perception, learning and reasoning, and the need to exercise the Word rather than repeat words without understanding. It is not cited as though it directly establishes the book's thesis about pleasure.

The principal biblical text quoted in this edition is the King James Version (KJV). Where a lexical or translation question matters, the manuscript identifies the distinction rather than silently replacing the wording of the KJV. In particular, Revelation 4:11 uses *thelēma* (will, purpose, good pleasure), not *hēdonē* (pleasure/enjoyment). Second Timothy 3:4 uses *philēdonos* (lover of pleasure). These are related to the subject but are not the same Greek word.

## Key Terms

**Abundant life:** The life Christ says He came to give (John 10:10); in this book, life understood in relation to God's purpose, not reduced to uninterrupted comfort or material accumulation.

**Delight:** A positive response of enjoyment, affection, admiration, or satisfaction toward something regarded as desirable or good.

**Divine good pleasure:** God's will and purpose as consistent with His character; in Revelation 4:11, the KJV phrase “for thy pleasure” translates a clause built around *thelēma*.

**Eudaimonia:** A Greek philosophical term often translated flourishing or living well; broader than a passing pleasurable sensation.

**Gratification:** Satisfaction of a desire or appetite; its moral quality depends on the desire, object, means, and consequences.

**Hēdonē (ἡδονή):** Greek noun commonly translated pleasure; in its five New Testament occurrences it appears in contexts of disordered or destructive desire.

**Hedonism:** A family of philosophical positions concerning pleasure, motivation, and value. This book uses “theological hedonism” only in a carefully qualified sense: delight in what is truly good, with God as its source and measure.

**Philēdonos (φιλήδονος):** Greek adjective in 2 Timothy 3:4, “lover of pleasure”; the verse condemns loving pleasure more than God.

**Pleasure:** The positive experience of delight, enjoyment, satisfaction, or fulfilment arising from an object, action, relationship, condition, or reality experienced as desirable. Its moral quality depends on what is enjoyed and how it is pursued.

**Thelēma (θέλημα):** Greek noun meaning will, desire, or that which is willed; used in Revelation 4:11 in the phrase rendered “for thy pleasure” in the KJV and commonly “because of your will” in modern translations.

**Theological hedonism:** The position advanced in this book that created pleasure is not evil by origin; that genuine delight belongs to the good purpose of God; and that human desire must be ordered by God's character and the good of others.
