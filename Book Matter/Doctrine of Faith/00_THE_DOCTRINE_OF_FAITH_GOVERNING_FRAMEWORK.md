## 1. PURPOSE AND AUTHORITY OF THIS FRAMEWORK

This document establishes the governing doctrine, intellectual progression, scriptural foundations, lexical direction, chapter architecture and editorial standards for *The Doctrine of Faith — Book I*.

It is a working authority for the author, editor and book-development process. It is not the publication manuscript and must not be reproduced as an author's guide in the reader-facing book.

This framework is to guide the development of the entire Doctrine of Faith, not merely provide a table of contents. The Contents must be derived from the argument established here; the argument must not be reduced to the Contents.

Every chapter, subsection, scriptural exposition, lexical investigation, doctrinal explanation and transition must serve one connected progression. The book must establish:

1. What faith is as hypostasis and pistis.
2. Why faith raises the question of the essential nature and state of the person.
3. What nature humanity was created to possess by God's Design.
4. How Scripture attests participation in divine nature and the believer's state in relation to God.
5. What it means that humanity came short of the glory of God.
6. What justification and redemption accomplish through Christ.
7. What restoration to the nature, glory and oneness intended by God means.

No older working file, outline, manuscript draft, editorial reconstruction or assistant-generated interpretation may silently displace this framework. Where an older document conflicts with it, this framework governs. Relevant material may be recovered only after it has been assessed for consistency with this authority and with the controlling Aletheia doctrinal source.

## 2. THE GOVERNING DOCTRINAL THESIS

# Faith is our state by Design.

The doctrine begins with faith as **hypostasis** in Hebrews 11:1 and develops the relationship between **hypostasis** and **pistis**. The inquiry is not primarily about what a person believes, what they expect, or how strongly they trust God. It is about what faith is as the substantial nature, state and reality of the person.

The governing question is therefore not simply, “What should a Christian believe about faith?” It is:

**What is faith as the nature and state of the person, what nature did God establish in humanity by Design, and what has Christ restored through redemption and justification?**

Faith must not be reduced to a belief, an opinion, a religious conviction, an act of believing, confidence in an outcome or a technique for obtaining something from God. Such words may occur in conventional definitions or describe particular expressions and effects, but they do not exhaust the doctrine being investigated here.

The book investigates faith as a matter of **nature, acceptance, identity, divine Design, glory and restoration**.

The Gospel of Jesus Christ must consequently be examined as more than the communication of religious propositions or the provision of forgiveness as an isolated benefit. The governing restoration argument is that humanity is restored through Christ to the nature, glory and relationship with God established in the original Design.

Justification and redemption must be developed within this larger argument. They must not be detached from the nature and glory from which humanity came short.

## 3. THE THREE-PART DOCTRINAL ARCHITECTURE

The book has three cumulative Parts. They are not separate books or independent theological discussions.

- **Part I — Faith as Hypostasis; Faith as Pistis:** establishes the epistemological and lexical foundation.
- **Part II — Hypostasis II:** investigates the nature that the foundation of Part I brings into view.
- **Part III — Justified:** develops justification and redemption as restoration to the nature and glory of God's Design.

# PART I — FAITH AS HYPOSTASIS; FAITH AS PISTIS
## The epistemological and lexical foundation of the Doctrine of Faith

Part I establishes the foundational meaning of faith upon which the entire book rests. It examines two essential Greek terms in their respective scriptural contexts and then develops their doctrinal relationship.

### Chapter 1 — Faith as Hypostasis

**Foundational Scripture: Hebrews 11:1**

The first chapter establishes hypostasis as the foundational inquiry into the nature of faith.

The lexical investigation must examine the compound and semantic range of the Greek term, including:

- That which is set or stands beneath.
- Support and underlying foundation.
- Essence and underlying reality.
- Substantial quality and nature of a person or thing.

The supplied lexical direction describes hypostasis as a setting under or support and, figuratively, as essence, substructure or foundation, and the substantial quality or nature of a person or thing. The investigation must verify the precise lexical source and wording before presenting any definition as a direct lexicon quotation.

Particular attention must be given to **nature as the substantial quality or essential reality of a person or thing**. This is not a passing lexical possibility. It is the central direction of the author's inquiry.

Hebrews 11:1 must be examined as a complete sentence. Both **hypostasis** and **elenchos** must be addressed. The exposition must not collapse the two terms into one, omit the second, or allow the attention given to hypostasis to distort the complete wording and argument of the verse.

The chapter must establish why defining faith as hypostasis raises a more fundamental question than conventional discussions of belief, confidence or expectation.

The governing question is:

**If faith is hypostasis, what does this tell us about the essential nature and state of the person?**

The chapter must prepare the reader for the investigation of pistis in Chapter 2. It must not prematurely substitute the discussion of pistis for the work of establishing hypostasis.

**Important scope correction:** Hebrews 10:35–39 must not be made a standalone argument, a central organizing point or an independent doctrinal excursion in this chapter. The previous approach that foregrounded Hebrews 10:35–39 and then used Hebrews 11 as an extended discussion of endurance, promises, unseen things and conduct displaced the actual core inquiry and was rejected by the author. Do not revive that structure. Any reference to surrounding material must be justified by the immediate exegetical need and must never displace the chapter's responsibility: establishing faith as hypostasis and opening the inquiry into nature.

The chapter must conclude by establishing the epistemological foundation for the investigation of pistis.

### Chapter 2 — Faith as Pistis

**Foundational Scripture: Hebrews 6:1**

This chapter investigates **pistis** as the Greek term rendered faith.

The lexical foundation begins with the definition of pistis as **credence**. The investigation must go beyond conventional treatments of faith as belief, trust, confidence, religious conviction or fidelity. Those expressions may describe aspects or applications of faith, but they do not exhaust the doctrinal significance developed by the Aletheia source.

The central relationship to establish is:

**Credence → Acceptance → Acceptable State → Nature**

Credence must be examined in relation to acceptance, especially the idea of the state of being acceptable. The argument must not casually treat a dictionary's list of possible glosses as though every gloss were the meaning of pistis in every context. The semantic range, the immediate scriptural usage and the author's doctrinal synthesis must be distinguished.

The chapter must engage the Aletheia Libraries 1 leaflet's explicit teaching:

> “Faith essentially defines our state as children of God.”

The leaflet's connection between pistis, credence, acceptance, true nature, adoption and Sonship is foundational. Its teaching must be engaged fully, accurately and in its own terms. It must not be diluted into the conventional claim that faith is merely a person's belief about God.

The investigation must establish the relationship with:

- **Galatians 4:5–7** — adoption, sonship and heirship.
- **Romans 8:15** — the Spirit of adoption.
- **Ephesians 1:5** — adoption through Jesus Christ.
- **Galatians 3:26** — children of God through faith in Christ Jesus.

#### The relationship between pistis and justification

An essential lexical and doctrinal connection must be developed between **pistis** and **dikaioō**.

Romans 3:24 introduces justification through Christ. The lexical investigation of dikaioō must examine the supplied rendering: **to render just or righteous; to render a person as he ought to be**. The precise lexical source and semantic range must be verified. This supplied rendering must not be silently replaced by a conventional explanation that narrows justification to a favourable verdict and leaves the restoration question unanswered.

The argument must investigate the relationship with **dikaiosynē**, righteousness, and the Aletheia source's explanation of equity as conformity with the nature of God.

The question is not merely whether a person has received a favourable verdict. The larger doctrinal inquiry is what it means to be rendered as one ought to be in relation to God's Design.

This chapter must prepare the reader for the full treatment of justification and redemption in Part III without prematurely exhausting those subjects.

Part I must establish that faith concerns nature, and that pistis brings the inquiry into credence, acceptance and the state of God's children.

# PART II — HYPOSTASIS II
## The investigation of our essential nature

Part II continues the inquiry established in Part I. Having examined faith as hypostasis and pistis, the next question is unavoidable:

**What exactly is the nature that faith defines?**

The answer is developed through three interconnected scriptural premises. These premises must be treated as a connected argument, not as unrelated proof texts.

### Premise One — Creation: The Original Design

**Foundational Scripture: Genesis 1:26–28**

Creation is the first and foundational witness to humanity's nature.

The argument begins with God's declaration:

> “Let us make man in our image, after our likeness.” — Genesis 1:26, KJV

The exposition must examine the significance of image, likeness and the original Design of humanity. Genesis 1:26–28 must be treated as a connected creation account, not reduced to an isolated quotation.

Genesis 1:27 must also be examined alongside the rendering supplied in **The Living Bible**:

> “Like God did God make man.”

The wording, verse reference and translation attribution must be checked against the actual edition before publication. Do not silently attribute this wording to the KJV or another translation.

The governing question is:

**If the Creator made humanity in His image and likeness, what does this establish about the nature humanity was designed to possess?**

The original Design is the reference point for understanding the nature from which humanity came short. The argument must explain why the Creator's act and declaration are cogent testimony to human nature by Design.

### Premise Two — Participation in Divine Nature

**Foundational Scripture: 2 Peter 1:4**

This premise provides further scriptural testimony concerning the nature under investigation.

The declaration that believers become **partakers of the divine nature** must receive full exposition. The chapter must examine the immediate context of 2 Peter 1:2–8, including:

- The knowledge of God and Jesus Christ.
- Divine power and the promises given.
- Participation in divine nature.
- Escape from the corruption in the world through lust.
- The qualities described in verses 5–7.
- Fruitfulness in the knowledge of Jesus Christ.

The relationship between Genesis 1:26–28 and 2 Peter 1:4 must be demonstrated. Creation establishes the original Design; participation in divine nature provides further testimony concerning the nature associated with God's promises.

The argument must then connect this testimony with Romans 3:23–24.

The question is:

**If humanity was created in God's image and likeness, and Scripture speaks of participation in divine nature, what does it mean to come short of the glory of God?**

The exposition must show why 2 Peter 1:4 is a direct and substantial witness in the investigation of the nature being discussed. It must not be reduced to a passing devotional statement.

### Premise Three — The Faith of God and Our State

**Foundational Scriptures: Mark 11:22; 1 John 4:17; John 17:20–23**

This premise develops the believer's state in relation to God's nature, faith and glory. It is a central doctrinal component, not an incidental application.

#### Mark 11:22 — “Have the faith of God”

The KJV wording must be quoted accurately:

> “And Jesus answering saith unto them, Have faith in God.” — Mark 11:22, KJV

The author's governing interpretation is that this expression is to be understood as **“Have the faith of God”**—not merely as an instruction to believe in God, trust that God exists, or exercise confidence that God will perform a particular act.

This interpretation is central to the doctrine and must be developed fully and responsibly. The question is what Jesus meant by directing his hearers to have the faith of God.

The doctrinal meaning being advanced is:

**Have the state of God. Have the nature of God.**

Why? Because this is who humanity is by Design, and redemption and justification restore the person to that Design. The instruction is therefore examined as a summons to live with an awareness of the divine nature and state that belong to humanity's Design and are restored in Christ—not as an instruction to manufacture a foreign nature through religious effort.

**Additional authorial clarification:** The question behind Jesus' words is, “What did he mean?” The intended interpretation, “Have the faith of God,” is to be understood as **have the state of God; have the nature of God**. This is not the acquisition of an identity foreign to humanity: it points back to who humanity is by God's Design. Redemption and justification restore us to that Design. This clarification reinforces the connection between Mark 11:22, the original nature established in creation, and the restoration developed in Part III.

The exposition must examine Jesus' teaching in Mark 11 in its immediate context, including the fig tree and the subsequent teaching. It must accurately distinguish:

1. The wording of the KJV translation: “Have faith in God.”
2. The author's interpretive rendering and doctrinal reading: “Have the faith of God.”
3. The scriptural and linguistic reasoning offered to establish that reading.
4. The doctrinal implication: the state and nature of God as humanity's Design and the reality to which redemption restores us.

Do not present the author's interpretive rendering as though it were the literal wording of the KJV. Equally, do not evade, dilute or replace the author's intended interpretation with the conventional explanation that Jesus was simply telling people to have confidence in God. The linguistic and contextual case must be made transparently and with precision.

#### 1 John 4:17 — “As he is, so are we in this world”

The KJV text must be quoted accurately:

> “Herein is our love made perfect, that we may have boldness in the day of judgment: because as he is, so are we in this world.” — 1 John 4:17, KJV

The statement **“as he is, so are we in this world”** must receive serious doctrinal treatment in relation to nature, identity and the believer's present state. Its immediate context must be examined. It must not be reduced to a general statement about confidence at judgment while its direct relevance to the nature and state of the believer is ignored.

#### John 17:20–23 — Oneness and glory

John 17:20–23 must establish the further dimension of oneness and glory. Particular attention must be given to Jesus' prayer for those who believe, the oneness of believers with the Father and the Son, the glory given to them, and their being made perfect in one.

The three witnesses must converge:

1. Humanity's original Design in creation.
2. Participation in divine nature through God's promises.
3. The believer's state in relation to the faith, nature and glory of God.

Part II must establish what nature is being discussed, why it belongs to humanity's original Design, and how the testimony of Scripture reveals the believer's relationship with that nature.

# PART III — JUSTIFIED
## Justification and redemption as restoration to our true nature

Part III brings the argument to its redemptive conclusion.

Having established the nature revealed through creation, participation in divine nature and the believer's oneness with the Father, the book must explain the significance of justification and redemption.

The central question is:

**What did humanity come short of, and what does Christ restore through justification and redemption?**

### Chapter 1 — The Glory We Came Short Of

**Foundational Scripture: Romans 3:23–24**

The exposition must begin with the complete relationship between sin, coming short of God's glory, justification and redemption.

The KJV text must be reproduced accurately:

> “For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God; Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus:” — Romans 3:23–24, KJV

The phrase **“come short of the glory of God”** must be examined in relation to the original Design established in Part II.

The chapter must demonstrate the relationship between:

- Genesis 1:26–28.
- 2 Peter 1:4.
- 1 Corinthians 11:7.
- Romans 3:23–24.
- 2 Corinthians 4:4–6.

The glory of God must not be treated as an incidental reference to splendour or an abstract religious expression. Its relationship to humanity's created nature and the image revealed in Christ must be developed.

### Chapter 2 — Dikaioō: What It Means to Be Justified

The lexical investigation must examine **dikaioō** in Romans 3:24 and its relationship with **dikaiosynē**.

The supplied Aletheia explanation of righteousness as equity, impartiality and conformity with the nature of God must be engaged fully.

The chapter must examine the expression **“render just or righteous, as one ought to be”** in relation to the restoration argument.

Relevant Scriptures include:

- Romans 3:21–26.
- Romans 5:17–21.
- 2 Corinthians 5:21.
- Galatians 4:4–7.
- Hebrews 1:8.

The argument must establish the doctrinal significance of being made the righteousness of God in Christ. It must connect justification with the restoration to God's Design rather than isolating it as a legal term without the nature and restoration dimensions established in the preceding Parts.

### Chapter 3 — Apolytrōsis: Redemption and the Ransom in Full

The investigation of **apolytrōsis** must establish the meaning and significance of redemption in Christ. The original-language form, lexical identification and semantic range must be verified.

Redemption must not be detached from justification or treated as an isolated benefit. The chapter must explain how the redemptive accomplishment of Christ relates to humanity's restoration from the condition of sin and the realization of God's original Design.

### Chapter 4 — One in Glory: Perfect in One

**Foundational Scriptures: John 17:20–23; 1 Corinthians 15:41; 1 John 4:17**

This chapter brings the entire doctrinal argument to its culmination.

John 17:20–23 must be examined in its complete movement, particularly Jesus' prayer for those who believe, their oneness with the Father and the Son, and the glory given to them.

1 Corinthians 15:41 must be quoted accurately:

> “There is one glory of the sun, and another glory of the moon, and another glory of the stars: for one star differeth from another star in glory.” — 1 Corinthians 15:41, KJV

The verse must be examined in its resurrection context while developing its relevance to the book's argument concerning glory. Its context must not be ignored or made to say, by itself, more than the passage establishes. Its contribution to the larger argument must be explained in relation to the other foundational witnesses.

The conclusion must bring together:

- The nature established in creation.
- Participation in divine nature.
- The state described by “as he is, so are we in this world.”
- The glory revealed in Christ.
- The oneness expressed in Jesus' prayer.
- Justification and redemption as the restoration argument.

The governing doctrinal conclusion is that restoration through Christ is restoration to the nature and glory of God's Design, culminating in oneness with the Father.

The statement **“If we are one in glory with Him, then we are Him”** represents the author's intended doctrinal conclusion. It must receive a full, coherent scriptural exposition rather than being presented as an unsupported slogan. The meaning of oneness, glory, nature and identity must be established through the connected testimony of the cited Scriptures. Do not silently dilute the author's conclusion into a generic statement that believers merely resemble God. Equally, the exposition must make clear what the author means by this conclusion within the scriptural argument and must not leave the reader to infer an unstated meaning.

The book must conclude with the significance of what Christ has accomplished and what the believer is in relation to God's original Design.

## 4. GOVERNING SCRIPTURAL AND LEXICAL METHOD

### 4.1 Scripture is the primary foundation

The argument must be established through Scripture. No Greek or Hebrew definition, commentary, theological tradition or secondary publication may replace the authority of the scriptural testimony.

Every major doctrinal conclusion must be supported by the relevant biblical passages and developed through their contexts.

### 4.2 Lexical investigations must be substantive and precise

Greek and Hebrew terms must be examined in their original forms, transliteration, lexical identification, semantic range and scriptural usage.

Particular terms include:

- Hypostasis.
- Elenchos.
- Pistis.
- Dikaioō.
- Dikaiosynē.
- Apolytrōsis.

Lexical definitions supplied by the author or Aletheia source must be engaged fully. Their wording must not be silently replaced with conventional theological explanations.

Where a lexical definition is presented as a direct dictionary or lexicon quotation, its precise source and wording must be verified. The semantic range of a word must be distinguished from the meaning established by its particular context. A lexical possibility is not automatically the contextual meaning of every occurrence.

The book must not use etymology as a substitute for exegesis. Compound analysis can contribute to an investigation, but a word's meaning must also be established through its documented usage and the context of the passage.

### 4.3 Preserve the Aletheia source's doctrinal authority

The **2015 Aletheia Libraries 1 leaflet** is the controlling Aletheia doctrinal source for the teaching developed in this book.

The book must exhaustively engage its relevant teaching, terminology, scriptural references and doctrinal relationships. It must not omit difficult or distinctive source positions merely because they differ from conventional theological presentations.

Editorial development must improve clarity, organization, reasoning and literary quality without silently replacing the source's doctrine.

The leaflet is not to be treated as a casual suggestion or a source whose central teaching can be discarded whenever a conventional interpretation is more familiar. Its relevant teaching must be faithfully and thoroughly represented. The task is to articulate and develop the doctrine, not to neutralize it.

### 4.4 Distinguish the levels of argument

The writing must maintain clear distinctions between:

1. Direct statements of Scripture.
2. Explicit teachings of the Aletheia source.
3. Lexical evidence from original-language resources.
4. The author's doctrinal synthesis and conclusions.

These elements must work together without being misrepresented as identical forms of evidence. The author's interpretation must be identified as interpretation where appropriate, while still being developed seriously and faithfully.

### 4.5 Contextual integrity

Every Scripture passage must be examined in its literary and argumentative context. A passage must not be quoted merely because it contains a convenient expression. Where a passage belongs to a larger argument, the relevant surrounding verses must be examined.

Contextual analysis must serve the doctrine's actual question. It must not become an excuse to introduce unrelated excursions, familiar theological themes or arguments that displace the chapter's assigned responsibility.

### 4.6 Do not drift into conventional treatments rejected by the author

The following approaches are prohibited as substitutes for the governing doctrine:

- Defining faith primarily as believing something.
- Treating faith chiefly as confidence, expectation or a technique for obtaining desired outcomes.
- Reducing the doctrine to religious conviction or intellectual agreement.
- Treating the believer's nature as something wholly foreign that must be acquired through effort.
- Explaining justification only as a favourable verdict while leaving the restoration of nature unexamined.
- Treating redemption as an isolated transaction unrelated to the original Design.
- Replacing the author's reading of Mark 11:22 with a conventional confidence-based explanation.
- Making Hebrews 10:35–39 a standalone or organizing argument in Chapter 1.
- Replacing the Aletheia source's distinctive teaching with generic theological summaries.

These prohibitions do not prevent accurate discussion of belief, trust, confidence, conduct, judgment or other biblical themes where they are genuinely relevant. They prevent those themes from displacing or redefining the doctrine.

## 5. SCRIPTURE QUOTATION AND VERIFICATION STANDARD

The **King James Version (KJV)** is the principal quotation translation unless another translation is explicitly identified.

Every direct quotation must be checked against the actual biblical text.

Mandatory controls:

- Preserve the exact wording of the identified translation.
- Preserve the correct verse reference.
- Identify the translation.
- Do not combine words from different translations without disclosure.
- Do not present paraphrases as direct quotations.
- Check punctuation and verse boundaries.
- Distinguish a quotation from an explanation placed in parentheses.
- Verify every quotation against its original source before publication.
- Where the 2015 leaflet uses a different translation, preserve and identify that translation accurately.
- The Living Bible wording associated with Genesis 1:27 must be checked against the edition and credited correctly.
- The KJV wording of Mark 11:22 must remain distinguishable from the author's interpretive rendering, “Have the faith of God.”

**Reference correction:** Romans 3:23–24 is the intended passage. Any occurrence of “Romans 3:23–34” in earlier notes is a typographical error and must be corrected to Romans 3:23–24.

The same verification standard applies to all biblical quotations, including the passages listed in the three Parts.

## 6. CHAPTER DEVELOPMENT AND PEDAGOGICAL STANDARD

Every chapter must be developed as a complete intellectual and doctrinal experience. The reader must be taken through a logical progression rather than confronted with unexplained conclusions.

A chapter will ordinarily include:

1. **Chapter title:** Clear, distinctive and doctrinally meaningful.
2. **Foundational Scripture:** The principal biblical text.
3. **Opening argument:** Establish the question the chapter answers.
4. **Lexical foundation:** Explain relevant original-language terminology where applicable.
5. **Scriptural exposition:** Examine the principal passages in context.
6. **Aletheia source engagement:** Develop the corresponding source teaching.
7. **Doctrinal reasoning:** Demonstrate how the evidence connects.
8. **Implications:** Explain what the established teaching means.
9. **Chapter conclusion:** State what has been established and prepare the reader for the next stage.
10. **Study and reflection:** Develop meaningful questions that test comprehension and doctrinal reasoning.

Not every chapter needs identical subsection headings. The architecture must serve the argument rather than force artificial uniformity.

### Pedagogical expectations

The writing must:

- Explain difficult ideas without diluting them.
- Introduce technical terms before relying on them.
- Build from established premises to conclusions.
- Explain why each Scripture is relevant.
- Develop major teachings fully rather than compressing them into a few sentences.
- Avoid repetitive explanations that do not advance the argument.
- Use illustrations only where they genuinely improve understanding.
- Provide transitions that maintain the movement of the entire book.
- Enable readers to follow the reasoning independently.
- Make clear which premises have been established and which conclusions depend upon them.

The book must be intellectually demanding but accessible to a serious reader without advanced theological or linguistic training.
