# Chapter 1 — Assessment

**Chapter:** “Faith as Hypostasis: Substance, Underlying Reality, and the Foundation of the Doctrine”  
**Assessment status:** **ASSESSMENT COMPLETE — TARGETED STRENGTHENING**  
**Amendment status:** **NOT STARTED — HOLD UNTIL ALL TEN CHAPTERS ARE ASSESSED**

## 1. Assigned responsibility

Chapter 1 must:

1. Establish Hebrews 11:1 and *hypostasis* as the foundation of Book I.
2. Examine the lexical range and contextual use of *hypostasis*, including substance/underlying reality and assurance/confidence.
3. Compare Hebrews 1:3 and Hebrews 3:14 without assuming identical senses, grammar, or referents.
4. Connect Hebrews 11:1 with the 2015 Aletheia teaching that *pistis* (credence) defines the state of God's children.
5. Establish the doctrinal thesis “faith is our state by Design” through visible reasoning rather than assertion alone.
6. Clarify that Book I develops the *hypostasis* foundation while Book II, *Faith Is Evidence*, develops the *elenchos* dimension. Both clauses remain part of Hebrews 11:1.

## 2. What the current chapter already accomplishes

- Presents both clauses of Hebrews 11:1 and distinguishes *hypostasis* from *elenchos*.
- Places Hebrews 11:1 within Hebrews 10–11: promise, endurance, confidence, obedience, the unseen, and perseverance.
- Gives a responsible account of the semantic range of *hypostasis* and warns against deriving contextual meaning from etymology alone.
- Represents both principal readings—substance/underlying reality and assurance/firm confidence—without caricaturing either.
- Compares Hebrews 1:3 and Hebrews 3:14 while expressly warning against assuming identical contextual meanings.
- Accurately reports the 2015 source's explicit *pistis* / credence / state / acceptance / true nature / adoption-as-sons teaching.
- Explicitly states that the leaflet's “Faith toward God” section does not quote Hebrews 11:1 or use *hypostasis*; the relationship is the book's doctrinal development.
- Introduces the wider Design and Sonship passages and preserves the Book I / Book II boundary.
- Identifies Mark 11:22 as a bridge while acknowledging that “state of God” is not established as a literal lexical gloss by grammar alone.

## 3. Findings requiring attention

### Primary gap — demonstrate the *hypostasis* / *pistis* state relationship more fully

The chapter states the bridge between Hebrews 11:1 and the leaflet's *pistis* teaching, but the reasoning is not yet developed with sufficient depth. It needs to explain more persuasively:

- what the substance/underlying-reality reading contributes to the question of what faith is;
- how the source's explicit description of *pistis* as credence defining the state of God's children illuminates the person’s state;
- how these two lines of argument can be brought together without claiming that *hypostasis* and *pistis* are synonyms;
- why the relationship supports the thesis “faith is our state by Design,” while making clear that this is the book's doctrinal synthesis, not a direct quotation from Hebrews 11:1 or the leaflet.

The chapter should not imply that Hebrews 11:1 grammatically defines human nature, or that the leaflet itself uses *hypostasis*.

### Secondary refinement — sharpen the substance reading's foundational consequence

The final reasoning should explain why the substance/underlying-reality reading is the chosen foundation for this volume, while fairly acknowledging the assurance/confidence interpretation and the contextual evidence supporting it. The argument must be reasoned, not merely asserted.

### Secondary refinement — strengthen Hebrews 1:3's representation contribution

Clarify how the Son as the *charaktēr* of the Father's *hypostasis* contributes to the wider representation thesis, “Jesus represents who we are by Design.” Preserve the distinction between the grammatical construction and referent in Hebrews 1:3 and the construction in Hebrews 11:1. Hebrews 1:3 contributes to the wider argument; it does not independently prove the believer-nature reading of Hebrews 11:1.

### Secondary refinement — define the role of Mark 11:22 here

Retain Mark 11:22 as a bridge, but make clear that Chapter 1 introduces the connection only. Its fuller treatment belongs with the Design argument in Chapter 3 and must preserve Mark 11:20–25. Do not let Chapter 1 become a second full exposition of the Design chapter.

### Whole-book coordination

Clarify which Scripture connections Chapter 1 introduces and which later chapters are responsible for demonstrating in depth. Avoid repeating the full arguments assigned to Chapters 2–10.

## 4. Controlled amendment direction

A single substantial development is recommended, provisionally titled:

**“From Substance to State: The Relationship Between Hypostasis, Pistis, and Our True Nature”**

It should develop, in a coherent sequence:

1. *Hypostasis* as the substance or underlying reality of things hoped for in Hebrews 11:1.
2. The distinction between that lexical/contextual inquiry and a claim about the believer's nature.
3. The 2015 source's explicit *pistis* / credence / state-of-God's-children teaching.
4. The source's connection of that state with acceptance, true nature, and adoption as sons.
5. The doctrinal reasoning by which these distinct lines are brought together under “faith is our state by Design.”
6. The role of Christ as the central revelation of Design and representation, without collapsing representation into redemption or displacing Christ's redemptive accomplishment.

Then make only the necessary local refinements to the substance-versus-assurance conclusion, Hebrews 1:3, Mark 11:22, and the chapter's closing transition. Do not create several disconnected additions or rewrite sound material.

# Chapter 2 — Assessment

**Chapter:** “Πίστις (*Pistis*): Credence, the State of God's Children, and Sonship”  
**Assessment status:** **ASSESSMENT COMPLETE — TARGETED STRENGTHENING**  
**Amendment status:** **NOT STARTED — HOLD UNTIL ALL TEN CHAPTERS ARE ASSESSED**

## 1. Assigned responsibility

Chapter 2 must develop the 2015 Aletheia teaching on “Faith toward God,” especially:

1. Hebrews 6:1 as the source passage introducing faith toward God.
2. *Pistis* as “credence,” preserving the leaflet's exact teaching and its force.
3. The source's direct statement that faith essentially defines the state of God's children.
4. The connection the leaflet makes between credence, acceptance, true nature, and espousal/adoption as sons.
5. The supporting Sonship passages named by the source: Galatians 4:5–7, Romans 8:15 (developed in the wider Romans 8:14–17 context), and Ephesians 1:5.
6. The relationship between faith, the Father's purpose and pleasure, the Spirit's witness, Sonship, and heirship, while distinguishing explicit Scripture from doctrinal synthesis.
7. A clear connection to Chapter 1's *hypostasis* foundation without treating *pistis* and *hypostasis* as synonyms or confusing their respective source contexts.

The chapter must also coordinate with later chapters: image and likeness / *’ĕlōhîm* (Chapter 3), divine nature (Chapter 5), and oneness (Chapter 9) are connected to the source's “Faith toward God” paragraph but receive their fuller treatment in those assigned chapters.

## 2. What the current chapter already accomplishes

- Opens with Hebrews 6:1, matching the 2015 leaflet's entry point for “faith toward God.”
- Reproduces the source's central statement that faith essentially defines the state of God's children.
- Identifies *pistis* as “credence” and accurately attributes that gloss to the leaflet.
- Preserves the source's connection to acceptance, true nature, and espousal/adoption as sons.
- Develops Galatians 4:1–7 in context, including the heir under tutors, the sending of the Son, redemption, adoption, the Spirit's cry, Sonship, and heirship through Christ.
- Develops Romans 8:14–17, including the Spirit's leading and witness, adoption, inheritance, suffering with Christ, and shared glory.
- Develops Ephesians 1:3–5, placing adoption within God's purpose and good pleasure in Christ.
- Brings Matthew 3:17 and Hebrews 11:2, 5–6 into the discussion of the Father's pleasure, good report, and faith pleasing God; it appropriately states that the doctrinal connection is a synthesis rather than making Matthew 3:17 prove the whole conclusion by itself.
- Distinguishes the leaflet's *pistis* teaching from Hebrews 11:1 and Chapter 1's *hypostasis* argument.
- Keeps the chapter's focus on Sonship and the state of God's children rather than fully taking over the later Design, divine-nature, redemption, or oneness chapters.

## 3. Findings requiring attention

### Primary gap — deepen the explanation of “credence”

The section “Credence: what is credited, and the state it defines” currently gives a short explanatory paraphrase: credence concerns the credit given to what is presented as true. That is a useful entry point, but it does not yet develop the doctrinal force of the leaflet's statement sufficiently.

Strengthen the reasoning by showing why the source's use of “credence” is not being reduced to a passing mental act, opinion, or moment of assent. The source's own sequence is the controlling guide: *pistis* / credence defines the state of God's children; that state is connected with acceptance (true nature) and espousal/adoption as sons. Explain the relationship among those elements so that “state” is not merely repeated as a slogan.

Keep the attribution precise: “credence” is the leaflet's stated gloss. Do not imply that this one English gloss exhausts every lexical use of Greek *pistis*, or substitute an unsupported lexical claim for the source's doctrinal statement.

### Secondary refinement — give “espousal” its proper treatment

The chapter quotes the leaflet's “espousal or adoption as sons” formulation, but its subsequent exposition concentrates almost entirely on adoption. Review whether the source's word “espousal” is being preserved as part of the source's wording and whether the chapter should briefly explain how it is being used in this context. Do not silently erase it, treat it as an established technical synonym without support, or invent a meaning. If the term cannot be responsibly developed from the source and its context, preserve the source wording and make the limit clear.

### Secondary refinement — clarify the relationship between faith, the Father's pleasure, and Sonship

The treatment of Matthew 3:17 and Hebrews 11:2, 5–6 is soundly cautious, but the synthesis could be made more explicit and easier to follow:

- Matthew 3:17 directly identifies Jesus as the beloved Son in whom the Father is well pleased.
- Hebrews 11:2, 5–6 directly connects faith with good report and pleasing God.
- Ephesians 1:5, Galatians 4:5–7, and Romans 8:14–17 establish the adoption / Sonship context for those in Christ.
- The conclusion that the Father's pleasure is for His sons to live in the state He designed is the chapter's doctrinal synthesis across those passages, not a direct quotation from any one of them.

Make that chain of reasoning visible without overstating what an individual passage says.

### Whole-book coordination — reduce repeated architecture summaries

The chapter's final two sections repeat the chapter's central claims and then provide a second step-by-step summary of the whole-book sequence. Some orientation is useful, but the cumulative effect is manuscript-conscious and partly duplicates the introduction, Chapter 1's closing section, and the chapter's own preceding exposition.

During the later whole-book reconciliation, decide what single concise transition is needed here. Preserve the doctrinal conclusion; remove only repetitive explanation of the book's architecture.

### Pedagogy — distinguish reader formation from editorial classification

Question 9 asks the reader to classify which statements are explicit source teaching, explicit Scripture, and doctrinal synthesis. That distinction is essential to editorial control, but in its current form it risks making the reader perform the editor's provenance audit. Consider replacing it with a substantive reflection question that tests the reader's understanding of the relationship among credence, state, acceptance, and Sonship. Retain source-provenance controls in this register and the appropriate scholarly notes, not as an unexplained reader exercise.

The other questions substantially test the chapter's actual content and may be retained subject to the consistent end-of-chapter pedagogy decision for the whole book.

## 4. Source coverage and chapter boundaries

The 2015 leaflet's “Faith toward God” paragraph also connects the state of God's children with oneness with God, *’ĕlōhîm*, Genesis 1:26–27, true nature, 2 Peter 1:4, Psalm 82:6, and the glory humanity came short of through sin. Those are genuine parts of the source's argument and must not be lost from the book. They are not all required to be fully developed in Chapter 2:

- Chapter 3 owns the *’ĕlōhîm*, image, likeness, and Design development.
- Chapter 4 owns Psalm 82 with John 10 and the judgment context.
- Chapter 5 owns participation in divine nature through 2 Peter 1:4 and its surrounding passage.
- Chapter 7 owns the source's “come short of the glory,” justification, righteousness, and redemption sequence.
- Chapter 9 owns the full oneness argument from John 17:20–23.

Chapter 2 should preserve the connections and make the transition clear, without duplicating those chapters. The source's use of “oneness with God” must remain part of the whole-book coverage; it should not be treated as an incidental phrase that can disappear merely because its full exposition is elsewhere.

## 5. Controlled amendment direction

**Targeted strengthening only.** Preserve the existing chapter structure and its substantial exposition. The later amendment should:

1. Deepen the “credence” section by explaining the source's progression from *pistis* / credence to state, acceptance / true nature, and espousal/adoption as sons.

Chapter 3 develops Genesis 1:26–28 as the creation foundation: image, likeness, blessing, fruitfulness, multiplication, replenishment, subduing, and dominion. It must address *’ĕlōhîm* and preserve the 2015 leaflet's “plural: Trinity” interpretation with accurate attribution; connect the source's “like his Maker” / true-nature teaching; develop supporting passages such as John 14:12, 1 John 4:17, Mark 11:20–25, and Matthew 5:43–48 in context; introduce but not absorb later treatments of Psalm 82 / John 10, divine nature, heirship, and oneness; and hold Jesus' representation together with His redemptive accomplishment.

## 2. What the current chapter already accomplishes

- Connects Chapters 1 and 2's distinct foundations—*hypostasis* and *pistis*—to the creation inquiry, correctly noting that Genesis 1:26–28 itself does not use either term.
- Quotes Genesis 1:26–28 and explains image, likeness, blessing, fruitfulness, and dominion as connected elements.
- Identifies *’ĕlōhîm* as the Hebrew term translated “God” and accurately attributes “plural: Trinity” to the leaflet as its theological reading.
- Does not make the noun's form alone carry the theological conclusion; it points to the full declaration and wider scriptural witness.
- Preserves the leaflet's “like his Maker” emphasis and its connection with true nature and 2 Peter 1:4.

## 3. Findings requiring attention

### Primary gap — deepen the explanation of Genesis 1:26–28

The chapter correctly identifies Genesis 1:26–28 as its foundation, but the exposition moves quickly from quotation to summary. Develop how the passage's elements work together: image and likeness in verse 26; the repeated image statement in verse 27, including male and female; and the blessing and commission in verse 28—fruitfulness, multiplication, replenishment, subduing, and dominion. Show why identity language and commission belong to one creation account. Do not add speculative anthropology or claims not supported by the passage and source.

### Secondary refinement — keep the *’ĕlōhîm* levels distinct

The existing treatment is appropriately careful. Preserve the distinction between (1) the Hebrew term *’ĕlōhîm* in Genesis 1:26, (2) the leaflet's stated theological interpretation “plural: Trinity,” considered alongside “Let us make” and “our image,” and (3) the wider doctrinal conclusion about humanity's image and likeness. Do not dilute the source's interpretation, but do not imply that morphology alone proves the full doctrine.

### Secondary refinement — clarify “like his Maker” and the TLB material

Retain the KJV of Genesis 1:27, the source's “like his Maker” emphasis, and the TLB wording where it is genuinely a TLB quotation. Keep the leaflet's separate Life Application Bible note distinct. The current provenance explanation is accurate in intent but reads partly like editorial apparatus; make the reader-facing presentation concise and clear. If the source note is ambiguous, preserve that ambiguity in source notes rather than silently conflating or correcting its attribution.

### Secondary refinement — strengthen passage-to-thesis reasoning

The chapter introduces important texts but sometimes moves quickly from quotation to doctrinal conclusion. Make the cumulative reasoning clearer for:
- John 14:12 and the works of Jesus;
- 1 John 4:17 and the identity statement in its perfected-love / confidence context;
- Romans 8:17 and children, heirs, joint-heirship, suffering, and shared glory;
- John 17:20–23 and oneness/glory as a signpost to Chapter 9.

No single passage should be made to prove the entire representation thesis.

### Secondary refinement — preserve the force and boundaries of Mark 11:22

The manuscript's distinction between *theou* as genitive and “state of God” as doctrinal interpretation is important and must remain. The strong formulation “we are God by design” is an intentional doctrinal statement in the current manuscript; do not casually dilute or remove it. Strengthen the reasoning through the connected Design argument and the source's *pistis* teaching, while continuing to state accurately that “state of God” is not a word-for-word lexical gloss established by the genitive alone. Keep Mark 11:20–25 in view; do not turn the passage into a detached formula or a guarantee that every desired result must occur.

### Whole-book coordination — keep later chapters' responsibilities intact

The chapter's references to Psalm 82, Romans 8:17, John 17, Romans 3:23–24, Ephesians 1:7, and Colossians 1:14 are legitimate connections. Their full development belongs in Chapters 4 (Psalm 82 / John 10), 5 (divine nature), 6 (why Jesus came, glory, deviation, Christ's person and work), 7 (justification, righteousness, redemption), 8 (Sonship and heirship), and 9 (John 17, oneness and glory). Chapter 3 should orient the reader without pre-empting those arguments. Its statement that no single verse carries the whole doctrine is a useful control.

### Pedagogy — refine the final exercise

Preserve the exercise's purpose: distinguish Genesis' explicit wording from doctrinal synthesis and read supporting passages in context. Its present scope is broad, asking the reader to cover many texts and the whole representation thesis at once. Consider a short sequence of progressive tasks or a narrower final synthesis question. The transition to Chapter 4 is clear and should be retained.

# Chapter 4 — Assessment

**Chapter:** “Psalm 82 with John 10: ‘Ye Are Gods’ and the Judgment Context”  
**Assessment status:** **ASSESSMENT COMPLETE — TARGETED STRENGTHENING**  
**Amendment status:** **NOT STARTED — HOLD UNTIL ALL TEN CHAPTERS ARE ASSESSED**

## 1. Assigned responsibility

Chapter 4 must examine Psalm 82:1–8 as a complete passage, especially the declaration in verse 6 and the judgment in verse 7, and then follow Jesus' appeal to Psalm 82 in John 10:30–38. It must preserve the force of “Ye are gods” without detaching it from the psalm's rebuke, judgment, and appeal to God. It should connect the passage to Chapter 3's creation foundation while retaining the distinction between the explicit wording of each passage, the 2015 Aletheia source's doctrinal use of Psalm 82:6, and the wider doctrinal synthesis. It must not take over Chapter 5's treatment of divine nature, Chapter 6's treatment of Christ's person and work, or Chapter 9's full treatment of John 17 and oneness.

## 2. What the current chapter already accomplishes

- Correctly identifies Psalm 82:6 as part of the 2015 source's teaching on true nature and the state of God's children.
- Holds Psalm 82:6 and 7 together, refusing both to isolate the declaration from the judgment and to use the judgment to erase the declaration.
- Reads Psalm 82:1–5 in context: God's judgment among the gods, the charge of unjust judgment and favouritism, the command to defend the poor and vulnerable, and the description of those addressed as walking in darkness.
- Reads Psalm 82:6–8 as a connected movement: declaration, judgment, and appeal for God to judge the earth.
- Explains that the designation “gods” does not place those addressed beyond God's authority or accountability.
- Follows John 10:30–33 from Jesus' statement of oneness and the accusation of blasphemy.
- Follows John 10:34–36 through Jesus' quotation of Psalm 82:6, His statement that Scripture cannot be broken, and His reference to those “unto whom the word of God came” and to the One sanctified and sent by the Father.
- Continues through John 10:37–38, including the appeal to the Father's works and the mutual indwelling of the Father and the Son.
- Distinguishes the explicit content of Psalm 82 and John 10 from the cumulative doctrinal conclusion developed with Genesis 1:26–28, 2 Peter 1:4, and John 17:20–23.
- Preserves the chapter boundaries by signposting Chapter 5's divine-nature treatment and Chapter 9's full treatment of oneness and glory.
- Provides study questions that ask the reader to read both passages in context and distinguish explicit statements from doctrinal synthesis.

## 3. Findings requiring attention

### Primary gap — make the interpretive question around “gods” explicit

The chapter explains the psalm's setting and Jesus' use of it, but it does not yet sufficiently prepare readers for the interpretive question raised by the designation “gods” in Psalm 82. Interpreters have understood those addressed in different ways, including as human rulers or judges, heavenly beings, or in relation to Israel.

The later amendment should acknowledge the principal interpretive possibilities in a concise, balanced way. It must not silently present one contested reading as though the wording alone settles the question. Equally, this discussion must not dilute or displace the 2015 Aletheia source's use of Psalm 82:6 in its teaching on true nature and the state of God's children.

Keep five levels distinct:
1. What Psalm 82 explicitly says in its own literary and judgment setting.
