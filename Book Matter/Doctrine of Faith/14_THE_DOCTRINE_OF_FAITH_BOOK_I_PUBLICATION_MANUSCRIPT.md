## Preface

Faith is often discussed as though it were chiefly a matter of what a person hopes to receive, how strongly a person believes, or how confidently a person speaks. The question pursued in these pages is more fundamental: what is faith, and what does it reveal about the state and identity of the children of God?

The Aletheia teaching on “Faith toward God” places *pistis*—credence—within the reality of sonship, true nature, acceptance, and the design in which humanity was created. Hebrews 11:1 gives this inquiry its foundation through *hypostasis*, rendered “substance” in the King James Version. The relationship between these scriptural lines leads to the central conviction developed here: **faith is our state by design.**

That conviction cannot be separated from Jesus Christ. He reveals the image and glory of God, and His redemptive work brings into view what God has accomplished for humanity. His representation and His accomplishment belong together.

This volume establishes the foundation. The companion volume, *Faith Is Evidence*, takes up the evidence dimension named in Hebrews 11:1 and develops it from that foundation.

## Introduction

The first question is not how to use faith, but what faith is. Hebrews 11:1 describes it as the *hypostasis* of things hoped for and the *elenchos* of things not seen. The King James Version renders these terms “substance” and “evidence.” Their relationship is essential, but they are not interchangeable. This first volume gives sustained attention to the foundation expressed by *hypostasis*; the companion volume examines the evidence dimension.

The inquiry then turns to the Aletheia teaching on “Faith toward God.” Its use of *pistis*—credence—connects faith with the state of God's children, acceptance, true nature, and adoption as sons. From there, the argument follows the scriptural connections through the image and likeness in Genesis, the partaking of divine nature, the Son and His inheritance, redemption and justification, the oneness for which Jesus prayed, and the glory revealed in His face.

These themes are not separate religious topics gathered under one title. They form a connected account of humanity's Design, the deviation described as coming short of glory, and what God has accomplished in Christ. The governing conviction is that **faith is our state by design**, and that **Jesus represents who we are by Design**. The argument must therefore hold together the identity revealed in Christ and the redemptive work accomplished through Him.

The chapters are intended to be read in sequence. Each develops a part of the argument while remaining connected to the whole. Biblical quotations are identified by translation; original-language terms are explained where they materially clarify the discussion. The 2015 Aletheia teaching remains the doctrinal foundation for the treatment of “Faith toward God.”

# Chapter 1 — Faith as Hypostasis: Substance and Underlying Reality

## Faith belongs to the foundation

Hebrews 5:11–14 calls believers to maturity in the Word of God. The writer speaks of those who, though they ought to be teachers, have need of instruction again in the first principles of the oracles of God. They have become dull of hearing and unskilful in the word of righteousness. They need milk rather than strong meat. The distinction is not between an important teaching and an unimportant one. It is between remaining at the beginning and growing into the understanding and practice of what God has spoken.

Hebrews 6:1–3 continues: “Therefore leaving the principles of the doctrine of Christ, let us go on unto perfection; not laying again the foundation of repentance from dead works, and of faith toward God.” Faith toward God is named among the foundations. It belongs to the first principles from which maturity proceeds. The writer does not present faith as an occasional religious exercise. He places it within the foundation of the doctrine of Christ.

The question, therefore, is fundamental: **What is faith?**

Hebrews 11:1 gives the answer:

> “Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen.” — Hebrews 11:1 (KJV)

The word translated “substance” is the Greek **ὑπόστασις (*hypostasis*)**. The verse declares that faith is the *hypostasis* of things hoped for. Faith is not described merely as an activity a person performs. It is described in terms of substance: the underlying reality, the actual being or foundation associated with what is hoped for.

The verse also speaks of the evidence of things not seen. Both statements belong together. Faith is presented as *hypostasis* and *elenchos*; substance and evidence. The foundation established here is the substance dimension. The evidence named in the same verse remains joined to it.

## The substance of faith

The Greek noun *hypostasis* is used in Scripture with meanings that include substance, actual being, underlying reality, foundation, confidence and assurance. Its precise force is understood from the passage in which it occurs. In Hebrews 11:1, the King James Version renders it “substance.” That rendering gives full expression to the foundational sense that governs the doctrine developed here.

To call faith substance is to speak of what is real and underlying, not something superficial or momentary. Faith is not merely a person's statement that he believes. It concerns the state from which that person's life proceeds. The substance of a person is not an appearance adopted for an occasion; it is the underlying reality of the person. In the declaration that faith is *hypostasis*, faith is placed in relation to substance and reality.

The words “of things hoped for” must remain in the sentence. The faith under consideration is related to what is hoped for. Hebrews 10:35–39 prepares the way for the declaration in chapter 11. The readers are told not to cast away their confidence, that they have need of patience, and that the just shall live by faith. The writer then speaks of those who live by faith and do not draw back. Hebrews 11:1 follows this instruction and gives the statement concerning faith.

The examples that follow show faith expressed in the lives of those who received God's word. Abel offered unto God; Noah, being warned of God concerning things not seen as yet, prepared an ark; Abraham obeyed when he was called to go out; Sarah judged Him faithful who had promised; and Moses chose to identify with the people of God. Their faith was not detached from their lives. It was expressed in their response to God and in the way they lived in relation to what He had spoken.

Hebrews 11:2 says that by faith the elders obtained a good report. Hebrews 11:5 records that Enoch had the testimony that he pleased God, and verse 6 declares that without faith it is impossible to please Him. Faith therefore belongs to the life and state from which a person responds to God. The good report and the testimony of pleasing God are presented in connection with faith.

## The underlying reality revealed in the Son

Hebrews 1:1–3 presents the Son as the brightness of God's glory and the express image of His person. God has spoken by His Son, whom He has appointed heir of all things, by whom also He made the worlds. The Son upholds all things by the word of His power and, having purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high.

The expression “the express image of his person” in Hebrews 1:3 translates the Greek words **χαρακτὴρ τῆς ὑποστάσεως (*charaktēr tēs hypostaseōs*)**. *Charaktēr* speaks of an exact impression or expression; *hypostasis* is rendered “person” in the King James Version. The Son is the express image of the Father's person. He reveals the Father's being and glory.

This passage gives substance to the relationship between *hypostasis*, nature, image and representation. The Son is not presented as an uncertain likeness. He is the express image of the Father's person. The Father's glory and being are revealed in Him.

This is central to the truth that **Jesus represents who we are by Design**. Genesis 1:26–27 declares that God made man in His image and after His likeness. First Corinthians 11:7 speaks of man as the image and glory of God. Second Corinthians 4:4 identifies Christ as the image of God. Hebrews 1:3 declares Him to be the express image of the Father's person. The Son reveals the image and glory of God; humanity's creation in God's image and likeness establishes the Design in which human nature is to be understood.

Hebrews 1:3 also declares that the Son purged our sins. His representation and His redemptive work belong together. The Son who reveals the Father's image is the Son who accomplished the purging of sins. The glory revealed in His person and the redemption accomplished through Him must be understood together. **Jesus represents who we are by Design, and through Him redemption is accomplished.**

The same Greek noun, *hypostasis*, appears in Hebrews 1:3 and Hebrews 11:1. In Hebrews 1:3 it describes the Father's person, whose express image is the Son. In Hebrews 11:1 it describes faith in relation to things hoped for. Each passage has its own statement, and each must be read in its own context. Their shared use of *hypostasis* brings the ideas of substance, being and the express image of the Father's person into the same scriptural consideration.

## Faith and our state by Design

The foundation of faith must be understood in relation to the nature in which God created humanity. Genesis 1:26 records God's declaration: “Let us make man in our image, after our likeness.” Verse 27 states that God created man in His own image; male and female He created them. This is the account of man's creation and Design.

The declaration is not about an identity humanity must invent. It is God's own statement concerning what He made. Man was created in the image and likeness of God. The image and likeness belong to the original account of humanity's creation.

Second Peter 1:4 speaks of the promises through which believers become “partakers of the divine nature,” having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust. The passage continues in verses 5–8 with the qualities that are to abound in the believer. The divine nature is not mentioned as an isolated expression; Peter connects the promise with escape from corruption and with a life in which the stated qualities abound.

Psalm 82:6 records the declaration, “Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.” The verse must be read with the judgment that follows in verse 7 and with the whole of Psalm 82. Jesus Himself refers to this Scripture in John 10:34–35. These passages belong to the scriptural consideration of man's relation to God, the declaration concerning the children of the Most High, and the words of Jesus. They will be examined in their full setting.

Faith as substance and faith as state belong to this same foundation. The doctrine is not that a person must manufacture a new identity by trying to believe hard enough. The truth begins with God's Design and what He has declared concerning humanity. **Faith is our state by Design.** It concerns the underlying reality of the children of God, not a deficiency that must be filled by pretending to possess an identity.

This is why the statement of Hebrews 11:1 is foundational. Faith is *hypostasis*: substance. The reality of faith is to be understood in relation to the nature and Design of the person God created, the Son who is the express image of the Father's person, and the redemption accomplished through Him.

## The state of God

In Mark 11:22, Jesus says, “Have faith in God.” The Greek expression is *pistin theou*. The wording brings the relationship between faith and God directly before us. The teaching developed here understands this declaration in relation to the state of God: **have the faith of God; have the state of God.**

This is not an admission of deficiency. It is an acknowledgment of our God State in relation to our Design. The statement is to be understood with the truth of creation in God's image and likeness, the true nature spoken of in 2 Peter 1:4, and the declaration concerning faith as the state of God's children. The meaning and implications of Jesus' words in Mark 11:20–25 will be brought fully into view in relation to the whole doctrine.

The foundation is therefore not a struggle to become something contrary to what God designed. It is the recognition of the state that belongs to God's Design and the life that proceeds from that reality. Faith is not separated from the person; it defines the state of the children of God.

Hebrews 11:1 calls faith substance. Hebrews 1:3 reveals the Son as the express image of the Father's person. Genesis 1:26–27 declares man's creation in God's image and likeness. Second Peter 1:4 speaks of partaking of the divine nature. These declarations establish the scriptural foundation for understanding faith as our state by Design.

Faith is our state by Design. Jesus represents who we are by Design. His person reveals the image and glory of God, and His redemptive work is the means through which redemption is accomplished. This is the foundation of the doctrine of faith.


# Chapter 2 — Pistis: Credence, Acceptance and Sonship
## Faith toward God: the state of His children

Hebrews 6:1 names “faith toward God” among the foundations of the doctrine of Christ:

> “Therefore leaving the principles of the doctrine of Christ, let us go on unto perfection; not laying again the foundation of repentance from dead works, and of faith toward God.” — Hebrews 6:1, KJV

The 2015 Aletheia leaflet opens its treatment of faith with this verse and gives a direct statement of what faith means in its teaching:

**Faith essentially defines our state as children of God.**

The leaflet identifies the Greek noun πίστις (*pistis*, G4102) as **credence**. It then connects credence with acceptance, true nature, and espousal or adoption as sons of God, citing Galatians 4:5–7, Romans 8:15, and Ephesians 1:5. Faith, in this account, is not reduced to a momentary feeling or a religious opinion. The source places it in relation to who God's children are.

This teaching builds on Chapter 1. Hebrews 11:1 describes faith as *hypostasis* in relation to things hoped for; the leaflet uses *pistis*—“credence”—to describe the state of God's children. The terms are not interchangeable, and the leaflet does not offer a *hypostasis* word study. Book I brings the two teachings into one foundational inquiry: what faith is and what it means to live in the reality God has made known.

## Credence: what is credited, and the state it defines

“Credence” concerns the credit given to what is presented as true. The object of faith is not a possibility invented by the believer. Faith toward God concerns what God has made known and the state of those who receive and credit His testimony.

The leaflet therefore joins credence to acceptance and true nature. Faith is not an isolated mental act that leaves identity untouched. Its statement reaches beyond assent: faith essentially defines the state of God's children. That force must be retained.

Nor should the word “state” be emptied of its relational meaning. The source identifies the state as that of children of God and connects it with adoption as sons. The passages it names explain the relationship: God sends His Son; redemption and adoption are joined; the Spirit bears witness to the Father–son relationship; and those who are sons are heirs through Christ.

## The Son sent, redemption accomplished, and adoption received

Galatians 4:1–7 develops the language of adoption and heirship. Paul begins with the figure of an heir who, while a child, differs nothing from a servant in his lived condition, although he is lord of all. He remains under tutors and governors until the time appointed by the father. Paul then applies the comparison to those who were under the elements of the world.

The movement reaches its decisive statement in verses 4–7:

> “But when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law,
>
> To redeem them that were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons.
>
> And because ye are sons, God hath sent forth the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, Abba, Father.
>
> Wherefore thou art no more a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ.” — Galatians 4:4–7, KJV

Paul's sequence is deliberate. God sent His Son, made of a woman and under the law, to redeem those under the law so that they might receive adoption as sons. Because they are sons, God sends the Spirit of His Son into their hearts, crying, “Abba, Father.” Paul states the consequence plainly: no longer a servant, but a son—and, if a son, an heir of God through Christ.

The words **“because ye are sons”** place the Spirit's cry within the relationship of Sonship. The cry does not create a substitute for that relationship; it expresses the reality already named. Paul's conclusion is freedom from servitude, Sonship, and heirship.

Redemption and adoption belong together. Adoption cannot be detached from the Son who came and redeemed; redemption cannot be separated from its stated purpose of adoption. The Aletheia source's account of faith as the state of God's children is set within this action of God through His Son.

## The Spirit's witness and the inheritance of God's children

Romans 8:14–17 develops the same relationship and includes the believer's life with Christ:

> “For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God.
>
> For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father.
>
> The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God.
>
> And if children, then heirs; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ; if so be that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together.” — Romans 8:14–17, KJV

Paul connects the Spirit's leading with Sonship, adoption, the cry “Abba, Father,” the Spirit's witness, and inheritance. He also includes suffering with Christ and shared glory. Inheritance is inseparable from Christ: Galatians speaks of “an heir of God through Christ,” while Romans names believers “joint-heirs with Christ.”

The Spirit's witness is not a passing sensation: it bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God. Paul then states the consequence—if children, heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ. Suffering with Christ belongs to this same movement and must remain in view alongside the promise of shared glory.

The source's language of state is relational and substantial. It concerns the identity of God's children, their relation to the Father through the Son, the Spirit's witness, and their inheritance in Christ. Faith is not a psychological condition detached from this scriptural reality.

## Adoption belongs to the Father's purpose

Ephesians 1:3–5 places adoption within God's purpose in Christ. Paul speaks of spiritual blessing in Christ, God's choosing in Him before the foundation of the world, and predestination unto adoption by Jesus Christ according to the good pleasure of God's will.

Paul states that adoption is by Jesus Christ and according to the good pleasure of God's will, placing it within God's purpose “before the foundation of the world.” The doctrinal conclusion is that adoption belongs to God's purpose; it is not an afterthought. This conclusion follows from the passage's sequence and wording rather than being presented as a separate quotation.

Galatians 4 shows the Son sent and redemption accomplished so that adoption might be received. Ephesians 1 shows adoption within the good pleasure of God's will. Together they hold God's purpose and Christ's redemptive action in one account.

## Faith, Sonship, and the Father's pleasure

The governing doctrinal conviction is clear: **faith is our state by design**. Humanity was created in the image and likeness of *’ĕlōhîm*; Scripture speaks of participation in the divine nature; and those who are sons are heirs through Christ. The Father's will and pleasure is for His sons to live in the state He designed and has made available in Christ. This is not a passing emotion but the state and identity of God's children.

Ephesians 1:5 says that adoption is according to the good pleasure of God's will:

> “Having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will,” — Ephesians 1:5, KJV

The Father's purpose and pleasure includes adoption as His children through Jesus Christ. Galatians 4:5–7 identifies those redeemed as sons and heirs; Romans 8:14–17 speaks of the Spirit's witness that they are children of God and, if children, heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ.

Matthew 3:17 records the Father's declaration over Jesus:

> “And lo a voice from heaven, saying, This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.” — Matthew 3:17, KJV

The Father identifies Jesus as His beloved Son and declares His pleasure in Him. The declaration contributes to the wider argument about divine pleasure and Sonship. The connection to the Father's pleasure in His sons is developed through Ephesians 1:5, Galatians 4:5–7, and Romans 8:14–17; Matthew 3:17 is not made to carry that conclusion by itself.

Hebrews 11:2 says:

> “For by it the elders obtained a good report.” — Hebrews 11:2, KJV

Of Enoch, Hebrews 11:5 says:

> “By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death; and was not found, because God had translated him: for before his translation he had this testimony, that he pleased God.” — Hebrews 11:5, KJV

The next verse states:

> “But without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him.” — Hebrews 11:6, KJV

These verses connect faith with good report and pleasing God. Matthew 3:17 records the Father's pleasure in His beloved Son; Ephesians 1:5 places adoption within the good pleasure of His will; and Galatians 4 and Romans 8 identify believers as sons and heirs through Christ. The doctrinal synthesis is that faith is our state by design and that the Father's pleasure is for His sons to live in that state, not beneath the identity and nature represented in the Son.

The wider scriptural framework gives this direction its substance. Genesis 1:26–27 describes humanity made in God's image and likeness. Second Peter 1:4 speaks of becoming partakers of the divine nature. Galatians 4:5–7 speaks of redemption, adoption, Sonship, and heirship through Christ. Romans 8:16–17 speaks of the Spirit's witness and of children who are heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ. These passages contribute distinct parts of the design, participation, Sonship, and heirship argument.

## The state of God's children within the book's argument

The Aletheia source's teaching on *pistis* belongs to a connected scriptural sequence. Genesis 1:26–28 gives the image-and-likeness account of humanity's creation and commission. Second Peter 1:4 speaks of partaking of the divine nature. Psalm 82:6 must be read with the judgment setting of the psalm, including verse 7, and John 10 must be read through the full argument in which Jesus cites it. Romans 3:23–24, 1 Corinthians 11:7, and 2 Corinthians 4:4–6 contribute to the book's treatment of glory and image.

These passages are not interchangeable proof-texts. Each has its own setting and contributes a distinct part of the case for faith, true nature, and the state of God's children. The governing convictions are that **faith is our state by design** and **Jesus represents who we are by Design**. That representation must be understood alongside Christ's person and accomplishment; it does not displace redemption or justification.

Chapter 1 established the book's foundational inquiry through *hypostasis* in Hebrews 11:1. This chapter has developed the Aletheia leaflet's explicit teaching on *pistis* as credence defining the state of God's children, connected with acceptance, true nature, and adoption as sons. Book I brings these teachings into relation: the substance foundation and the state of God's children are considered together, while the terms and their source attributions remain distinct.

The sequence developed here can be followed plainly:

- Hebrews 11:1 supplies the foundational *hypostasis* statement.
- The Aletheia leaflet explicitly identifies *pistis* as “credence” and says that faith defines the state of God's children.
- Galatians 4:4–7 develops the sending of the Son, redemption, adoption, the Spirit's cry, Sonship, and heirship.
- Romans 8:14–17 develops the Spirit's witness, the children's inheritance, suffering with Christ, and shared glory.
- Ephesians 1:3–5 places adoption within God's purpose and the good pleasure of His will.
- Matthew 3:17 and Hebrews 11:2, 5–6 bring the Father's pleasure, faith, good report, and pleasing God into the argument.
- The subsequent chapters develop image and likeness, divine nature, redemption, righteousness, heirship, oneness, and the present reality in Christ.

Faith is therefore neither a passing mood nor a verbal formula. The Aletheia source identifies it as credence that defines the state of God's children. The Scriptures considered here describe that state through Sonship, the Father's purpose, the Spirit's witness, and inheritance through Christ. The following chapters develop the wider argument concerning Design and restoration.

# Chapter 3 — Elohim: Image, Likeness and Human Design
## From faith as state to humanity's design

The preceding chapters have established two foundations. Hebrews 11:1 describes faith as *hypostasis* in relation to things hoped for. The Aletheia leaflet's “Faith toward God” teaching identifies *pistis* as “credence” and states that faith defines the state of God's children, connecting that state with acceptance, true nature, and Sonship.

The leaflet develops true nature through the account of creation, bringing Genesis 1:26–28, 2 Peter 1:4, and Psalm 82:6 into relation. The inquiry therefore moves beyond an inward belief or abstract definition to ask what humanity was made to be and how that design relates to the state of God's children.

Genesis 1:26–28 contributes a distinct foundation: humanity is made in God's image and likeness, blessed, and given a commission. Although the passage does not use *pistis* or explain *hypostasis*, it supplies the creation language through which the wider argument about faith and the state of God's children is developed.

The governing thesis is that **Jesus represents who we are by Design.** He is not presented only as the One who accomplished something on our behalf. His person reveals the design, while His redemptive accomplishment is essential to its restoration. Representation and accomplishment belong together.

## ’Ĕlōhîm: image and likeness

Genesis 1:26 records God's words:

> “And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth.” — Genesis 1:26, KJV

The Hebrew word translated “God” here is *’ĕlōhîm*. The Aletheia leaflet gives the interpretation “plural: Trinity”; this is the source's stated theological reading. The leaflet considers it alongside the verse's language, “Let us make” and “our image.” Its theological significance is developed from the whole declaration and the wider scriptural witness, not from the noun's form in isolation.

The words “image” and “likeness” describe humanity's relationship to the Creator, and the same declaration joins that identity to dominion. Genesis 1:27 confirms the image statement; Genesis 1:28 records God's blessing and commission.

Genesis 1:27 says:

> “So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.” — Genesis 1:27, KJV

The 2015 leaflet places the paraphrase “So God made man like his Maker” beside this verse and connects it with “Man's True Nature” (2 Peter 1:4) and the glory humanity came short of through sin. The leaflet also includes the following wording, which is reproduced here as the Living Bible quotation:

> **Genesis 1:27 (The Living Bible, TLB)**  
> “So God made man like his Maker.  
> Like God did God make man;  
> Man and maid did he make them.”

This wording is retained alongside the KJV, not as a replacement for it. The full quotation is identified with *The Living Bible* (TLB), copyright © 1971 by Tyndale House Ministries. The leaflet separately places the line “Like God did God make man” beside a parenthetical attribution to the Life Application Bible. That source note is preserved as part of the leaflet's presentation; the full quotation above is attributed to the translation actually quoted. Reference: https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+1%3A27&version=TLB.

Genesis 1:28 continues:

> “And God blessed them, and God said unto them, Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth, and subdue it: and have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over every living thing that moveth upon the earth.” — Genesis 1:28, KJV

Blessing and commission are spoken together. God blesses humanity and commands fruitfulness, multiplication, replenishment, subduing, and dominion. Image and likeness must therefore be read with the commission that accompanies them; neither part should be detached from the creation account.

These verses ground the doctrinal synthesis that humanity's identity must be understood in relation to its design and to the One who designed it. They provide the creation foundation for the source's connection between true nature and the state of God's children.

## The design argument in the wider Scripture

The following passages extend the design argument, each contributing a distinct element without losing its own context.

**John 14:12** records Jesus' words:

> “Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that believeth on me, the works that I do shall he do also; and greater works than these shall he do; because I go unto my Father.” — John 14:12, KJV

Jesus connects the works of the believer with His own works and gives His going to the Father as the reason. The promise is not a detached motivational slogan: it concerns what the one who believes on Him shall do. The doctrinal exposition draws out the significance of this connection without confusing that interpretation with the wording of the verse.

**1 John 4:17** says:

> “Herein is our love made perfect, that we may have boldness in the day of judgment: because as he is, so are we in this world.” — 1 John 4:17, KJV

The words “as he is, so are we in this world” are direct and present-tense. Their force must be retained, together with the context John gives: perfected love and boldness in the day of judgment. The design argument considers both the identity statement and the setting in which it appears.

**Psalm 82:6** declares:

> “I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.” — Psalm 82:6, KJV

## Jesus represents humanity's design

The governing doctrinal statement is:

> **Jesus represents who we are by Design.**

The thesis holds two questions together: What did Jesus accomplish for us, and what does He represent? In His person, Jesus reveals the reality toward which humanity was designed. His representation does not displace His redemptive work; redemption is essential to the restoration argument.

The argument is cumulative:

- **Genesis 1:26–28** establishes image, likeness, blessing, fruitfulness, and dominion.
- **Hebrews 1:2–3** presents the Son as heir of all things and the express image of the Father's person.
- **Romans 8:17** identifies believers as children, heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ, while retaining the verse's reference to suffering and shared glory.
- **1 John 4:17** declares, in the context of perfected love and confidence in the day of judgment, “as he is, so are we in this world.”
- **John 17:20–23** records Jesus' prayer concerning the oneness of believers with the Father and the Son.
- **Romans 3:23–24**, **Ephesians 1:7**, and **Colossians 1:14** belong to the connected argument concerning coming short of glory, justification, and redemption through Christ.

No single verse carries the entire doctrine. Genesis supplies the creation foundation; the passages concerning the Son, children and heirs, oneness, glory, justification, and redemption develop the relationship between humanity's design and its representation in Christ.

## Faith, design, and the Father's nature

The design inquiry also considers Jesus' teaching about faith and the Father's nature, developing the conviction that humanity is to operate in the state corresponding to its design.

### Mark 11:20–25 — “Have faith in God”

Jesus' words in Mark 11:22 are:

> “And Jesus answering saith unto them, Have faith in God.” — Mark 11:22, KJV

The setting is the withered fig tree (Mark 11:20–21). Jesus then speaks of addressing the mountain, believing without doubting in the heart, praying in faith, and forgiving when standing in prayer (Mark 11:23–25). Faith is presented in relation to speech, the heart, action, prayer, and forgiveness—not as a detached formula for obtaining whatever one wants.

The Greek phrase is *pistin theou* (πίστιν θεοῦ). *Pistin* is the accusative form of *pistis*, “faith”; *theou* is the genitive form of *theos*, “God.” The familiar English rendering “faith in God” interprets the genitive as indicating the object toward which faith is directed. “Faith of God” reflects the genitive more formally, but the grammatical form by itself does not settle the theological relationship expressed.

Mark 11:22 underscores our design identity and calls us to operate in the state of God because we are God by design. This is the doctrinal interpretation of the verse within the full Scripture framework; it is not presented as a word-for-word lexical gloss of the Greek genitive. The grammatical construction is acknowledged, while the settled doctrinal direction is developed through the connected teaching on *hypostasis*, *pistis*, the state of God's children, and humanity's image and likeness.

The argument is cumulative. Chapter 1 examines *hypostasis* as the foundation of faith as substance or underlying reality; Chapter 2 develops the leaflet's explicit teaching that *pistis*—credence—defines the state of God's children; Genesis 1:26–28 supplies the language of image, likeness, and dominion. Mark 11:22 belongs within this connected argument, not in isolation.

The immediate context also governs the interpretation. Any account of faith as God's state must reckon with Jesus' words about the heart, speech, prayer, and forgiveness. The passage is neither reduced to generic trust nor turned into a promise that every desired result must follow from the force of belief.

### Matthew 5:43–48 — perfection expressed in love

> “Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy. But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you; That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.” — Matthew 5:43–45, KJV

He continues:

> “For if ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? do not even the publicans the same? And if ye salute your brethren only, what do ye more than others? do not even the publicans so? Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect.” — Matthew 5:46–48, KJV

Jesus gives the command to be perfect within His teaching about loving enemies, blessing those who curse, doing good to those who hate, and praying for persecutors. The Father's generosity toward both evil and good is central to the context. Perfection is therefore inseparable from expressing the Father's nature in love.

Matthew 5:48 affirms identity and calls us to operate in the Father's own nature and perfection. Read it alongside 1 John 4:17—“as he is, so are we in this world”—while retaining each passage's setting: love toward enemies in Matthew and perfected love and confidence in the day of judgment in First John.

# Chapter 4 — Ye Are Gods: Psalm 82 and the Testimony of Jesus in John 10
## The statement and the setting

The Aletheia source includes Psalm 82:6 in its teaching on true nature and the state of God's children:

> “I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.” — Psalm 82:6, KJV

The declaration must be allowed to speak with its full force, but verse 6 is not the whole psalm. The next verse says:

> “But ye shall die like men, and fall like one of the princes.” — Psalm 82:7, KJV

Both verses belong to the same passage. A faithful reading cannot quote verse 6 as though verse 7 were absent, nor use verse 7 to erase the declaration in verse 6. The chapter therefore holds the two together and then considers the way Jesus Himself appeals to the writing in John 10.

The governing doctrinal direction is that humanity is God by design, created in the image and likeness of *’ĕlōhîm*. Psalm 82:6 contributes to this teaching; its wording, judgment setting, and Jesus' use of it must all remain in view.

## Psalm 82:1–5 — the judgment scene

The psalm opens:

> “God standeth in the congregation of the mighty; he judgeth among the gods.
>
> How long will ye judge unjustly, and accept the persons of the wicked? Selah.
>
> Defend the poor and fatherless: do justice to the afflicted and needy.
>
> Deliver the poor and needy: rid them out of the hand of the wicked.
>
> They know not, neither will they understand; they walk on in darkness: all the foundations of the earth are out of course.” — Psalm 82:1–5, KJV

The psalm opens with God standing in the congregation and judging among the gods. The charge is explicit: those addressed judge unjustly and show favour to the wicked. They are commanded to defend the poor and fatherless, do justice to the afflicted and needy, and deliver the poor and needy from the wicked.

The psalm describes their failure: they do not know or understand, they walk in darkness, and the foundations of the earth are out of course. Judgment is tied to their conduct and to the consequences of injustice.

The designation “gods” is therefore spoken within a rebuke. The psalm does not present the title as permission for injustice or exemption from accountability. Those addressed answer to God for how they judge and treat the vulnerable.

The source's later use of *dikaiosynē* (δικαιοσύνη) and its explanation of equity as conformity with the nature of God belongs to the wider doctrinal sequence. Chapter 7 develops righteousness and justification. Here, the immediate point is the psalm's own demand for just judgment and protection of those who are vulnerable.

## Psalm 82:6–8 — the declaration and the judgment

The next lines read:

> “I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.
>
> But ye shall die like men, and fall like one of the princes.
>
> Arise, O God, judge the earth: for thou shalt inherit all nations.” — Psalm 82:6–8, KJV

The declaration begins, “I have said,” and is attributed to God: “Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.” The source includes both the designation “gods” and the description “children of the most High” in its teaching; neither part should be omitted.

Verse 7 immediately follows: “But ye shall die like men, and fall like one of the princes.” Verse 8 calls upon God to arise and judge the earth, declaring that He shall inherit all nations. The movement is from the declaration, to judgment upon those addressed, to the appeal for God's judgment over the earth.

Verse 6 must not become a slogan detached from the psalm, just as verse 7 must not be used as though the declaration in verse 6 had never been made. The passage contains both designation and judgment; its force emerges when they are read together.

The judgment setting rules out the conclusion that those called “gods” are beyond God's authority or free to act unjustly. The psalm places them under His judgment. The source's design teaching must be developed with this fact in view, not by suppressing it.

## John 10:30–33 — the charge against Jesus

Jesus' appeal to Psalm 82 occurs in a particular exchange. John records:

> “I and my Father are one.
>
> Then the Jews took up stones again to stone him.
>
> Jesus answered them, Many good works have I shewed you from my Father; for which of those works do ye stone me?
>
> The Jews answered him, saying, For a good work we stone thee not; but for blasphemy; and because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God.” — John 10:30–33, KJV

The exchange begins with Jesus' statement, “I and my Father are one.” His hearers take up stones. When He asks which good work from the Father has prompted their response, they answer that they accuse Him of blasphemy because, being a man, He makes Himself God.
Jesus answers by appealing to the written Scriptures. His statement of oneness, the accusation, His question about the works, and His appeal to what is written form one continuous exchange.

## John 10:34–36 — Jesus invokes the writing

Jesus answers:

> “Jesus answered them, Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?
>
> If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came, and the scripture cannot be broken;
>
> Say ye of him, whom the Father hath sanctified, and sent into the world, Thou blasphemest; because I said, I am the Son of God?” — John 10:34–36, KJV

Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6—“I said, Ye are gods”—then refers to those “unto whom the word of God came” and declares that “the scripture cannot be broken.” He asks why His hearers accuse the One whom the Father sanctified and sent into the world of blasphemy for saying, “I am the Son of God.”

Jesus deliberately brings the written statement into His answer to the accusation and affirms the authority of the Scripture He quotes: it cannot be broken.

Jesus refers to the designation in the writing, those to whom the word of God came, and His identity as the One sanctified and sent by the Father. His argument is not a detached definition of “gods”; it answers a specific charge through Scripture and His relation to the Father.

The source's design argument reads this appeal alongside Genesis 1:26–28 and the wider teaching on true nature and Sonship. In doing so, it must retain the distinction between what John explicitly records and the cumulative doctrinal conclusion drawn across the passages. Jesus' quotation is explicit; the full account of humanity's design is developed through the connected Scripture set.

## John 10:37–38 — the works and mutual indwelling

Jesus continues:

> “If I do not the works of my Father, believe me not.
>
> But if I do, though ye believe not me, believe the works: that ye may know, and believe, that the Father is in me, and I in him.” — John 10:37–38, KJV

The exchange continues beyond the quotation from Psalm 82. Jesus appeals to His Father's works: if He does not do them, they should not believe Him; if He does, they are to believe the works and know that the Father is in Him and He in the Father.

This returns to Jesus' opening statement of oneness in John 10:30. His answer moves through the written Scripture, His identity as the One sent by the Father, and the testimony of the Father's works.

The language of the Father in the Son and the Son in the Father also prepares the reader for John 17:20–23, where Jesus prays concerning the oneness of those who believe. Chapter 9 develops that prayer in its full context. John 10 should not be made to say everything John 17 says, but the two passages belong in the wider inquiry into oneness and glory.

## What the paired passages establish

Psalm 82 and John 10 must be read together while retaining their distinct settings.

Psalm 82 portrays God judging among the gods. It rebukes unjust judgment and failure to defend the vulnerable, declares that those addressed are “gods” and “children of the most High,” and immediately places them under judgment. The psalm closes with an appeal for God to judge the earth.

In John 10, Jesus quotes that declaration in response to the charge of blasphemy. He affirms that Scripture cannot be broken, refers to those to whom the word of God came, and asks why the One sanctified and sent by the Father is accused of blasphemy for saying, “I am the Son of God.” He then appeals to the Father's works and to the mutual indwelling of the Father and the Son.

The paired passages therefore do not allow “Ye are gods” to be dismissed as though Jesus had not invoked it, nor do they allow the psalm's rebuke and judgment to be ignored. The designation, accountability, and appeal to the written word all belong in the interpretation.

The design thesis is developed cumulatively through the full Scripture set:

- **Genesis 1:26–28** supplies the creation language of image, likeness, blessing, and dominion.
- **Psalm 82:6–8** supplies the declaration “Ye are gods,” the identification “children of the most High,” and the surrounding judgment.
- **John 10:30–38** records Jesus' appeal to Psalm 82, His statement that Scripture cannot be broken, and His testimony concerning the Father and the Son.
- **2 Peter 1:4** speaks of becoming partakers of the divine nature; Chapter 5 develops this passage in its own sequence.
- **John 17:20–23** develops Jesus' prayer concerning oneness and glory; Chapter 9 treats that prayer in context.

The doctrinal conclusion rests on the passages considered together, with each retaining its own wording, argument, and setting.

# Chapter 5 — Partakers of the Divine Nature
## From created image to participation in divine nature

The preceding chapters have established the creation foundation in Genesis 1:26–28, the Aletheia teaching that *pistis*—credence—defines the state of God's children, and the relationship of true nature to Sonship. They have also considered Psalm 82:6–7 in its judgment setting and Jesus' appeal to that writing in John 10. Chapter 5 now follows the source's next step in the true-nature argument.

The next passage in the source's true-nature argument is 2 Peter 1:2–8. Its central declaration is that believers may become “partakers of the divine nature.” Peter places this declaration within a larger sequence: knowledge of God and Jesus Christ; divine power and its provision for life and godliness; the call to glory and virtue; precious promises; escape from corruption; and the diligence and qualities that must abound.

Participation in divine nature must therefore be read within Peter's full movement. The promises, escape from corruption, and qualities named in verses 5–8 are not optional additions; they belong to the same passage.

## Knowledge, divine power, and the call

Peter begins:

> “Grace and peace be multiplied unto you through the knowledge of God, and of Jesus our Lord,
>
> According as his divine power hath given unto us all things that pertain unto life and godliness, through the knowledge of him that hath called us to glory and virtue.” — 2 Peter 1:2–3, KJV

Peter connects multiplied grace and peace with the knowledge of God and of Jesus our Lord. He then identifies the source of the provision: God's divine power has given all things pertaining to life and godliness. The call, too, is specific—God has called us to glory and virtue.

Peter begins not with human ability but with what divine power has given. His call is equally definite: it is a call to glory and virtue, received through the knowledge of the One who calls.

Knowledge is central to the passage, but it is not the accumulation of disconnected information. Peter speaks of knowing God and Jesus our Lord, and connects that knowledge with grace and peace and the divine provision for life and godliness.

## The promises and participation in divine nature

Peter continues:

> “Whereby are given unto us exceeding great and precious promises: that by these ye might be partakers of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust.” — 2 Peter 1:4, KJV

The verse holds three elements together:

1. **The promises:** exceeding great and precious promises are given.
2. **Participation:** through these promises, believers may become partakers of the divine nature.
3. **Escape:** believers have escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust.

Participation is related to the promises God has given and is placed alongside escape from corruption. Peter does not present it as an abstract title detached from the life from which believers have escaped.

The Aletheia source places this promise within its teaching on humanity's true nature from creation, connecting the design described in Genesis with Peter's statement about participation in divine nature. Genesis 1:26–28 says that humanity was made in God's image and likeness; 2 Peter 1:4 speaks of becoming partakers of divine nature through the promises. The passages use different wording, and their relationship is the source's doctrinal connection developed across the Scripture argument.

This connection gives substance to the book's treatment of true nature. The source does not separate humanity's creation in God's image and likeness from Peter's promise of participation in divine nature. It reads them together in its account of what God gives through His promises.

## Corruption, deviation, and being brought back on course

Peter's statement about participation in divine nature must be read together with its conclusion: escape from “the corruption that is in the world through lust.” Divine participation and escape from corruption belong to the same sentence.

The Aletheia source connects this argument with Psalm 82:5, which describes the foundations of the earth as “out of course,” and Romans 3:23–24, which speaks of coming short of God's glory and of free justification through redemption in Christ Jesus. In the source's conceptual language, sin is a deviation from true nature, and restoration is described as being brought “back on course.”

The source also explains *dikaiosynē* (δικαιοσύνη) through equity—impartiality to and conformity with the nature of God—and adds the wording “original: back on course.” This is retained as the Aletheia source's explanation, not offered as an exhaustive dictionary definition of the Greek noun. Chapter 7 examines the source's treatment of righteousness and justification in detail.

The connection is cumulative. Psalm 82:5 describes foundations out of course; Romans 3:23–24 speaks of coming short of God's glory and justification through redemption in Christ; 2 Peter 1:4 speaks of participation in divine nature and escape from corruption. The source's “back on course” formulation brings these statements into one conceptual line: restoration concerns a return to the true nature associated with God's design.

This is a doctrinal synthesis across the passages. No single Greek word is made to prove the entire restoration thesis; each text contributes its own statement, and the source's interpretation connects them.

## Participation does not end at verse 4

Peter continues immediately:

> “And beside this, giving all diligence, add to your faith virtue; and to virtue knowledge;
>
> And to knowledge temperance; and to temperance patience; and to patience godliness;
>
> And to godliness brotherly kindness; and to brotherly kindness charity.
>
> For if these things be in you, and abound, they make you that ye shall neither be barren nor unfruitful in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ.” — 2 Peter 1:5–8, KJV

Peter names the qualities to accompany faith: virtue, knowledge, temperance, patience, godliness, brotherly kindness, and charity. He introduces the sequence with the call to give “all diligence.” The Greek noun in verse 5 is σπουδή (*spoudē*), not the related verb σπουδάζω (*spoudazō*). The distinction matters: the text uses the noun here.

Peter's order is deliberate: faith, virtue, knowledge, temperance, patience, godliness, brotherly kindness, and charity. When these qualities are present and abound, he says, they prevent barrenness and unfruitfulness in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ.

The qualities are not a detachable list. Peter connects them to divine provision and participation: those who have received the promises are called to diligence, and the qualities are to be present and increasing. Verse 4 cannot be taught while verses 5–8 are ignored.

Nor should the sequence reverse Peter's opening emphasis. He begins with what divine power has given and what the promises make possible. The qualities are not a human attempt to manufacture divine nature apart from God's provision; they are the diligent and fruitful expression Peter calls for.

Peter's conclusion is relational: fruitfulness occurs “in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ.” The passage begins with knowledge of God and Jesus our Lord (verses 2–3) and returns to knowledge of Christ in verse 8. Knowledge frames the movement from divine provision and promise to participation, diligence, and fruitfulness.

## True nature within the argument of Book I

The Aletheia source develops true nature through the relationship between creation, participation, corruption, and restoration. Genesis 1:26–28 supplies the image-and-likeness foundation. Second Peter 1:2–4 speaks of divine provision, precious promises, participation in divine nature, and escape from corruption; verses 5–8 describe the diligence and qualities that are to abound in the knowledge of Christ.

The source's “back on course” formulation connects 2 Peter 1:2–8 with Psalm 82:5 and Romans 3:23–24. It links what is out of course and what has fallen short of glory with the restoration developed through righteousness and redemption in Christ. Chapter 7 examines that teaching directly; this chapter establishes Peter's place in the argument.

Participation in divine nature belongs to humanity's designed state and to the restoration accomplished through Christ. This conclusion arises from the source's connection between creation and true nature and from Peter's full sequence, not from verse 4 in isolation.

Participation in divine nature does not make Peter's instructions unnecessary. The same passage calls for diligence, virtue, knowledge, temperance, patience, godliness, brotherly kindness, and charity, all of which are to be present and abound. The chapter thus holds together God's provision, the promises, escape from corruption, and the fruitful life Peter describes.

Book I's governing convictions remain that **faith is our state by design** and **Jesus represents who we are by Design**. This chapter has developed the promise of participation in divine nature and the source's account of true nature and restoration. The next chapter asks why Jesus came and examines humanity's coming short of glory in relation to His work.

# Chapter 6 — Why Did Jesus Come? The Glory We Came Short Of
## The question follows the design

The preceding chapters have established the foundation of faith, the Aletheia teaching that *pistis*—credence—defines the state of God's children, humanity's creation in God's image and likeness, Psalm 82:6–7 in its judgment context, and Peter's account of participation in divine nature.

The Aletheia leaflet now asks a direct question: **Why did Jesus come?**

The leaflet places the question after Genesis 1:26–27 and its statement that humanity was made like its Maker. It then cites Romans 3:23–24, interpreting “come short of the glory of God” as “deviation from true nature,” and points to 1 Corinthians 11:7 for the description of man as the image and glory of God. The sequence places Jesus' coming within the source's argument about humanity's design and the glory from which humanity has come short.

The chapter follows three connected matters:

1. **Design:** humanity was made in God's image and likeness.
2. **The human condition:** humanity sinned and came short of God's glory; the Aletheia source interprets this as deviation from true nature.
3. **God's action in Christ:** justification is given freely by grace through redemption in Christ Jesus.

The Aletheia source's interpretation is retained with its doctrinal force. Accurate exposition also makes clear which wording belongs to Scripture, which is the source's explanatory gloss, and which is the wider synthesis developed from the passages.

## The creation statement and the glory of humanity

Genesis 1:26 records God's declaration:

> “And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth.” — Genesis 1:26, KJV

Genesis 1:27 continues:

> “So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.” — Genesis 1:27, KJV

The Aletheia leaflet places the explanatory wording “So God made man like his Maker” beside verse 27. It connects this statement with “Man's True Nature” (2 Peter 1:4) and “the glory he came short of through sin” (Psalm 82:6). The leaflet also supplies the following Living Bible quotation:

> **Genesis 1:27 (The Living Bible, TLB)**  
> “So God made man like his Maker.  
> Like God did God make man;  
> Man and maid did he make them.”

This quotation is retained alongside, not in place of, the KJV. The leaflet separately attributes the line “Like God did God make man” to the Life Application Bible; that note is distinct from the full TLB quotation reproduced here.

Genesis 1:26–27 establishes the creation foundation: image and likeness. The passage itself also identifies male and female as included in the humanity God created. The wider creation account continues in verse 28 with God's blessing and commission to be fruitful, multiply, replenish the earth, subdue it, and have dominion. The image statement should not be detached from the creation declaration and commission in which it appears.

Chapter 5 developed the source's connection between this creation account and 2 Peter 1:4. Here, Genesis establishes the design setting for the leaflet's question about why Jesus came.

## “Come short of the glory”: the source's interpretation

The leaflet quotes Romans 3:23–24:

> “For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God; Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus.” — Romans 3:23–24, KJV

The source places its explanatory gloss directly after “the glory of God”:

> **“deviation from true nature: Man the express image and Glory of God: 1Co. 11:7”**

That is the Aletheia teaching's interpretation and must be taught as such. The source is not merely repeating the phrase “come short of the glory”; it is explaining that coming short in relation to humanity's true nature, and it directs the reader to 1 Corinthians 11:7.

Romans 3:23–24 must be read as one movement. Verse 23 states that all have sinned and come short of God's glory; verse 24 immediately declares justification freely by grace through the redemption in Christ Jesus. The source's argument does not stop at deviation. It moves directly to justification and redemption.

“Deviation from true nature” is the source's doctrinal gloss on “come short of the glory of God.” This chapter retains and develops that interpretation through the source's Scripture references. It neither presents the gloss as a word-for-word lexical translation of the Greek phrase nor reduces the source's teaching to speculation.

## Man: the image and glory of God

The leaflet's reference is 1 Corinthians 11:7:

> “For a man indeed ought not to cover his head, forasmuch as he is the image and glory of God: but the woman is the glory of the man.” — 1 Corinthians 11:7, KJV

Paul makes this statement within his discussion of headship, head coverings, and conduct in the assembly (1 Corinthians 11:2–16). Verse 7 has two clauses: the man is described as the image and glory of God, and the woman as the glory of the man. The source draws particular attention to the first clause in relation to Romans 3:23.

The Aletheia connection brings three statements together: Romans speaks of coming short of God's glory; 1 Corinthians 11:7 describes man as the image and glory of God; Genesis 1:26–27 establishes humanity's creation in God's image and likeness. The source reads them together in its teaching that the glory humanity came short of is connected with true nature and created design.

The connection must not erase the context of 1 Corinthians 11, nor should the source's use of the image-and-glory clause be ignored because the surrounding passage addresses headship and covering. The exposition retains the whole setting while explaining the clause the leaflet brings into its argument.

## The glory language in the resurrection context

The Scripture discussion also includes 1 Corinthians 15:41. This verse belongs to Paul's extended answer about the resurrection of the dead, beginning with the question, “How are the dead raised up? and with what body do they come?” (1 Corinthians 15:35). Paul uses the differences between the glory of the sun, moon, and stars to explain that there are differences of glory. He then applies the comparison to the resurrection of the dead (verses 42–49).

> “There is one glory of the sun, and another glory of the moon, and another glory of the stars: for one star differeth from another star in glory.” — 1 Corinthians 15:41, KJV

The verse speaks explicitly of differing glories among the created lights. Its immediate function is Paul's explanation of resurrection and the differing glory of the bodies he describes. It cannot, by itself, be made to say that humanity is the image and glory of God; that statement appears in 1 Corinthians 11:7.

First Corinthians 15:41 belongs in the wider Scripture cluster because Paul uses “glory” within his account of the resurrection body's transformation. The surrounding argument contrasts corruption with incorruption, dishonour with glory, weakness with power, and the natural body with the spiritual body (1 Corinthians 15:42–44). Paul then contrasts the first man, Adam, with the second man, the Lord from heaven (verses 45–49).

This passage contributes to the book's wider examination of glory, humanity, and Christ. Its context must remain visible: Paul is explaining resurrection and the nature of the body raised. The connection to the design-and-restoration thesis is developed across the Scripture set, not extracted from verse 41 in isolation.

## Why Jesus came: righteousness manifested and redemption accomplished

Romans 3:21–26 supplies the fuller movement behind the leaflet's question. Paul first describes the manifestation of God's righteousness, then the universal condition of sin, justification through redemption, and Christ's role in the declaration of God's righteousness. Verses 23–24 belong within this complete argument.

### Righteousness manifested

> “But now the righteousness of God without the law is manifested, being witnessed by the law and the prophets;
>
> Even the righteousness of God which is by faith of Jesus Christ unto all and upon all them that believe: for there is no difference.” — Romans 3:21–22, KJV

Paul says that God's righteousness has been manifested apart from the law, while being witnessed by the law and the prophets. He then speaks of the righteousness of God by faith of Jesus Christ unto all and upon all who believe, “for there is no difference.”

Paul sets the discussion within God's manifested righteousness. The universal need addressed in the following verses must be read in that light.

### Sin, coming short, and free justification

> “For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God;
>
> Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus.” — Romans 3:23–24, KJV

Paul's statement is universal: all have sinned and come short of God's glory. Verse 24 then announces justification freely by grace through redemption in Christ Jesus. The Aletheia source interprets this coming short as deviation from true nature and connects the glory with humanity as the image and glory of God.

“Freely” is essential to the source's question. The leaflet asks, “Has man been justified? Yes! And FREELY!” It gives *apolutrōsis* (ἀπολύτρωσις) the gloss “Redemption; Ransom in full.” The declaration emphasizes that justification is not a human achievement or a payment made by the recipient: Romans 3:24 says it is freely by grace through redemption in Christ Jesus.

Chapter 7 develops *dikaioō*, *dikaiosynē*, and *apolutrōsis* in detail. Here, they remain within Paul's argument: justification is freely given by grace, and redemption is in Christ Jesus.

### Christ, propitiation, and the declaration of God's righteousness

> “Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God;
>
> To declare, I say, at this time his righteousness: that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus.” — Romans 3:25–26, KJV

Paul identifies Christ as the One God set forth as a propitiation through faith in His blood. This declares God's righteousness. The passage concludes that God is just and the justifier of the one who believes in Jesus.

This completes the movement of Romans 3:21–26:

- God's righteousness is manifested and witnessed by the law and the prophets.
- All have sinned and come short of God's glory.
- Believers are justified freely by grace through the redemption in Christ Jesus.
- Christ is set forth as propitiation through faith in His blood.
- God's righteousness is declared, and He is shown to be just and the justifier of the one who believes in Jesus.

Why did Jesus come? In the leaflet's argument, His coming addresses humanity's deviation from true nature and the glory humanity came short of. Romans 3 explains God's provision of free justification and redemption in Him. The answer must include both the human condition and God's action; it cannot reduce His coming to a moral example or to forgiveness detached from righteousness, blood, propitiation, and redemption.

## Redemption in the wider witness

The wider Scripture framework includes Ephesians 1:7 and Colossians 1:13–14. These passages add their own statements to the redemption argument.

> “In whom we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins, according to the riches of his grace.” — Ephesians 1:7, KJV

Ephesians locates redemption in Christ, identifies His blood and the forgiveness of sins, and relates both to the riches of His grace. It states what believers have in Him.

> “Who hath delivered us from the power of darkness, and hath translated us into the kingdom of his dear Son:
>
> In whom we have redemption through his blood, even the forgiveness of sins.” — Colossians 1:13–14, KJV

Colossians places redemption within a larger movement: deliverance from the power of darkness and translation into the kingdom of God's dear Son. Verse 14 identifies redemption through His blood and forgiveness of sins. The clauses belong together.

These passages explicitly speak of redemption, blood, forgiveness, deliverance, and translation into the Son's kingdom. Their relation to the source's wider restoration thesis is a synthesis across the passages; neither Ephesians 1:7 nor Colossians 1:13–14 independently states every element of the “back on course” formulation.

## Jesus represents who we are by Design

The governing doctrinal thesis is that **Jesus represents who we are by Design**. This teaching is developed alongside, and never in place of, the redemption He accomplished.

Genesis 1:26–27 establishes humanity's creation in God's image and likeness. Second Peter 1:4 speaks of participation in divine nature. Galatians 4:5–7 and Romans 8:15–17 develop adoption, sonship, and heirship. Hebrews 1:2–3 presents the Son as heir of all things and the express image of the Father's person. These passages contribute distinct parts of the cumulative argument: creation, divine nature, sonship, inheritance, and the Son's representation.

The source's question about the glory humanity came short of belongs within this wider design setting. Sin is understood as falling short of the state for which humanity was designed; justification and redemption restore humanity to that design. This is the doctrinal synthesis developed through the source and the connected Scriptures.

Representation does not cancel redemption. Jesus does not merely display an ideal while His redemptive work is set aside; nor does redemption erase what His person reveals about humanity's design. The two belong together:

**Jesus represents who we are by Design, and He accomplished the redemption through which we are restored to that design.**

The source's “back on course” language belongs to this restoration argument. Chapter 7 develops the leaflet's treatment of justification and righteousness and its declaration that redemption is “ransom in full.” This chapter has established why those terms matter: the question begins with humanity's created nature and the glory from which humanity came short, then moves to God's free justification and redemption in Christ.

## The wider identity witness: love, likeness, and perfection

Two further passages in the book's Scripture framework help articulate the relationship between Christ and humanity's designed identity. They must be read in their own contexts.

Jesus says in Matthew 5:48:

> “Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect.” — Matthew 5:48, KJV

This command concludes Matthew 5:43–48. Jesus has been teaching His hearers to love their enemies, bless those who curse them, do good to those who hate them, and pray for those who persecute them. He points to the Father's generosity, who makes His sun rise on the evil and the good and sends rain on the just and the unjust. The command to be perfect as the Father is perfect belongs to that teaching about love. The doctrinal reading treats this as an affirmation of the Father's nature as the pattern for humanity, without detaching perfection from the love Jesus commands.

First John 4:17 says:

> “Herein is our love made perfect, that we may have boldness in the day of judgment: because as he is, so are we in this world.” — 1 John 4:17, KJV

John's statement “as he is, so are we in this world” is direct and present. Its context is also explicit: love made perfect and boldness in the day of judgment. This statement is considered together with Matthew 5:48 in the identity-and-likeness argument. The connection does not remove either passage from its context. Matthew speaks of the Father's perfection expressed in love, including love of enemies; First John speaks of perfected love and confidence in the day of judgment.

These passages contribute to the wider thesis that Jesus represents who we are by Design, but they do not replace the central argument of Romans 3. The question of why Jesus came still requires the full account of sin, coming short of glory, free justification, propitiation, and redemption.

## The answer held together

The Aletheia source places “Why did Jesus come?” immediately after its account of humanity made like its Maker. It interprets coming short of God's glory as deviation from true nature, points to 1 Corinthians 11:7's description of man as the image and glory of God, and then turns to Romans 3:23–24: justification is free, by grace, through redemption in Christ Jesus.

The answer must preserve the sequence the source joins:

- **Creation:** humanity is made in God's image and likeness (Genesis 1:26–27).
- **True nature:** the source connects this design with participation in divine nature (2 Peter 1:4).
- **Deviation:** the source interprets coming short of God's glory as deviation from true nature (Romans 3:23; 1 Corinthians 11:7).
- **Justification:** humanity is justified freely by God's grace (Romans 3:24).
- **Redemption:** redemption is in Christ Jesus, through His blood (Romans 3:24–26; Ephesians 1:7; Colossians 1:13–14).
- **Representation:** Jesus represents who humanity is by Design, while His redemptive accomplishment remains essential to restoration.

The question cannot be answered with representation alone or with redemption detached from the design from which the argument begins. Christ's person and accomplishment belong together: the design identifies what is being restored, and His redemptive work reveals God's provision in Christ.

# Chapter 7 — Justification, Righteousness and Redemption in Christ
## The response to deviation: redemption and righteousness

Chapter 6 followed the Aletheia source's question, “Why did Jesus come?” It developed the source's interpretation of coming short of God's glory as deviation from true nature and examined Romans 3:23–24: all have sinned and come short of God's glory, and are justified freely by grace through the redemption in Christ Jesus.

This chapter examines the terms through which the source describes God's response. Three Greek terms are central; their relationship matters, but they must remain distinct:

- *Dikaioō* (δικαιόω): the verb associated with justification. The leaflet's printed gloss is “render just or innocent (free from evil or guilt).”
- *Dikaiosynē* (δικαιοσύνη): the related noun the source connects with righteousness/justification, equity, impartiality, and conformity with God's nature; it adds the phrase “original: back on course.”
- *Apolutrōsis* (ἀπολύτρωσις): redemption, which the leaflet glosses “ransom in full.”

The source's wording is preserved throughout. The Aletheia teaching developed here is that justification restores humanity to its designed state and that redemption is complete ransom with restoration to original design. These conclusions belong together, but they are not interchangeable lexical definitions. Keeping the distinction clear allows the source's teaching to be stated fully without making a single word carry the entire doctrinal argument.

## *Dikaioō*: justified freely

In Romans 3:24, the Greek form is δικαιούμενοι (*dikaioumenoi*), from δικαιόω (*dikaioō*, Strong's G1344). It is a present middle/passive participial form, nominative masculine plural, with passive force in this clause; it is commonly rendered “being justified.” Paul places it in the declaration that justification is free, by grace, through redemption in Christ Jesus. The form must be read within Romans 3:21–26, including the preceding statement about sin and coming short of glory.

The full G1344 entry supplied for this study is reproduced here, with Strong's and Thayer's material distinguished:

> **δικαιόω — STRONG'S NUMBER: G1344**  
> *dikaioō*; from 1342; to render (i.e. show or regard as) just or innocent: — free, justify(-ier), be righteous.  
> **AV (40):** justify 37, be freed 1, be righteous 1, justifier 1.  
> **Thayer's Greek Lexicon — G1344:**  
> 1. to render righteous or such he ought to be;  
> 2. to show, exhibit, evince, one to be righteous, such as he is and wishes himself to be considered;  
> 3. to declare, pronounce, one to be just, righteous, or such as he ought to be.

The Aletheia leaflet's printed gloss is “render just or innocent (free from evil or guilt).” That wording remains the source's teaching; the fuller entry expands the lexical discussion. Thayer's qualification is also important: he describes the first sense, “to render righteous,” as extremely rare, if not altogether doubtful. He then distinguishes showing or exhibiting someone to be righteous from declaring or pronouncing someone just. In the Pauline technical use, he lists Romans 3:24 under the gracious declaration of righteousness or acceptance.

The complete entry must be retained, including its first definition and Thayer's qualification. The doctrinal conclusion, however, develops through the full Scripture framework; it does not rest on one lexical sense alone.

### Justification and the glory humanity came short of

Romans 3:23–24 (KJV) must be read and quoted as one continuous movement:

> “For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God; Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus:”

Verse 23 identifies sin and humanity's coming short of God's glory. Verse 24 declares that justification is given freely by grace through redemption in Christ Jesus. The Aletheia source interprets coming short of glory as “deviation from true nature” and connects that glory with humanity's image and likeness in Genesis 1:26–27 and 1 Corinthians 11:7.

The doctrinal argument developed here is that humanity fell short of the glory belonging to its designed identity. To come short of a glory presupposes a real relation to it. The glory humanity came short of, in this argument, belongs to humanity's designed identity as made in the image and likeness of *Elohim*. Justification is therefore not treated in this Aletheia design argument as merely a change in legal label. It is God's gracious act of rendering us just or righteous—such as we ought to be—and restoring us to the state for which humanity was made.

This is the doctrinal synthesis developed from Romans 3, Genesis 1, 1 Corinthians 15, and the leaflet's teaching about true nature and glory. The lexical range of *dikaioō* remains as presented above; the restoration-to-design conclusion is developed through the connected Scripture argument.

The source asks emphatically: **Has man been justified? Yes! And FREELY!** Romans 3:24 grounds the answer: justification is by grace, through redemption in Christ Jesus. It is neither a wage earned through conduct nor a human achievement added to Christ's work.

### Glory and identity: 1 Corinthians 15:41

Paul writes:

> “There is one glory of the sun, and another glory of the moon, and another glory of the stars: for one star differeth from another star in glory.” — 1 Corinthians 15:41, KJV

The immediate subject of 1 Corinthians 15 is the resurrection of the dead and the nature of the resurrection body. Paul uses the differing glories of the sun, moon, and stars as part of his explanation of the difference between the body that is sown and the body that is raised. The following verses develop the contrasts between corruption and incorruption, dishonour and glory, weakness and power, and the natural body and the spiritual body (1 Corinthians 15:42–44).

The verse explicitly says that the heavenly lights differ in glory. The doctrinal inference developed in this book is that a thing is identified by its glory: glory expresses its nature and distinction. Within the wider argument about humanity's creation in God's image and likeness, this contributes to the teaching that humanity came short of the glory belonging to its designed identity.

This is an inference drawn across passages, not a claim that 1 Corinthians 15:41 is directly about humanity's creation in God's image. Genesis 1:26–27 supplies that foundation. The Aletheia source connects the texts in its account of true nature, glory, and deviation.

### Redemption: *apolutrōsis*, ransom in full

Romans 3:24 connects justification freely by grace with redemption in Christ Jesus. The Aletheia leaflet gives the Greek term ἀπολύτρωσις (*apolutrōsis*, G629) and the gloss:

> **“Redemption; Ransom in full.”**

The source's phrase “ransom in full” is retained exactly: there is no unpaid remainder in the ransom it describes. The book develops this as complete ransom and restoration to original design. Redemption is not an incomplete transaction that leaves humanity permanently beneath its intended state. In this argument, redemption and restoration belong together: the ransom is complete, and its purpose and result are humanity's return to the state for which it was made.

Lexically, Strong's G629 derives *apolutrōsis* from G575 (*apo*) and G3083 (*lytron*, ransom). Its gloss includes ransom in full, figuratively “riddance,” or Christian salvation. Thayer describes release effected by payment of ransom, including redemption, deliverance, or liberation procured by ransom.

These details support the lexical ideas of ransom, release, and deliverance. “Restoration to original design” is the doctrinal meaning and result developed through the Aletheia Scripture framework; it is not a literal translation of the Greek components. That framework includes Genesis 1:26–28, Romans 3:23–24, 2 Corinthians 5:17–21, Ephesians 1:7, Colossians 1:13–14, and 2 Peter 1:4.

### Justification is not earned

Romans 3:27–28 continues:

> “Where is boasting then? It is excluded. By what law? of works? Nay: but by the law of faith. Therefore we conclude that a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law.” — Romans 3:27–28, KJV

Paul excludes boasting and concludes that a person is justified by faith without the deeds of the law. This connects with the Aletheia leaflet's teaching on *pistis*—credence—and the state of God's children. Justification is not earned as a wage: it is freely given by grace and received through faith. The source's “ransom in full” wording reinforces that personal merit does not complete redemption.

## You are the *dikaiosynē* of God in Christ

The source moves from free justification and redemption to the declaration in 2 Corinthians 5:21. Paul's sequence must be followed: reconciliation, the new creature, and the righteousness of God in Christ.

### Reconciliation and the new creature

> “Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new. And all things are of God, who hath reconciled us to himself by Jesus Christ, and hath given to us the ministry of reconciliation; To wit, that God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself, not imputing their trespasses unto them; and hath committed unto us the word of reconciliation.” — 2 Corinthians 5:17–19, KJV

Paul declares that the person in Christ is a new creature. He then identifies God as the source of reconciliation: God reconciled us to Himself through Jesus Christ and entrusted to us the ministry and word of reconciliation. God was in Christ, reconciling the world to Himself and not imputing their trespasses unto them.

The new creature and reconciliation belong together. Paul's declaration about righteousness in verse 21 must remain connected to the reconciliation described in verses 17–19.

### Made the righteousness of God in Him

> “Now then we are ambassadors for Christ, as though God did beseech you by us: we pray you in Christ’s stead, be ye reconciled to God. For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him.” — 2 Corinthians 5:20–21, KJV

Paul describes the ministry of Christ's ambassadors and their appeal, “be ye reconciled to God.” He then declares that God made Christ, who knew no sin, to be sin for us, so that we might be made the righteousness of God in Him.

The Aletheia teaching emphasizes the present force of this declaration:

> **“So right now, You’re the *dikaiosynē* of God in Christ.”**

The leaflet explains *dikaiosynē* (δικαιοσύνη) as righteousness/justification; equity; impartiality to and conformity with God's nature; “original: back on course.” This source formulation is retained. The declaration is not presented as an aspiration deferred to a future achievement; it is spoken of the believer **in Christ**. The words “in Him” are essential.

This connects with the Aletheia source's account of *pistis* as credence and as the state of God's children. The new creature is in Christ, and the righteousness of God is in Him. The declaration is present-tense, yet remains located in Christ and within Paul's account of Christ's sin-bearing and the ministry of reconciliation.

## The three terms and their relationship

The three Greek terms belong to one doctrinal movement, but they are not synonyms:

| Term | Source's wording | Place in the argument |

The source's “back on course” language and “ransom in full” gloss contribute to the book's doctrinal conclusion that justification and redemption restore humanity to its designed state.

The lexical entries establish the meanings and usage recorded by their respective sources. The complete restoration-to-design doctrine is developed from the Aletheia teaching and the connected Scriptures. This distinction strengthens the exposition: it preserves the source's teaching without attributing the entire doctrinal conclusion to a single Greek word.

## The answer in the full Scripture movement

The Aletheia source's question, “Has man been justified?” receives its emphatic answer: **Yes! And FREELY!** Romans 3:23–24 identifies the condition and the divine response. Humanity has sinned and come short of God's glory; justification is freely given by grace through the redemption in Christ Jesus.

The source glosses *dikaioō* as rendering just or innocent, free from evil or guilt. The full G1344 entry adds Strong's and Thayer's material, including the definitions concerning rendering righteous and declaring or pronouncing someone just. The source connects *dikaiosynē* with equity and conformity to God's nature—“back on course”—and glosses *apolutrōsis* as “ransom in full.”

The doctrinal conclusion is that justification restores humanity to its designed state and redemption is complete ransom with restoration to original design. The conclusion is developed through Genesis 1:26–28, Romans 3:21–28, 1 Corinthians 11:7 and 15:41, 2 Peter 1:4, Ephesians 1:7, Colossians 1:13–14, and 2 Corinthians 5:17–21.

Book I's governing thesis remains: **Jesus represents who we are by Design.** He also accomplished the redemption through which humanity is restored to that design. Representation and redemptive accomplishment must remain together: the design identifies what humanity was made to be, while justification and redemption describe God's gracious action in Christ to accomplish restoration.

# Chapter 8 — The Son and the Heir: Our Inheritance in Christ
## From righteousness in Christ to sonship and heirship

The preceding chapters have developed humanity's design, the Father's nature, the glory humanity came short of, and restoration through redemption and justification. This chapter brings those themes together in the Son and in the inheritance given to God's children.

The governing doctrinal direction is clear: **Jesus represents who we are by Design.** He is the One who accomplished redemption for us and the Son in whom our designed identity is represented and revealed. His sonship, image, glory, and heirship are central to understanding the sonship and inheritance believers receive in Him.

The relationship must be stated accurately. Jesus is the appointed Son and heir of all things; believers do not replace Him or take His unique place. Scripture instead describes what is given through Him: adoption as sons, the Spirit of the Son, the status of God's children, and heirship with Christ. His unique identity and work remain the foundation of the inheritance shared with us.

The chapter follows two movements. Hebrews presents the Son—appointed heir, agent of creation, brightness of glory and express image, the One who purged sins and was exalted. Galatians and Romans then explain the sonship and heirship believers receive through Christ. Together these passages develop the thesis that Jesus represents who we are by Design and that His redemptive accomplishment is essential to our restoration.

## The Son: appointed heir of all things

Hebrews opens by contrasting God's former speech through the prophets with His speech in these last days through the Son:

> “God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets,
>
> Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son, whom he hath appointed heir of all things, by whom also he made the worlds.” — Hebrews 1:1–2, KJV

The opening identifies the Son as God's means of speaking in these last days and makes two declarations about Him: God appointed Him heir of all things, and through Him He made the worlds. Heirship is not an incidental title added later; it belongs to Hebrews' opening description of the Son and His relation to all things.

“Heir of all things” must be read within the whole chapter. The Son is not presented as one heir among others: He is the appointed heir, and the worlds were made by Him. The later passages about believers becoming heirs through Christ must be understood in relation to this unique Son and appointed heir.

## The brightness of glory and the express image

Hebrews 1:3–4 continues:

> “Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power, when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high;
>
> Being made so much better than the angels, as he hath by inheritance obtained a more excellent name than they.” — Hebrews 1:3–4, KJV

Hebrews describes the Son as the brightness of God's glory, the express image of His person, the One who upholds all things by His powerful word, the One who purged our sins, the One seated at the right hand of the Majesty on high, and the One who inherited a more excellent name than the angels.

These descriptions belong together. Hebrews holds the Son's identity, sustaining power, redemptive accomplishment, exaltation, and inheritance in one portrait. “Express image” must be read alongside “purged our sins”; His accomplishment cannot be separated from the identity the passage declares.

The creation-and-representation argument now comes into focus. Genesis 1:26–27 says humanity was made in God's image and likeness; Hebrews 1:3 describes the Son as the express image of God's person; and 2 Corinthians 4:4 calls Christ the image of God. Each statement retains its own wording and context. Together they contribute to the book's cumulative argument that Jesus reveals and represents the divine nature and the identity humanity was designed to bear.

The passages are not interchangeable, and the Son's unique place is not erased. Hebrews identifies Him as the Son, the appointed heir of all things, the express image, and the One who purged our sins. The representation thesis draws on who He is, what He has done, and the Scriptures that identify believers as sons and heirs through Him.

## The Son in the Father's declaration: Hebrews 1:5–8

Hebrews continues by distinguishing the Son from the angels and presenting the scriptural declarations concerning Him:

> “For unto which of the angels said he at any time, Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee? And again, I will be to him a Father, and he shall be to me a Son?
>
> And again, when he bringeth in the firstbegotten into the world, he saith, And let all the angels of God worship him.
>
> And of the angels he saith, Who maketh his angels spirits, and his ministers a flame of fire.
>
> But unto the Son he saith, Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever: a sceptre of righteousness is the sceptre of thy kingdom.” — Hebrews 1:5–8, KJV

The passage asks which angel was ever addressed as the Son with the words “Thou art my Son.” It records the Father's declaration, the command for the angels to worship the firstbegotten, the description of angels as spirits and ministers, and finally the direct address to the Son concerning His throne and righteous sceptre.

Hebrews 1:8 remains because it is part of this scriptural portrait. It directly addresses the Son, declares His throne everlasting, and describes His kingdom through the sceptre of righteousness. It contributes to the portrayal of His identity and reign.

The former leaflet question, “According to Hebrews 1:8, what does that make you?”, remains abandoned. It is neither an unresolved doctrinal question nor an exercise for the reader, and it is not a required inference in this chapter. The verse itself remains in the biblical portrait of the Son; the abandoned question is not revived.

Hebrews 1:1–8 presents a continuous portrait: God speaks through the Son; appoints Him heir of all things; makes the worlds through Him; and declares Him the brightness of His glory and express image. The Son purges sins and is exalted, while the Father's declarations distinguish Him from the angels and speak of His Sonship, throne, and righteous kingdom. This is the portrait within which our sonship and heirship through Him must be understood.

## From servants to sons: Galatians 4:1–7

The Aletheia source directs the reader to Galatians 4:5–7 and asks, “Who is heir of God?” Paul's full sequence supplies the answer; it should not be reduced to the final phrase alone.

Paul begins with an heir who, while a child, does not differ from a servant, though he is lord of all; he remains under guardians and stewards until the time appointed by the father (Galatians 4:1–2). Paul applies this illustration to those in bondage under the elements of the world (verses 3–4), before reaching the central declaration:

> “To redeem them that were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons.
>
> And because ye are sons, God hath sent forth the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, Abba, Father.
>
> Wherefore thou art no more a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ.” — Galatians 4:5–7, KJV

Paul's sequence is explicit:

1. Christ redeems those who were under the law.
2. The purpose stated is that we might receive the adoption of sons.
3. Because we are sons, God sends the Spirit of His Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba, Father.”
4. The conclusion is that we are no more servants, but sons.
5. If sons, then heirs of God through Christ.

The order matters: redemption and adoption are connected, and the Spirit of the Son is sent because we are sons. Paul is not merely saying that a servant has received a new task. He declares a change of status: no more a servant, but a son. Heirship follows sonship, and it is “through Christ.”

The author describes this as natural heirship: because we are sons, heirship follows from sonship. Paul's wording is “if a son, then an heir of God through Christ.” Here, “natural” expresses the consequence of sonship; it does not suggest that heirship is independent of Christ. Paul's statement explicitly says “through Christ.”

## Children, heirs, and joint-heirs with Christ: Romans 8:14–17

Romans 8 develops the same relationship through the Spirit's witness:

> “For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God.
>
> For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father.
>
> The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God:
>
> And if children, then heirs; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ; if so be that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together.” — Romans 8:14–17, KJV

Paul identifies those led by God's Spirit as sons of God. He contrasts the Spirit of adoption with a spirit of bondage and fear, then says that the Spirit bears witness with our spirit that we are God's children. From there he draws the inheritance conclusion: if children, then heirs—heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ.

Paul connects joint-heirship with suffering with Christ and being glorified together. Inheritance must therefore be read within the passage's full account of the Spirit, adoption, suffering, and shared glory.

Galatians 4 and Romans 8 reinforce one another but make distinct contributions. Galatians emphasizes redemption from under the law, adoption, the Spirit of the Son, and the change from servant to son and heir. Romans emphasizes being led by the Spirit, the Spirit's witness, and the relationship among children, heirs, joint-heirship, suffering, and glory.

Together they answer the source's question: **Who is heir of God?** The sons and children of God are heirs through Christ. This is not inheritance apart from the Son; it is inheritance through Him and joint-heirship with Him.

## The righteousness of God in Him

Second Corinthians 5:17–21 develops the themes of the new creature, reconciliation, and righteousness in Christ. Its central declaration is:

> “For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him.” — 2 Corinthians 5:21, KJV

The phrase **“in him”** is essential. Righteousness is not detached from Christ; Paul places the declaration within his account of the new creature and the ministry of reconciliation (2 Corinthians 5:17–20).

This is central to the representation argument. The Son represents who we are by design, and our identity as sons, heirs, and the righteousness of God is located in Him. The verse itself also names Christ's accomplishment: God acted through the One who knew no sin. Neither His accomplishment nor the identity represented in Him should be separated from the other.

## As He is, so are we in this world

First John 4:17 states:

> “Herein is our love made perfect, that we may have boldness in the day of judgment: because as he is, so are we in this world.” — 1 John 4:17, KJV

The words “as he is, so are we in this world” are direct and present-tense. John places them within his teaching about perfected love and boldness in the day of judgment. The identity statement must be read with that context.

Within the doctrinal framework of this book, the statement underscores that the identity represented in the Son is not postponed to a future age: it speaks of who we are in this world. Read with Galatians 4:5–7, Romans 8:14–17, Hebrews 1:2–3, and 2 Corinthians 5:21, it contributes to the representation thesis:

- The Son is the express image of the Father's person.
- Through Christ, we receive adoption as sons.
- Because we are sons, the Spirit of the Son is sent into our hearts.
- If children, we are heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ.
- We are made the righteousness of God in Him.
- “As he is, so are we in this world.”

The statements contribute distinct parts of the argument. They are not collapsed into one another, and their immediate contexts remain part of the exposition.

## The Father's purpose and the representation of design

The Father's will and pleasure is for His sons to live in the state corresponding to their design. This includes sonship and heirship, participation in divine nature, and the identity represented in Christ.

The argument has been built through the book's connected passages:

- **Genesis 1:26–28:** humanity is made in God's image and likeness and receives the blessing and commission.
- **2 Peter 1:4:** believers may become partakers of divine nature through the precious promises.
- **Hebrews 1:1–8:** the Son is appointed heir of all things, the express image of the Father's person, the One who purged our sins, and the Son addressed in the declarations about His throne and kingdom.
- **Galatians 4:5–7:** redemption, adoption, sonship, and heirship through Christ.
- **Romans 8:14–17:** the Spirit's witness, children of God, heirs, joint-heirs with Christ, suffering, and shared glory.
- **2 Corinthians 5:21:** the righteousness of God in Christ.
- **1 John 4:17:** “as he is, so are we in this world,” within the context of perfected love and confidence in the day of judgment.

The governing thesis is not that believers displace the Son. Jesus represents who we are by Design, and through Him we receive the sonship and inheritance Scripture describes. His unique place as Son and appointed heir remains foundational, and His redemptive accomplishment remains essential. The Father's purpose for His sons is understood through the identity and inheritance given in Christ.

## The chapter's conclusion

Jesus is the Son, the appointed heir of all things, the brightness of God's glory, and the express image of His person. He upholds all things and by Himself purged our sins. Hebrews 1 presents His identity and accomplishment together.

Through Christ, we receive adoption as sons. Because we are sons, God sends the Spirit of His Son into our hearts. We are no more servants, but sons—and if sons, heirs of God through Christ. The Spirit bears witness that we are God's children; and if children, heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ. Paul's statement also retains suffering with Christ and shared glory.

The Aletheia teaching brings these passages into the design argument: **Jesus represents who we are by Design.** He represents the identity of the sons, while His redemptive work restores us and brings us into the inheritance described in Scripture. Representation and accomplishment must remain together.

# Chapter 9 — Our Oneness with the Father and the Glory of God
## The prayer that brings sonship and glory into view

The discussion of the Son and the heir leads naturally to the prayer Jesus prayed for those who would believe in Him. The Aletheia source directs attention to John 17:20–23 and asks the reader to consider the prayer of oneness. That invitation deserves more than a passing reference to Christian unity. Jesus' words describe a relationship: the Father in the Son, the Son in believers, believers in the Father and the Son, and the glory given to the Son also given to them.

The prayer also tells us why this oneness matters. Jesus connects it with the world's believing that the Father sent Him and knowing that the Father loves believers as He loves the Son. Oneness, glory, indwelling, love, and witness belong together in the passage. To understand the prayer, we must follow its movement rather than isolate one phrase.

## The Son speaks to the Father

> **John 17:1 (KJV)** — “These words spake Jesus, and lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said, Father, the hour is come; glorify thy Son, that thy Son also may glorify thee.”

Jesus addresses the Father as the hour approaches. He asks to be glorified so that He may glorify the Father. Glory in this prayer is therefore not introduced as a possession detached from relationship or purpose. The Son speaks to the Father, and the glory sought by the Son is bound to the Father's glory.

The verses that follow continue this setting. Jesus speaks of the authority given Him, eternal life, the work He has been given to do, and the glory He had with the Father before the world was. The prayer begins with the Son's own relation to the Father before it turns explicitly to the disciples and those who will believe through their word. This order matters: the oneness for which Jesus prays is grounded in the relationship He names between Himself and the Father.

## Sent, sanctified, and sent into the world

> **John 17:18–19 (KJV)** — “As thou hast sent me into the world, even so have I also sent them into the world. And for their sakes I sanctify myself, that they also might be sanctified through the truth.”

Jesus places His sending alongside the sending of His disciples: “As thou hast sent me into the world, even so have I also sent them into the world.” He then speaks of sanctifying Himself for their sakes, so that they may be sanctified through the truth.

The comparison is deliberate. The disciples do not occupy the Son's personal relation to the Father; nevertheless, Jesus makes His own sending the pattern for theirs. Their relationship with Him does not end in private spiritual experience: they are sent into the world, and their sanctification through the truth belongs to that mission.

This prepares the way for the wider prayer. The movement from the Son to those sent by Him is not a claim that every distinction disappears. It is a movement of relationship, participation, purpose, and witness.

## The prayer extends to everyone who believes

> **John 17:20–23 (KJV)** — “Neither pray I for these alone, but for them also which shall believe on me through their word; That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us: that the world may believe that thou hast sent me. And the glory which thou gavest me I have given them; that they may be one, even as we are one: I in them, and thou in me, that they may be made perfect in one; and that the world may know that thou hast sent me, and hast loved them, as thou hast loved me.”

Jesus expressly extends His prayer beyond the disciples who were with Him. He prays also for those who will believe in Him through their word. The reach of the prayer therefore includes later believers. Its subject is not limited to the first group of disciples.

The passage then unfolds in a connected sequence:

1. Jesus prays for all who will believe through the disciples' word.
2. He asks that they all may be one.
3. He places the Father-and-Son relationship before them: “as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee.”
4. He asks that believers also may be one in the Father and the Son.
5. He states that this oneness is to serve the world's believing that the Father sent Him.
6. He says that the glory given to Him by the Father has been given to them.
7. He again connects the glory given with the purpose that they may be one, even as the Father and Son are one.
8. He speaks of His presence in them and the Father in Him, so that they may be made perfect in one.
9. He connects this perfected oneness with the world's knowing that the Father sent Him and loved believers as He loved the Son.

The repeated purpose clauses are important. Jesus does not speak of oneness as an end that terminates in the believers themselves. Their oneness is to make something known to the world: that the Father sent the Son and that the Father's love toward believers is as His love toward the Son.

Nor is the prayer merely a request that people get along. Agreement, fellowship, and the absence of conflict may be valuable, but they do not exhaust the language Jesus uses. He speaks of being in the Father and the Son, of the Son being in believers, of glory given, and of being made perfect in one. The prayer describes a reality deeper than institutional coordination or a shared feeling.

## What does it mean to be one “as” the Father and Son are one?

Jesus does not leave the comparison unstated. He says, “as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee,” and later, “that they may be one, even as we are one.” He also says that the Father has loved believers “as thou hast loved me.” These comparisons must be allowed to retain their full force.

The word “as” establishes a correspondence. It directs the reader to consider the relationship between the Father and the Son when considering the oneness Jesus desires for believers. It should not be removed simply because the comparison is demanding. At the same time, the comparison should be read with the relationships the prayer itself preserves. Jesus addresses the Father; the Father sends the Son; the Son sends His disciples; and believers are to be one in the Father and the Son. The prayer does not collapse these persons and relations into one undifferentiated person.

The correspondence includes being in the Father and the Son, the Son being in believers, the sharing of glory, and the completion of their unity. The comparison is neither mere cooperation nor a claim that every person is identical in every respect. Jesus describes a real oneness and identifies the relationships in which it exists.

The Aletheia teaching on image and likeness gives this prayer a wider doctrinal setting. Genesis 1:26–28 presents humanity as made in God's image and after His likeness. Hebrews 1:3 calls the Son the express image of God's person. John 17 now directs attention to the oneness of the Father and Son and to the oneness into which believers are brought. The relationship among these passages is part of the book's doctrinal synthesis; John 17 should not be made to repeat the wording of Genesis or Hebrews when it does not.

## The glory given

One of the prayer's most striking declarations is: “And the glory which thou gavest me I have given them.” Jesus does not merely pray that believers may one day hear about glory. He says that the glory given to Him has been given to them, and He connects that gift with their being one.

The statement belongs within the book's wider examination of glory. Romans 3:23 says that all have sinned and come short of the glory of God. The Aletheia source interprets coming short of glory in relation to deviation from true nature. First Corinthians 11:7 calls man the image and glory of God, in a passage whose immediate context concerns headship and covering in the assembly. John 17:22, in turn, speaks of the glory given to the Son and given by Him to believers.

Together, these passages contribute to the book's argument about humanity's Design, the coming short of glory, and the glory given in Christ. This is a synthesis across the Scripture set; the passages do not use identical wording or independently state the entire conclusion. John 17's direct statement remains clear: Jesus says that the glory given to Him has also been given to those for whom He prays, and He connects that gift with their oneness.

Verse 23 continues the thought: “I in them, and thou in me, that they may be made perfect in one.” The prayer brings together the glory given, the Son's presence in believers, the Father's presence in the Son, and the completion of their oneness. This is not an incidental detail. It is central to the reality Jesus asks the Father to bring about.

## Loved as the Son is loved

The prayer's final purpose statement is equally significant: “that the world may know that thou hast sent me, and hast loved them, as thou hast loved me.” Jesus places the Father's love for believers alongside the Father's love for Him. The comparison is explicit.

This statement belongs with the preceding chapters' treatment of sonship and heirship. Galatians 4:5–7 speaks of adoption, the Spirit of the Son in our hearts, and the believer's status as a son and heir through Christ. Romans 8:16–17 speaks of the Spirit bearing witness with our spirit that we are children of God, and, if children, heirs—heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ. John 17 adds the language of the Father's love: believers are loved as the Father loves the Son.

Each passage retains its own emphasis: Galatians develops redemption, adoption, and heirship; Romans develops the Spirit's witness, sonship, suffering, and shared glory; John 17 records the Son's prayer for oneness and the world's knowledge of the Father's love. Together they show that the believer's relation to God is not merely distant or external. Jesus prays for believers to participate in the oneness and love of the Father and the Son.

## Oneness, image, and present representation

The Aletheia source's question—how would you liken the prayer of oneness?—invites the reader to connect John 17 with the design and nature passages already examined. Genesis 1:26–28 speaks of image and likeness; 2 Peter 1:4, of participation in divine nature; Galatians 4:5–7 and Romans 8:16–17, of sonship and heirship; Hebrews 1:3, of the Son as the express image of God's person; and 2 Corinthians 5:21, of the righteousness of God in Christ. First John 4:17 adds, within the context of perfected love and confidence, “as he is, so are we in this world.”

John 17 contributes its own language: believers are to be one in the Father and the Son; the glory given to the Son is given to them; Christ is in them; they are to be made perfect in one; and the Father loves them as He loves the Son. The prayer therefore brings the relational and glory dimensions into the book's connected teaching on Design, nature, sonship, and righteousness.

The governing thesis remains that **Jesus represents who we are by Design**. This representation is not a substitute for redemption, justification, or the work of Christ. Nor does redemption make the representation theme unnecessary. The One who reveals the Son's relation to the Father and prays for believers to share in the oneness and glory He describes is also the One through whom the book has developed redemption and restoration. Christ's person and Christ's accomplishment must remain together.

The prayer presses beyond a shallow definition of unity. It calls the reader to consider the oneness, indwelling, glory, and love Jesus names, together with their stated purpose: that the world may believe the Father sent the Son and know that the Father loves believers as He loves the Son. This oneness has a witness beyond itself.

## The glory made known in Christ

The prayer of John 17 does not end the book's inquiry into glory. It prepares the reader to consider how the knowledge of God's glory is made visible in Christ. Jesus speaks of the glory given to Him and given to believers; the next passage under examination declares that the light of the knowledge of God's glory is given “in the face of Jesus Christ.”

That movement brings the discussion to 2 Corinthians 4:1–6. There Paul names Christ as the image of God and describes the light that shines in the heart. The prayer and that passage belong together in the book's cumulative argument: the Son reveals the Father's glory, and the oneness for which He prays is connected to the world's knowledge of His sending and the Father's love.

## Questions for reflection and study

1. Why does Jesus extend His prayer beyond the disciples who were with Him?
2. What relationships does Jesus name when He prays that believers may be one?
3. What does the repeated word “as” contribute to the prayer?
4. What does Jesus say about the glory given to Him and given to believers?
5. How are oneness, indwelling, the Father's love, and the world's knowledge connected in John 17:20–23?
6. How does this prayer connect with the book's earlier treatment of image and likeness, sonship, heirship, and righteousness in Christ?
7. Why must Christ's representation of humanity's design be held together with His redemptive accomplishment?

# Chapter 10 — Have the Faith of God: Faith as the State of God
## The glory of God made known in Christ

The final movement of this book brings us to Paul's declaration that the light of the knowledge of the glory of God is given “in the face of Jesus Christ.” This is not an isolated statement about religious admiration. It gathers the book's inquiry into the image of God, humanity's design, the glory from which humanity has come short, the work of redemption, and the present reality of righteousness and sonship in Christ.

The Aletheia teaching directs attention to Christ as the image of God and to the knowledge of God's glory in His face. The passages considered throughout this book have led us from the creation account and the image and likeness of God, through the teaching on *pistis*, sonship, divine nature, justification, heirship, and oneness, to this declaration about Christ. The closing task is to see what Paul's words contribute to that connected argument.

Two inseparable truths remain together: Jesus represents who we are by Design, and He has accomplished the redemption through which humanity is restored to that design. His person and His work belong together; the image revealed in Him must not be separated from the redemption accomplished through Him.

## A ministry of truth, not distortion

> **2 Corinthians 4:1–2 (KJV)** — “Therefore seeing we have this ministry, as we have received mercy, we faint not; But have renounced the hidden things of dishonesty, not walking in craftiness, nor handling the word of God deceitfully; but by manifestation of the truth commending ourselves to every man's conscience in the sight of God.”

Paul begins with the mercy by which the ministry has been received. That mercy gives reason not to faint. He then describes the character of the ministry: hidden dishonesty is renounced, craftiness is rejected, and the word of God is not handled deceitfully. Truth is manifested in the sight of God and commended to conscience.

These opening words are especially fitting for a study that brings several passages into one doctrinal argument. We must allow each passage to speak in its own wording and setting. We must not diminish what it says, but neither should we make a verse carry words or conclusions that belong to a wider synthesis. Faithful handling of the word requires both conviction and care.

Paul's statement also places the ministry under the seriousness of divine accountability. The subject is not the construction of an attractive religious idea. It is the manifestation of truth. What follows about the gospel, the image of God, light, and glory must therefore be read as Paul's connected argument.

## Christ, the image of God, and the blinded mind

> **2 Corinthians 4:3–4 (KJV)** — “But if our gospel be hid, it is hid to them that are lost: In whom the god of this world hath blinded the minds of them which believe not, lest the light of the glorious gospel of Christ, who is the image of God, should shine unto them.”

Paul identifies the gospel as the light of the glorious gospel of Christ, and identifies Christ as “the image of God.” He also describes the opposition to that light: the minds of those who believe not are blinded, lest the light should shine to them. The blinding is not presented as a neutral lack of information. It stands against the shining of the gospel.

The image is central to the verse. Christ is not merely someone who speaks about God or supplies information about Him; Paul calls Him the image of God. The light being resisted is the light of the gospel of this Christ.

This statement belongs beside the earlier creation account. Genesis 1:26–28 speaks of humanity made in God's image and after His likeness. Hebrews 1:3 describes the Son as the express image of God's person. Second Corinthians 4:4 calls Christ the image of God. These passages do not use identical expressions, and each must retain its own context. Read together, however, they contribute to the book's argument about the image revealed in the Son and humanity's creation design.

This connection is significant. Humanity's creation is described in terms of God's image and likeness, while Christ is declared to be the image of God. His person is therefore central to the book's inquiry into Design. The Aletheia thesis is not that humanity invents its own identity, but that humanity's Design is understood in relation to God and represented in Christ.

Paul also tells us why the light matters: it is the light of the glorious gospel of Christ. The image is not detached from the gospel. Christ's identity and the good news concerning Him belong together.

## The light of the knowledge of God's glory

> **2 Corinthians 4:5–6 (KJV)** — “For we preach not ourselves, but Christ Jesus the Lord; and ourselves your servants for Jesus' sake. For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.”

Paul immediately guards the focus of the ministry: “we preach not ourselves, but Christ Jesus the Lord.” The servants are not the message. Christ Jesus the Lord is the One they preach.

Paul then recalls the God who commanded light to shine out of darkness. The same God has shined in our hearts. The stated purpose is “to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.” The wording brings together light, knowledge, glory, and the face of Christ. It is not simply that light appears; light gives knowledge. Nor is the knowledge vague or detached from the person of Christ: it is the knowledge of God's glory in His face.

The verse brings the chapter's subject into focus: God's glory is made known in Christ. The One identified in verse 4 as the image of God is the One in whose face the knowledge of that glory is given. Paul directs the reader to the glory of God as it is made known in Christ.

The reference to the God who commanded light to shine out of darkness also recalls the creation account. Genesis begins with God's creative command concerning light; Paul uses that act to describe God's shining in human hearts. The comparison is Paul's own. The connection to humanity's image and likeness in Genesis 1:26–28 is developed across the wider Scripture set, rather than being substituted for the particular point Paul makes in 2 Corinthians 4:6.

## The image revealed in Christ and humanity's design

The book began with the foundation of faith and followed the Aletheia source's connected teaching on credence, the state of God's children, true nature, and sonship. It then examined the creation account, the language of image and likeness, the declaration in Psalm 82 and Jesus' appeal in John 10, participation in divine nature, Christ's coming, redemption and justification, the Son and the heir, and the prayer of oneness in John 17.

Second Corinthians 4:4–6 brings the image and glory inquiry to Christ. Genesis 1:26–28 speaks of humanity's creation in God's image and likeness. Hebrews 1:2–3 presents the Son as heir of all things and the express image of God's person. Second Peter 1:4 speaks of partaking of the divine nature. Galatians 4:5–7 and Romans 8:16–17 develop sonship and heirship. John 17:20–23 records Jesus' prayer that believers be one in the Father and Son, and His declaration that the glory given to Him has been given to them.

These passages form a connected argument, not one long quotation. Their contribution is cumulative. They direct attention to God's nature, humanity's design, the Son who reveals the image, and the relationship into which believers are brought through Him. The Aletheia source's teaching on true nature and the doctrinal synthesis developed in this book give this connection its governing force: **Jesus represents who we are by Design.**

This statement does not mean that Christ's unique person and work can be reduced to a general description of humanity. The book has treated Him as the Son, the heir, the express image, and the One through whom redemption is accomplished. His representation is grounded in who He is; it is not a denial of His uniqueness or of His saving work.

## Coming short of glory and the work of restoration

The Aletheia source connects the words “come short of the glory of God” in Romans 3:23 with deviation from true nature. Romans 3:23–24 must be read as one movement:

> **Romans 3:23–24 (KJV)** — “For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God; Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus.”

The passage joins sin and coming short of glory with justification freely by grace through redemption in Christ Jesus. The source's interpretation places this coming short in relation to deviation from true nature. The book develops the conclusion that sin is a falling short of the designed state and that justification and redemption restore humanity to that state.

That restoration argument must not be separated from the person of Christ revealed in 2 Corinthians 4. The One who is the image of God is also the One in whom redemption is found. Second Corinthians 5:17–21 develops reconciliation and the righteousness of God in Christ. Ephesians 1:7 and Colossians 1:13–14 belong to the wider redemption teaching. These passages speak of Christ's work; they are not displaced by the representation theme.

The Aletheia leaflet's gloss for *apolutrōsis* is “redemption; ransom in full.” Its treatment of *dikaiosynē* includes the wording “equity: impartiality to and conformity with the nature of God,” together with “original: back on course.” The book retains these source formulations and develops the doctrinal conclusion that complete ransom and justification are related to restoration to original design. “Back on course” expresses that doctrinal restoration; it is not offered as an exhaustive dictionary definition of every Greek term.

The relationship can be stated simply: **Christ's work restores what Christ's person reveals.** This is the book's synthesis, not a direct quotation from one verse. It holds together the image and glory disclosed in Christ with the redemption, reconciliation, and justification accomplished in Him. The work does not erase the representation; the representation does not make the work unnecessary.

## Present reality in Christ

The book has also considered the present-tense force of its connected Scripture passages. Second Corinthians 5:21 speaks of being made the righteousness of God in Christ. First John 4:17 declares, within the context of perfected love and confidence, “as he is, so are we in this world.” Matthew 5:48 remains within Jesus' command to love even one's enemies and is considered alongside 1 John 4:17 in the book's treatment of identity and likeness.

John 17:22–23 records Jesus' declaration that the glory given to Him has been given to believers, and His prayer that they may be one, with the Son in them and the Father in Him. These statements are not merely about a distant future. They speak of what Christ gives and of the relationship for which He prays. The book's argument about present reality is therefore grounded in the passages themselves and in the Aletheia teaching that connects them.

Present reality does not mean that every aspect of the believer's experience is already complete or that these passages have no future dimension. John 17 speaks of being made perfect in one, and Romans 8 includes suffering and shared glory. The point is that the identity, righteousness, love, and oneness described here must not be treated as wholly external or postponed entirely to a future state. They are presented in relation to Christ and what is given in Him.

The light of the knowledge of God's glory in the face of Jesus Christ therefore speaks directly to the question of humanity's Design. Christ is not merely an example placed before humanity, disconnected from its intended nature. In the Aletheia doctrine, He represents that Design, and through His redemptive work humanity is restored to it.

2. Why is Christ identified as “the image of God” in 2 Corinthians 4:4, and how does that statement relate to Genesis 1:26–28 and Hebrews 1:3?
3. What does 2 Corinthians 4:6 say about the knowledge of God's glory and the face of Jesus Christ?
4. How does Paul's reference to the creation of light contribute to his description of God's work in the heart?
5. How does Romans 3:23–24 connect coming short of glory with justification and redemption?
6. Explain the synthesis expressed in the sentence: “Christ's work restores what Christ's person reveals.” Which parts are explicit scriptural statements, and which part is the book's doctrinal conclusion?
7. How does the book hold together Jesus' representation of humanity's Design and His unique redemptive accomplishment?

## Conclusion: the foundation for the evidence

Book I has established its foundation through Hebrews 11:1 and the discussion of *hypostasis*, substance, and underlying reality. It has then developed the Aletheia leaflet's teaching on *pistis* as credence and its connection with the state of God's children, true nature, and Sonship. The argument has followed the connected passages concerning image and likeness, divine nature, glory, Christ's person and work, justification, heirship, and oneness. The final image is the image of God revealed in Christ, in whose face the knowledge of God's glory shines.

The next inquiry is distinct but dependent on this foundation. Book II, *Faith Is Evidence*, develops the evidence dimension of faith. It does not replace or postpone the foundation established here. The question is no longer whether the foundation belongs in Book I; it does. The next question is how faith as evidence is demonstrated and operates from that foundation.

The book closes, then, with its attention fixed on Christ: the image of God, the One in whose face the knowledge of God's glory is given, and the One whose person reveals humanity's design while whose work accomplishes redemption and restoration. The foundation has been laid. The inquiry into evidence can now proceed from it.

# Glossary of Key Terms

The explanations below clarify how important terms are used in this volume. Where a term has a wider lexical range, the entry identifies the particular role it plays in the book's argument. A glossary entry does not replace the context of the passage in which the term appears.

**Apolutrōsis (ἀπολύτρωσις)** — Redemption or release effected by ransom. The Aletheia leaflet gives the wording “redemption; ransom in full.” This book develops the restoration-to-Design conclusion from that source wording and the wider Scripture argument; “restoration to original Design” is not presented as a word-for-word dictionary gloss.

**Charaktēr (χαρακτήρ)** — The term translated “express image” in Hebrews 1:3. It contributes to the description of the Son as the exact representation of the Father's *hypostasis*.

**Credence** — The English word used by the 2015 Aletheia leaflet for *pistis*. In the leaflet's “Faith toward God” teaching, *pistis* / credence defines the state of the children of God and is connected with acceptance, true nature, and Sonship.

**Design** — The doctrinal framework used throughout this book for humanity's created image and likeness, true nature, and identity as revealed in Christ. The governing formulation is that **Jesus represents who we are by Design** and that **faith is our state by design**. These are the book's doctrinal conclusions developed from the controlling source and the connected Scripture argument.

**Dikaioō (δικαιόω)** — The Greek verb associated with justification. The leaflet's printed gloss is “render just or innocent (free from evil or guilt).” Strong's and Thayer's lexical entries are treated separately in the references; the book's full restoration argument is not reduced to a single dictionary definition.

**Dikaiosynē (δικαιοσύνη)** — Righteousness. The Aletheia leaflet explains it through equity, impartiality to and conformity with God's nature, and gives the source wording “original: back on course.” This book preserves that as the leaflet's explanation and develops its doctrinal implications in the wider Scripture set.

**Elenchos (ἔλεγχος)** — The term rendered “evidence” in Hebrews 11:1. It is joined to *hypostasis* in the verse. Book I concentrates on the foundation expressed by *hypostasis*; Book II, *Faith Is Evidence*, develops this evidence dimension.

**’Ĕlōhîm (אֱלֹהִים)** — The Hebrew term used in Genesis 1:1 and in Genesis 1:26. The 2015 Aletheia leaflet gives the gloss “plural: Trinity.” This entry records the leaflet's stated wording; the chapter's treatment considers the term within Genesis 1:26–28 and the source's image-and-likeness teaching.

**Hypostasis (ὑπόστασις)** — The Greek noun rendered “substance” in Hebrews 11:1 in the King James Version. Its documented range includes substance or underlying reality and confidence or assurance. This book gives foundational attention to the substance reading and develops its relationship to the Aletheia teaching that *pistis* defines the state of God's children. The contexts of Hebrews 1:3, 3:14, and 11:1 remain distinct.

**Pistis (πίστις)** — The Greek noun translated “faith.” The 2015 Aletheia leaflet identifies it as “credence” and states that it defines the state of the children of God. In this volume, *pistis* is considered in relation to the source's teaching on acceptance, true nature, and Sonship.

**Spoudē (σπουδή)** — The noun translated “diligence” in 2 Peter 1:5. It is distinct from the related verb *spoudazō* (σπουδάζω), which appears in other passages and must not be substituted for the noun in this verse.

**True nature** — A central expression in the Aletheia teaching, connected with humanity's creation in the image and likeness of God, the state of God's children, and the source's interpretation of coming short of glory as deviation from true nature. The expression is developed across the Scripture cluster rather than assigned to one isolated verse.
