**Book:** *The Doctrine of Faith* — Book I

## Executive finding

The earlier nine-chapter arrangement was built around the leaflet's Faith Toward God section and the existing app companion, while incorrectly postponing Hebrews 11:1 and hypostasis to Book II. That is a structural error. The manuscript now requires a foundation-first architecture.

The governing owner thesis is that hypostasis—especially as examined through Hebrews 11:1, the word's semantic range, and the connected source notes—helps explain faith as substance and therefore faith as the person's underlying nature and state. This is a doctrinal direction to investigate and develop fully. It is not a claim that the 2015 leaflet itself explicitly discusses hypostasis: the leaflet's Faith Toward God section cites Hebrews 6:1 and explicitly defines pistis as credence that defines our state as children of God; it does not quote Hebrews 11:1 or use the term hypostasis.

That distinction is essential. Book I must bring together (a) the leaflet's explicit faith/sonship/nature teaching, (b) Hebrews 11:1 and the lexical/scriptural study of hypostasis, (c) the owner's notes about faith as state and underlying nature, and (d) the connected scriptural architecture. It must make the reasoning between those materials visible.

## 2. Lexical investigation: what hypostasis can and cannot establish

### 2.1 Form and semantic range

The Greek noun is ὑπόστασις (hypostasis). Standard lexical resources connect it with ὑφίστημι (hyphistēmi, to stand/set under) and document a range including a supporting/substructure, actual or substantial reality, substance/nature, and firmness/confidence/assurance. The word's history and formation are relevant, but the meaning of a word in a particular sentence cannot be derived from its components alone.

For this project, the crucial lexical observation is not simply “hypo means under, therefore the word means underlying nature.” The more defensible sequence is:

1. The word's formation and history suggest a “standing under / support” image.
2. Greek lexical evidence records established uses such as substructure, actual reality/substance, the substantial quality or nature of a person or thing, and confidence/assurance.
3. The New Testament uses the word in different contexts, so the contextual force must be examined at each occurrence.
4. Hebrews 11:1 predicates hypostasis of pistis in relation to things hoped for. The KJV renders it “substance”; other translations render it “assurance” or “reality.”
5. Hebrews 1:3 uses the same noun for the Father's hypostasis, rendered “person” in the KJV and “nature” or “being” in some modern translations. This use is relevant to the inquiry into substance/nature, but does not by itself prove that the identical nuance must be carried unchanged into Hebrews 11:1.
6. Hebrews 3:14 uses the word in a context conventionally rendered “confidence” or “assurance.” That occurrence is a necessary control against claiming the word has only one meaning.

### 2.2 New Testament distribution as a control

Commonly listed New Testament occurrences are 2 Corinthians 9:4; 2 Corinthians 11:17; Hebrews 1:3; Hebrews 3:14; and Hebrews 11:1. Their contexts do not all use the term in the same way:

- **2 Corinthians 9:4 and 11:17:** confidence / confident boasting in context.
- **Hebrews 1:3:** the Son as the exact expression / representation of God's hypostasis (KJV: “express image of his person”; other translations use “nature” or “being”).
- **Hebrews 3:14:** holding the beginning of one's hypostasis firm to the end, usually rendered confidence or assurance.
- **Hebrews 11:1:** faith is the hypostasis of things hoped for and the elenchos of things not seen; KJV “substance” and “evidence.”

**Interpretive implication:** The lexical range supports a serious inquiry into substance, reality, nature, and assurance. It does not permit us to treat “underlying nature” as a mechanical gloss that ends exegesis. The manuscript must argue why the substance/reality/nature sense is foundational to the owner's reading of Hebrews 11:1, how the surrounding Hebrews context supports that reading, and how it connects with the Aletheia teaching about faith as state.

### 2.3 Hebrews 11:1 must be read as a sentence and in context

The Greek line is:

> Ἔστιν δὲ πίστις ἐλπιζομένων ὑπόστασις, πραγμάτων ἔλεγχος οὐ βλεπομένων.

The sentence sets pistis in a relation to (1) things hoped for and (2) things not seen, using hypostasis and elenchos. The KJV renders the pair “substance” and “evidence.” Both halves matter. Book I must establish the first half—faith as hypostasis—without pretending that the second half, evidence, is irrelevant; Book II then develops evidence as its distinctive focus.

The immediate literary context also matters. Hebrews 10:32–39 recalls endurance under suffering, calls for confidence and patience, and warns against drawing back; Hebrews 11 then presents people who act and endure by faith, beginning with the elders' good report (11:2) and Enoch's testimony of pleasing God (11:5–6). Thus hypostasis is not to be detached from hope, God's promise, faithful endurance, testimony, and the unseen.



### 2.5 The interpretive alternatives must be engaged, not bypassed

The lexical range does not settle Hebrews 11:1 automatically. The initial commentary cross-check surfaced a real interpretive debate:

- The KJV reads “substance.” Some commentaries explain this as underlying essence/reality, a basis or foundation, or a substantial reality associated with what is hoped for.
- Other translations and commentaries favor “assurance,” “firm confidence,” or a settled posture of confidence toward what is hoped for. The NIV Application Commentary sample favors “firm, solid confidence,” relating the examples that follow to confidence in God's promises before their fulfillment.
- A Cambridge commentary on Hebrews 1:3 treats *hypostasis* there as “substance” or “essence,” and discusses why “person” can be a misleading English rendering for that context. A commentary on Hebrews 11:1, however, argues that “assurance” better describes faith's relation to its object, rather than claiming faith itself is substantial being.

These alternatives must be represented fairly. They do not veto the owner's intended doctrinal thesis, but they establish the work the thesis must do. The manuscript must not argue: “the word can mean substance; therefore it must mean underlying human nature in Hebrews 11:1; therefore the full doctrine is proven.” That would skip contextual and theological steps.

The required reasoning is more exact:

1. Establish the text's predicate: *pistis* is described as *hypostasis* in relation to things hoped for.
2. Explain why the KJV's “substance” and the lexical senses of underlying reality / nature are important to the owner's interpretation.
3. Test the “assurance/confidence” reading against Hebrews 10:32–39, Hebrews 3:14, and the examples in Hebrews 11.
4. Compare Hebrews 1:3's use of the same noun, while respecting the difference in grammatical relation and referent: the Son is the exact expression of God's *hypostasis* there; faith is *hypostasis* of things hoped for here.
5. State the doctrinal bridge that the owner intends: the substance/reality of faith is related to the underlying state/nature of the person. Identify the passages and Aletheia propositions that support this bridge.
6. Show how the Aletheia leaflet's explicit statement that *pistis* defines our state as children of God contributes to that bridge. Do not claim this is a lexical conclusion supplied by the leaflet itself.
7. Preserve the possibility that “substance,” “reality,” and “assurance” illuminate different aspects of the verse. Do not treat the options as automatically mutually exclusive, but do not declare them identical without argument.

This is a key ratiocination requirement for Chapter 1. A serious treatment of the owner's thesis must be able to state the strongest alternative reading accurately and explain why the book's chosen interpretation is warranted.


### 2.4 Why Hebrews 1:3 is a major internal cross-reference

The same author uses hypostasis in Hebrews 1:3 while describing the Son as the brightness of God's glory and the exact expression of His hypostasis. The Aletheia leaflet itself connects the Son/image/glory line through Hebrews 1:8, 2 Corinthians 4:4,6, 1 Corinthians 11:7, and the question of what the Son represents. The manuscript should therefore investigate Hebrews 1:3 alongside Hebrews 11:1, not as a shortcut but as an author-internal comparison:

- Hebrews 1:3: the Son's relation to the Father's hypostasis and the Son as exact expression/image.
- Hebrews 11:1: pistis as hypostasis of things hoped for.
- Aletheia's Faith Toward God teaching: faith defines the state of children; acceptance is based on true nature from creation; Christ is the image of God; the reader is the righteousness of God in Christ.
- Owner's thesis: faith concerns the person's underlying nature/state, and Jesus represents who we are by Design.

The comparison is potentially architecturally decisive, but the precise theological inference must be made explicit and subjected to owner review. The fact that the same noun occurs in both verses is a reason to compare them, not proof that every sense and referent are identical.

## 3. Foundational argument: the chain that must be demonstrated

The intended Book I argument should be reconstructed in this order of dependency. This is a reasoning map, not a claim that each arrow is stated verbatim in one verse.

### Proposition A — Faith is described as hypostasis in Hebrews 11:1

Begin with the sentence itself and the KJV's “substance” rendering, alongside the lexical range and the alternative “assurance/reality” renderings. Identify the interpretive question rather than hiding it.

### Proposition B — Hypostasis can denote substance / actual reality / nature, as well as assurance

Establish this from lexical evidence and compare the New Testament occurrences. Do not claim that etymology alone establishes the verse's meaning.

### Proposition C — The Aletheia source already defines faith as a state, not merely as a mental event

The leaflet explicitly states that pistis / credence defines our state as children of God and relates that state to acceptance, true nature, and adoption as sons. This is the direct Aletheia bridge from the faith word to state/nature.

### Proposition D — The owner's thesis joins the two lines

The owner's intended thesis is that the hypostasis language of Hebrews 11:1 is foundational for understanding faith as substance and the person's underlying nature/state. The manuscript must demonstrate the relationship between Hebrews 11:1 and the leaflet's pistis/state teaching; it must not falsely attribute Hebrews 11:1 or hypostasis to the leaflet.

### Proposition E — The nature/state claim must be tested across the design and Sonship cluster

Genesis 1:26–28; Galatians 4:5–7; Romans 8:14–17; Ephesians 1:3–5; 2 Peter 1:3–8; Psalm 82:1–8 with John 10:30–39; Matthew 3:17 with Hebrews 11:5–6; and 1 John 4:17 must be read with their own contexts. Together they form the cluster through which the manuscript examines design, image/likeness, adoption, divine nature, pleasing God, heirship, and present reality.

### Proposition F — Deviation and restoration must be explained, not assumed

Romans 3:21–26 and 1 Corinthians 11:7 provide the leaflet's “come short of the glory” / image and glory knot. The source's gloss of “deviation from true nature,” free justification, dikaiosyne as conformity with God's nature / “back on course,” and apolutrosis as redemption / ransom in full must be retained as source teaching. The owner's “render righteous or such as he ought to be” and redemption-as-restoration line must remain separately identified as owner formulations / synthesis where appropriate.

### Proposition G — Christ's person and work must remain joined

Hebrews 1:2–3; 1:8; 2 Corinthians 4:4–6; 2 Corinthians 5:17–21; Ephesians 1:7; Colossians 1:13–14; and John 17:20–23 must be considered as a cumulative cluster. The owner's thesis is that Jesus represents who we are by Design, while His work accomplishes redemption and restoration. The working synthesis “Christ's work restores what Christ's person reveals” must be argued across the cluster, not attributed as a quotation from Scripture.

### Proposition H — Book I establishes the state/substance foundation; Book II develops evidence

Book I must establish why hypostasis is foundational to the doctrine and how that foundation relates to faith as state/nature. It should still acknowledge that Hebrews 11:1 contains elenchos (“evidence”), but it must not exhaust Book II's distinctive argument. Book II, *Faith Is Evidence*, develops the evidence side from the foundation laid in Book I.
