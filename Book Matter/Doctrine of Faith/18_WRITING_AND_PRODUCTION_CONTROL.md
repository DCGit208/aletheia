## 3. Non-negotiable authorial voice

- Write as the author presenting and developing his doctrine. Do not narrate the book as a detached commentator reporting that “Peter says,” “Paul thinks,” “Aletheia teaches,” or “the source says” as a repeated rhetorical device.
- Integrate Scripture authoritatively and contextually into the argument. Identify the biblical writer or passage where useful, but do not frame Scripture as a collection of personal opinions.
- Do not repeatedly announce the source, the author, the book, the chapter's purpose, or the writing process in reader-facing prose.
- Keep provenance, bibliographic facts, translation identification, and lexical qualifications in appropriate notes and back matter.
- Preserve the author's doctrinal formulations and their force. Do not dilute them into generic religious language or replace them with an unrelated theological system.
- Do not invent analogies, parables, stories, illustrations, testimonies, or rhetorical examples. Use none unless supplied by the author or demonstrably necessary and approved.
- Avoid generic religious clichés, motivational filler, shallow sermon language, repetitive conclusions, and empty transitions.
- Do not manufacture length. Develop each argument to the depth required by its Scripture and doctrinal responsibility.
- Do not include editorial commentary, research notes, production instructions, audit language, or manuscript architecture inside the reader-facing exposition.
- Do not use “this chapter will” or “this book will” as a substitute for making the argument.
- The prose must be clear, forceful, rigorous, readable, and continuous. Explain terms and implications without turning every paragraph into a glossary entry.

## 4. Source hierarchy and evidence discipline

Maintain these distinctions throughout the manuscript:

1. **Scripture explicitly states:** what the passage says in its wording and immediate literary/historical context.
2. **The 2015 Aletheia Libraries 1 leaflet explicitly teaches:** what is actually printed in “Faith toward God.”
3. **Lexical or grammatical evidence:** what a cited lexicon, interlinear, or language resource supports.
4. **Doctrinal synthesis:** a conclusion developed by connecting multiple passages and source teachings.
5. **Author's interpretive formulation:** the author's own stated doctrinal language and developing interpretation.

Never silently turn a synthesis or author's interpretation into a direct quotation or explicit statement of a single verse. This distinction is for intellectual honesty and strength; it is not permission to weaken the doctrine.

### Controlling source boundary

The 2015 leaflet explicitly identifies *pistis* as “credence” and connects faith with the state of God's children, acceptance, true nature, and adoption as sons. It connects Genesis 1:26–27, 2 Peter 1:4, Psalm 82:6, Romans 3:23–24, 1 Corinthians 11:7, justification, righteousness, redemption, heirship, John 17, and 2 Corinthians 4.

The leaflet does **not** itself explicitly develop Hebrews 11:1 or use *hypostasis*. The *hypostasis* foundation is the author's separate controlling direction for Book I. Never attribute the *hypostasis* treatment to the 2015 leaflet.

The supplied Bible Introduction is a supplementary method guide: read literally where possible, observe grammar and context, gather related passages, harmonize Scripture, and avoid unsupported theories. It is not the source of the leaflet's doctrine or its Greek/Hebrew glosses.

The author's additional notes in `study/15_ADDITIONAL_NOTES_FAITH_SONSHIP_DESIGN.md` are standing developmental instructions. Integrate them where they belong; do not append them mechanically or treat them as optional.

## 5. Core doctrinal formulations to preserve and develop

- **Faith is our state by Design.**
- **Jesus represents who we are by Design.**
- **Christ's work restores what Christ's person reveals.**
- Faith is not merely a mental act, optimism, religious activity, or assent to propositions.
- Hold representation and redemption together.
- Sonship, heirship, divine nature, oneness, glory, and present reality are connected dimensions of the doctrine.
- Book I develops faith as *hypostasis*—substance and underlying reality. Book II, *Faith Is Evidence*, develops *elenchos*. Hebrews 11:1 joins both clauses; neither is to be erased or treated as unrelated.

### Chapter 10 — mandatory authorial direction

The author explicitly requires Chapter 10 to fully develop Mark 11:22 and the God State as an acknowledgement of our Design, **not deficiency**. Develop the user's formulation “Have the faith of God” / “Have the state of God” as the culmination of the doctrine. Do not reduce it to a motivational formula or promise of extraordinary outcomes.

Mark 11:20–25 must be considered in context: the withered fig tree, Jesus' statement about faith, prayer, and forgiveness. Where the Greek construction or translation “faith in God” / “faith of God” is discussed, verify the grammatical and lexical evidence and distinguish it from the author's doctrinal interpretation. Do not falsely claim that a disputed translation is settled by assertion.

Matthew 5:48 must be considered in its context of love, including love toward enemies. The design/nature reading is a wider doctrinal synthesis, not the only words of the verse. The chapter must culminate in the author's God-State direction without introducing a new chapter architecture.

## 6. Chapter boundaries and responsibilities

### 1. Faith as Hypostasis: Substance and Underlying Reality
Develop Hebrews 11:1 and *hypostasis* as substance/underlying reality. Establish the Book I foundation. Bring in Genesis 1:26–27, Hebrews 1:1–3, and 2 Peter 1:4 only to the extent needed to establish the relationship between faith, Design, and the Son as express image. Do not prematurely develop *pistis* in full, Elohim, Psalm 82, the full divine-nature passage, justification, heirship, John 17, or Mark 11:22. No detached verse-jumping.

### 2. Pistis: Credence, Acceptance and Sonship
Develop the leaflet's *pistis* / credence teaching, Hebrews 6:1, Galatians 4:4–7, Romans 8:14–17, Ephesians 1:3–5, Hebrews 11:2, 5–6, and Matthew 3:17. Explore Sonship, acceptance, adoption, the Spirit's witness, good report, and the Father's pleasure. Distinguish explicit text from synthesis.

### 3. Elohim: Image, Likeness and Human Design
Develop Genesis 1:26–28, Genesis 5:1–2, 1 Corinthians 11:7, Matthew 5:43–48, 1 John 4:17, and 2 Peter 1:4. Preserve the leaflet's interpretation of *Elohim* as its own source formulation; do not misrepresent Genesis as literally using the English phrase “as Gods.” Develop image, likeness, blessing, fruitfulness, and dominion.

### 4. Ye Are Gods: Psalm 82 and the Testimony of Jesus in John 10
Develop Psalm 82:1–8 in its full judgment setting, including verses 6–8, then Jesus' appeal in John 10:30–39. Give full weight to “the Scripture cannot be broken,” the sanctified and sent Son, and the Father's works. Do not isolate verse 6 or erase verse 7.

### 5. Partakers of the Divine Nature
Develop 2 Peter 1:2–8 as a connected passage: knowledge, divine power, life and godliness, calling, promises, participation in divine nature, escape from corruption, diligence, and the qualities in verses 5–8. Do not stop at verse 4.

### 6. Why Did Jesus Come? The Glory We Came Short Of
Develop Romans 3:23–24 in context alongside Genesis 1:26–27, 1 Corinthians 11:7, 1 Corinthians 15:35–49, 2 Corinthians 4:4–6, Ephesians 1:7, and Colossians 1:13–14. Preserve the leaflet's reading of coming short of glory as deviation from true nature while identifying it as the leaflet's interpretation. Hold Design, glory, sin, Christ's representation, and redemption together.

### 7. Justification, Righteousness and Redemption in Christ
Develop Romans 3:21–28, 2 Corinthians 5:17–21, Ephesians 1:7, Colossians 1:13–14, and 2 Peter 1:4. Preserve and accurately attribute the leaflet's glosses: *dikaioō* “render just or innocent (free from evil or guilt)”; *dikaiosynē* equity, impartiality, conformity with God's nature, and the source wording “back on course”; *apolutrōsis* “redemption; ransom in full.” Keep lexical meanings distinct and do not make a dictionary entry prove the whole restoration doctrine. Give 2 Corinthians 5:21 substantial treatment.

### 8. The Son and the Heir: Our Inheritance in Christ
Develop Hebrews 1:1–8, Galatians 4:1–7, Romans 8:14–17, 2 Corinthians 5:21, and 1 John 4:17. The Son is appointed heir of all things and the express image. Believers receive inheritance through Him as children and joint-heirs. Preserve Jesus' unique identity; do not imply believers replace Him or become the same person.

### 9. Our Oneness with the Father and the Glory of God
Develop John 17:1–26, especially verses 20–23, alongside 2 Corinthians 4:4–6, Romans 8:17, and 1 John 4:17. Treat the prayer in its full movement: sent, sanctified, believers included, oneness “as,” glory given, indwelling, love, and the world's witness. Do not reduce it to generic unity.

### 10. Have the Faith of God: Faith as the State of God
Fully develop Mark 11:20–25, especially verse 22, as the culmination of the entire book. The God State is an acknowledgement of Design, not deficiency. Connect the earlier arguments—*hypostasis*, Sonship, image and likeness, Psalm 82 / John 10, divine nature, glory, redemption, heirship, and oneness—without simply repeating their chapters. Bring in Genesis 1:26–28, Matthew 5:48, 2 Corinthians 4:4–6, Hebrews 1:3, and Hebrews 11:1 where they genuinely serve the argument. End with a strong doctrinal conclusion, not an editorial roadmap to Book II.

## 7. Scripture and exposition method

- Read each passage in its immediate context before connecting it to other passages.
- Explain why a passage belongs in the argument; do not jump between references as decoration.
- Follow the grammar, argument, speaker, audience, literary form, and historical setting.
- Quote accurately and identify the translation. Do not silently combine translations or alter wording.
- When a translation or lexical issue matters, explain the issue and cite the relevant source in the back matter.
- Distinguish the meaning of a Greek/Hebrew term in context from a broader dictionary range.
- Do not claim that a lexical gloss independently proves a theological synthesis.
- Do not erase contextual tensions. Develop them honestly and explain how the argument accounts for them.
- Do not make unsupported claims about the original languages. If verification is incomplete, narrow the claim and state the limitation in a note rather than bluffing.

## 8. Pedagogical quality

The book must teach, not merely assert. Each major movement should:
- introduce the real doctrinal question generated by the text;
- explain the passage and its context;
- develop the reasoning step by step;
- show how the passage contributes to the central argument;
- address the necessary implications and apparent tensions;
- lead to a conclusion earned by the exposition.

Use reflection/study questions only where they deepen comprehension. Questions must test the actual chapter argument, not repeat generic prompts. Avoid excessive end-of-chapter question lists that interrupt the book's literary force.

## 9. What must not happen

Do not:
- write a shallow “woke” or generic inspirational book;
- dilute the doctrine to avoid its implications;
- overstate a verse beyond its context;
- treat the 2015 leaflet as though it explicitly contains *hypostasis*;
- attribute the author's synthesis directly to Scripture;
- insert unsupported lexical claims or invented Greek/Hebrew etymologies;
- use detached proof-text chains instead of sustained exegesis;
- repeat source provenance in the prose;
- invent analogies, examples, testimonies, or illustrations;
- pad with repetition, summaries, disclaimers, or manuscript-process language;
- move Mark 11:22 out of its culminating Chapter 10 role;
- prematurely consume later chapter material in Chapter 1;
- change approved titles or order;
- end Chapter 10 with an editorial announcement about Book II;
- call the manuscript complete or publication-ready before the required checks are actually performed.
